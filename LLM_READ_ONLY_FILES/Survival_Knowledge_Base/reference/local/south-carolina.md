# Local Emergency Reference — South Carolina

**Compiled 2026-08 — verify current details when possible.**

## 1. Read this first

This file goes stale — phone numbers, agency leadership, and web layouts change; verify against the sources below when you have connectivity. **Call 911 in a life-threatening emergency if phones work.** National numbers that are stable and safe to rely on: **911** (emergency), **988** (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline), **211** (health/human services), **1-800-222-1222** (Poison Control), **1-800-985-5990** (Disaster Distress Helpline), **511** (traveler/road info, available in South Carolina), and NOAA Weather Radio, which broadcasts on one of seven fixed channels (162.400–162.550 MHz) — your local transmitter determines which; look it up at weather.gov/nwr.

## 2. State emergency management

South Carolina's state-level agency is the **South Carolina Emergency Management Division (SCEMD)** — website **scemd.org**. A verifiable main/EOC phone number could not be confirmed at compile time from the agency's own pages; use the website as the stable point of contact instead. [Source: scemd.org]

South Carolina issues public alerts through the national **Wireless Emergency Alert (WEA)** system and **IPAWS** (Integrated Public Alert and Warning System), alongside **NOAA Weather Radio**. SCEMD's site also directs residents to sign up for **CodeRED** alerts, a mass-notification system used by South Carolina emergency-management agencies for local/county-level warnings — check with your county EMA for enrollment details, since coverage is county-by-county rather than a single statewide feed. [Source: scemd.org]

## 3. Weather alerts

South Carolina is covered by **four NWS Weather Forecast Offices**: **Columbia, SC** (weather.gov/cae — central SC/Midlands), **Charleston, SC** (weather.gov/chs — coastal SC/Lowcountry), **Greenville-Spartanburg** (office located in Greer, SC; weather.gov/gsp — Upstate SC), and **Wilmington, NC** (weather.gov/ilm — extreme northeastern coastal SC, e.g. the Grand Strand/Horry County area). If unsure which office covers your exact location, weather.gov looks it up by city/zip. [Sources: weather.gov/cae, weather.gov/chs, weather.gov/gsp, weather.gov/ilm]

**NOAA Weather Radio (NWR)** broadcasts on one of seven channels between 162.400 and 162.550 MHz; find your local transmitter's exact frequency at weather.gov/nwr.

## 4. Roads & travel

South Carolina's official traveler-information service is **511SC** (511sc.org), run by the South Carolina Department of Transportation (SCDOT). It provides real-time road conditions, traffic cameras, incident alerts, and evacuation-route information, and **511** is available by dialing it in-state. SCDOT's main site is scdot.org. [Sources: 511sc.org, scdot.org]

## 5. Shelters & mass care

South Carolina is served by the **American Red Cross Palmetto SC Region** (redcross.org/local/south-carolina), made up of four chapters — Lowcountry, Eastern, Central, and Upstate SC — covering the state's counties. To find an open shelter during a disaster: check the **Red Cross Emergency App**, call **211**, or watch local TV/radio news — shelter locations open reactively based on where the need is. [Source: redcross.org/local/south-carolina/about-us/locations.html]

## 6. Main hazards

- **Hurricanes & tropical storms** — the SC coast (Myrtle Beach, Charleston, Hilton Head, and the barrier islands) is directly exposed to landfalling and passing tropical systems, June–November.
- **Coastal flooding & storm surge** — low-lying coastal areas face surge and tidal flooding during hurricanes and even some non-tropical coastal storms ("king tide" flooding in Charleston is a recurring issue).
- **Tornadoes & severe thunderstorms** — occur statewide, with tornado risk rising during landfalling tropical systems and in spring severe-weather season.
- **Inland/riverine flooding** — heavy rainfall (tropical or otherwise) can produce significant flash and river flooding well away from the coast, as in the historic October 2015 statewide flood event.
- **Wildfire** — periodic drought conditions, especially in the Sandhills and coastal-plain forests, create elevated wildfire risk.
