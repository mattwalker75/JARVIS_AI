# Local Emergency Reference — Florida

**Compiled 2026-08 — verify current details when possible.**

## 1. Read this first

This is a **light** reference sheet: short, stable facts only (agency names, websites, office locations) — not a phonebook of utilities, hospitals, or repeater frequencies, which go stale fast. Call **911** in a life-threatening emergency if phones work.

National constants that don't change by state: **911** (emergency), **988** (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline), **211** (health/human services referral), **1-800-222-1222** (Poison Control), **1-800-985-5990** (Disaster Distress Helpline), **511** (traveler/road info, where participating), and NOAA Weather Radio's seven-channel band (162.400–162.550 MHz — local transmitter determines the exact frequency).

For a fuller, deep-tier example of this document type (utilities, trauma centers, ARRL/ARES, per-metro detail), see **texas.md**, **oklahoma.md**, or **arkansas.md** in this same directory. For radio specifics see `../comms-frequencies.md`; for maps see `../maps/INDEX.md`.

## 2. State emergency management

**Florida Division of Emergency Management (FDEM)** — website: **floridadisaster.org**. Main/headquarters phone: **850-815-4000** (Tallahassee HQ). Source: floridadisaster.org (verified live).

Public alerting reaches Floridians through the standard **Wireless Emergency Alerts (WEA)** system (pushed to cell phones on a registered tower regardless of carrier plan) plus **Alert Florida**, the state's own public notification portal (apps.floridadisaster.org/alertflorida). Counties also run their own local opt-in alert systems layered on top of these — check your county emergency management office for local sign-up. Source: floridadisaster.org.

## 3. Weather alerts

Florida is large enough to be covered by **several NWS Weather Forecast Offices**, each handling its own region:

- **Tallahassee (TAE)** — Panhandle/Big Bend
- **Jacksonville (JAX)** — northeast Florida
- **Tampa Bay, based in Ruskin (TBW)** — west-central Florida
- **Melbourne (MLB)** — east-central Florida
- **Miami (MFL)** — south Florida
- **Key West (KEY)** — the Florida Keys
- (the far western Panhandle is served by the **Mobile/Pensacola, AL office (MOB)**)

The **National Hurricane Center (NHC)** is co-located with the Miami WFO in Miami, Florida, and is the authoritative source for tropical cyclone forecasts and advisories (nhc.noaa.gov). Start at **weather.gov** and use each office's page (e.g., weather.gov/tae, weather.gov/jax, weather.gov/tbw, weather.gov/mlb, weather.gov/mfl, weather.gov/key) for current watches, warnings, and hazardous weather outlooks for your county. Source: weather.gov, nhc.noaa.gov.

**NOAA Weather Radio (NWR):** broadcasts on one of seven dedicated VHF channels (162.400–162.550 MHz); the specific frequency depends on your nearest transmitter. Look yours up at **weather.gov/nwr** before you need it.

## 4. Roads & travel

Florida's official traveler-information resource is **FL511 (fl511.com)**, run in conjunction with the Florida Department of Transportation (FDOT), offering real-time traffic, incident, and closure data via website, mobile app, and text alerts. The site also references a phone-based voice system (with a 711 TDD relay path into it for the deaf/hard-of-hearing), consistent with dialing **511** in-state; treat **fl511.com** and the FL511 app as the primary verified channels. Source: fl511.com.

## 5. Shelters & mass care

The **American Red Cross** serves Florida (redcross.org); its site organizes coverage into local/regional chapters, but this compile could not independently load the specific current Florida region name(s) to cite live — don't guess a chapter name. To find an open shelter during an active disaster: use the **Red Cross shelter/chapter locator** at redcross.org or the **Red Cross Emergency app**, call **211**, or watch local TV/radio news — shelter locations open and close as an event evolves.

## 6. Main hazards

- **Hurricanes & storm surge** — Florida has more coastline exposure than any other state; surge, not wind, is typically the deadliest hazard, and evacuation zones vary by county.
- **Flooding** — flash flooding from tropical downpours and slow-moving storms, plus chronic low-lying/coastal flooding in many areas.
- **Severe thunderstorms & tornadoes** — Florida leads the US in lightning strikes and frequently sees tornadoes embedded in hurricane outer rainbands.
- **Wildfire** — elevated risk statewide during the spring dry season (roughly April–May) before summer rains arrive.
- **Extreme heat & humidity** — a year-round hazard, especially for outdoor exposure during and after storm recovery.
