# Local Emergency Reference — Hawaii

**Compiled 2026-08 — verify current details when possible.**

## 1. Read this first

This is a **light** reference sheet: short, stable facts only (agency names, websites, office locations) — not a phonebook of utilities, hospitals, or repeater frequencies, which go stale fast. Call **911** in a life-threatening emergency if phones work.

National constants that don't change by state: **911** (emergency), **988** (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline), **211** (health/human services referral), **1-800-222-1222** (Poison Control), **1-800-985-5990** (Disaster Distress Helpline), **511** (traveler/road info, where participating — see Section 4 for Hawaii's limits), and NOAA Weather Radio's seven-channel band (162.400–162.550 MHz — local transmitter determines the exact frequency).

For a fuller, deep-tier example of this document type (utilities, trauma centers, ARRL/ARES, per-metro detail), see **texas.md**, **oklahoma.md**, or **arkansas.md** in this same directory. For radio specifics see `../comms-frequencies.md`; for maps see `../maps/INDEX.md`.

## 2. State emergency management

**Hawaii Emergency Management Agency (HI-EMA)**, part of the state Department of Defense, coordinates emergency response with the four county emergency management agencies. Agency site: **dod.hawaii.gov/hiema**; public-facing preparedness site: **ready.hawaii.gov**.

Public alerting runs through the **Hawaii Statewide Alert & Warning System (SAWS)**, layering FEMA's **Integrated Public Alert & Warning System (IPAWS)** — **Wireless Emergency Alerts (WEA)** and the **Emergency Alert System (EAS)** — on top of Hawaii's well-known statewide **outdoor warning siren network**, tested monthly. Source: dod.hawaii.gov/hiema.

## 3. Weather alerts

Hawaii is covered by the **National Weather Service Forecast Office in Honolulu (HFO)** — **weather.gov/hfo**. Two specialized centers are co-located there:

- **Central Pacific Hurricane Center (CPHC)** — issues tropical cyclone watches/warnings for the central Pacific basin (equator to 140°W–180°W), the authority for Hawaii tropical storms and hurricanes. **nhc.noaa.gov/cphc**.
- **Pacific Tsunami Warning Center (PTWC)** — issues tsunami warnings for Hawaii and the wider Pacific. **tsunami.gov**.

**NOAA Weather Radio (NWR)** broadcasts on one of seven dedicated VHF channels (162.400–162.550 MHz); the specific frequency depends on your nearest transmitter. Look yours up at **weather.gov/nwr**.

## 4. Roads & travel

The **Hawaii Department of Transportation (HDOT)** publishes road conditions, closures, and weather-related traffic alerts at **hidot.hawaii.gov** (see the Highways and Weather-Related Traffic Alerts pages). **511 is only partial in-state**: the **511 GoAkamai** phone/app/web service (**goakamai.org**) covers real-time traffic for **Oahu only**. Maui, Hawaii Island (Big Island), Kauai, and Molokai/Lanai do not have a matching statewide 511 build-out — **verify locally** via hidot.hawaii.gov or county sources for those islands.

## 5. Shelters & mass care

The **American Red Cross Pacific Islands Region** (redcross.org/local/pacific-islands.html) serves Hawaii along with American Samoa, Guam, and the Northern Mariana Islands. To find an open shelter during an active disaster: the **Red Cross Emergency app**, **211**, or local news/EAS broadcasts — shelter locations open and close as an event evolves.

## 6. Main hazards

- **Hurricanes & tropical storms** — central Pacific storms can affect any island; tracked by the Central Pacific Hurricane Center.
- **Tsunamis** — Hawaii's outdoor sirens and the Pacific Tsunami Warning Center are the backbone of tsunami warning; know your evacuation zone and move to high ground immediately on a natural warning (strong/long earthquake, ocean recession, roar).
- **Volcanic activity & vog** — active volcanism (notably Kilauea) monitored by the USGS Hawaiian Volcano Observatory; eruptions can produce ashfall and **vog** (volcanic smog) affecting air quality, especially on Hawaii Island.
- **Flash floods** — sudden, heavy rainfall causes fast-rising streams and road washouts, especially in valleys and near stream crossings.
- **High surf** — seasonal large-wave events (especially winter north-facing shores) create dangerous ocean and shoreline conditions independent of storms.
