# Local Emergency Reference — Alabama

**Compiled 2026-08 — verify current details when possible.**

## 1. Read this first

This is a **light** reference sheet: short, stable facts only (agency names, websites, office locations) — not a phonebook of utilities, hospitals, or repeater frequencies, which go stale fast. Call **911** in a life-threatening emergency if phones work.

National constants that don't change by state: **911** (emergency), **988** (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline), **211** (health/human services referral), **1-800-222-1222** (Poison Control), **1-800-985-5990** (Disaster Distress Helpline), **511** (traveler/road info, where participating), and NOAA Weather Radio's seven-channel band (162.400–162.550 MHz — local transmitter determines the exact frequency).

For a fuller, deep-tier example of this document type (utilities, trauma centers, ARRL/ARES, per-metro detail), see **texas.md**, **oklahoma.md**, or **arkansas.md** in this same directory. For radio specifics see `../comms-frequencies.md`; for maps see `../maps/INDEX.md`.

## 2. State emergency management

**Alabama Emergency Management Agency (AEMA)** — website: **ema.alabama.gov**. Main phone: **(205) 280-2200** (also listed: 1-800-843-0699). Source: ema.alabama.gov (verified live).

Public alerting in Alabama runs through the standard **Wireless Emergency Alerts (WEA)** system (pushed to cell phones on a registered tower regardless of carrier plan) plus **CodeRED**, a state-level opt-in notification system (text/call/email) introduced in 2021 in coordination with the Alabama Law Enforcement Agency (ALEA) and local emergency management offices; enrollment and coverage are set locally by county/city, so check with your county EMA for CodeRED sign-up in your area. Source: ema.alabama.gov, governor.alabama.gov.

## 3. Weather alerts

Alabama is covered by **three NWS Weather Forecast Offices**:

- **Birmingham (BMX)** — central Alabama
- **Huntsville (HUN)** — north Alabama
- **Mobile/Pensacola (MOB)** — south Alabama and the Gulf coast (shared office also covering the western Florida panhandle)

Start at **weather.gov** and use the office pages (weather.gov/bmx, weather.gov/hun, weather.gov/mob) for current watches, warnings, and hazardous weather outlooks for your county. Source: weather.gov.

**NOAA Weather Radio (NWR):** broadcasts on one of seven dedicated VHF channels (162.400–162.550 MHz); the specific frequency depends on your nearest transmitter. Look yours up at **weather.gov/nwr** before you need it.

## 4. Roads & travel

Alabama's official traveler-information resource is **ALGO Traffic** (algotraffic.com), run jointly by the Alabama Department of Transportation (ALDOT) and ALEA, with live traffic cameras, incident data, and a companion app. ALDOT's general site is **dot.state.al.us** (also aldot.gov). A statewide **511** phone service was not confirmed as ALDOT's current primary channel during this compile — treat ALGO Traffic and dot.state.al.us as the verified sources, and try 511 as a secondary option if available in your area. Source: dot.state.al.us, algotraffic.com.

## 5. Shelters & mass care

Alabama is served by the **American Red Cross of Alabama & Mississippi Region**, organized into local chapters (including Central West Alabama, Central East Alabama, Mid Alabama, North Alabama, and South Alabama). To find an open shelter during an active disaster: check the **Red Cross Emergency app**, call **211**, or watch local news/EAS broadcasts — shelter locations open and close as an event evolves, so don't rely on a static list. Source: redcross.org/local/al-ms.

## 6. Main hazards

- **Tornadoes** — Alabama sits in "Dixie Alley," with a primary spring severe-weather season and a secondary fall window; nighttime tornadoes are a notable regional risk.
- **Hurricanes & tropical systems** — Gulf coast counties (Mobile, Baldwin) face direct landfall risk; inland counties can still see major wind/rain/tornado impacts from decaying storms.
- **Flooding** — river and flash flooding occur statewide, especially with slow-moving tropical systems or heavy spring rain.
- **Severe thunderstorms & winter weather** — damaging straight-line winds and hail are common; occasional winter ice storms can disable roads and power across a region unaccustomed to them.
