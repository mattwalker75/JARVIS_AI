# Local Emergency Reference — Wisconsin

**Compiled 2026-08 — verify current details when possible.** Contact details drift faster than this sheet is updated; websites are more stable than phone numbers and are preferred below.

## 1. Read this first

Call **911** in a life-threatening emergency if phones work. Beyond that, these national numbers are stable and worth memorizing: **988** (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline), **211** (health/human-services referrals), **1-800-222-1222** (Poison Control), **1-800-985-5990** (Disaster Distress Helpline), and **511** for traveler/road info where available (Wisconsin participates — see Section 4). A NOAA Weather Radio broadcasts on one of seven VHF channels (162.400, 162.425, 162.450, 162.475, 162.500, 162.525, or 162.550 MHz) depending on your local transmitter.

This is the **light tier**: short, durable, verifiable facts only — no per-utility numbers, hospital lists, or repeater frequencies, since those go stale fast. For the fuller reference model this knowledge base uses, see the deep-tier sheets at `../local/texas.md`, `../local/oklahoma.md`, and `../local/arkansas.md`. For radio guidance see `../comms-frequencies.md`; for maps see `../maps/INDEX.md`.

## 2. State emergency management

**Wisconsin Emergency Management (WEM)**, a division of the Wisconsin Department of Military Affairs, is the state's lead emergency-management agency. Website: **wem.wi.gov**. WEM operates the State Emergency Operations Center (SEOC) and coordinates disaster preparedness, response, and recovery with local and tribal governments. WEM also runs a public preparedness site, **ReadyWisconsin** (readywisconsin.wi.gov), which includes a "Be Alert" section on public warning.

Wisconsin does not appear to operate a separately branded statewide public-alert system; alerts reach the public through the standard national channels — **Wireless Emergency Alerts (WEA)** pushed to cell phones via the Integrated Public Alert & Warning System (IPAWS), the **Emergency Alert System (EAS)** on broadcast radio/TV, and NOAA Weather Radio — issued by WEM, the National Weather Service, and local county emergency-management offices as appropriate. Check with your county emergency management office for any local siren or alert programs.

## 3. Weather alerts

Wisconsin is covered by multiple National Weather Service Weather Forecast Offices, each responsible for watches/warnings in its own county set:

- **Milwaukee/Sullivan** — weather.gov/mkx — southeast and south-central Wisconsin (~20 counties).
- **Green Bay** — weather.gov/grb — northeast and north-central Wisconsin (~22 counties), plus northwest Lake Michigan.
- **La Crosse** — weather.gov/arx — western Wisconsin.
- **Duluth, MN** — weather.gov/dlh — far northwestern Wisconsin, including Bayfield, Douglas, Ashland, Burnett, Iron, Sawyer, and Washburn counties.

Find your specific office and forecast at **weather.gov** (enter your city/ZIP). NOAA Weather Radio broadcasts continuously on the 7-channel VHF band noted above; use **weather.gov/nwr** to find your local transmitter and exact frequency.

## 4. Roads & travel

Wisconsin DOT publishes road conditions, traffic cameras, and winter driving info at **511wi.gov** (also **wisconsindot.gov**). **511** is available as a phone/traveler-information service within Wisconsin, in addition to the website and the 511 Wisconsin app.

## 5. Shelters & mass care

The **American Red Cross Wisconsin Region** (redcross.org/local/wisconsin) covers the state through five chapters (Southwest, Northwest, North Central, Northeast, and Southeast). To find an open shelter during an active disaster, use the Red Cross app, call **211**, or check local news/county emergency management channels — open-shelter lists change hour to hour and are never safe to hardcode.

## 6. Main hazards

- **Severe winter storms and blizzards** — heavy snow, ice, and dangerously cold wind chills, especially December–March.
- **Extreme cold** — sustained sub-zero temperatures and wind chill, statewide in winter.
- **Tornadoes and severe thunderstorms** — peak spring through summer, statewide, with damaging wind and hail.
- **Flooding** — river and flash flooding, especially in spring snowmelt and after heavy rain.
- **Lake-effect snow** — localized heavy snow bands downwind of Lake Michigan and Lake Superior.
