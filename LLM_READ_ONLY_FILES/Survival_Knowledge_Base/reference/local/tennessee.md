# Local Emergency Reference — Tennessee

**Compiled 2026-08 — verify current details when possible.**

## 1. Read this first

This is a **light** reference sheet: short, stable facts only (agency names, websites, office locations) — not a phonebook of utilities, hospitals, or repeater frequencies, which go stale fast. Call **911** in a life-threatening emergency if phones work.

National constants that don't change by state: **911** (emergency), **988** (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline), **211** (health/human services referral), **1-800-222-1222** (Poison Control), **1-800-985-5990** (Disaster Distress Helpline), **511** (traveler/road info, where participating), and NOAA Weather Radio's seven-channel band (162.400–162.550 MHz — local transmitter determines the exact frequency).

For a fuller, deep-tier example of this document type (utilities, trauma centers, ARRL/ARES, per-metro detail), see **texas.md**, **oklahoma.md**, or **arkansas.md** in this same directory. For radio specifics see `../comms-frequencies.md`; for maps see `../maps/INDEX.md`.

## 2. State emergency management

**Tennessee Emergency Management Authority (TEMA)** — website: **tn.gov/tema**. As of July 1, 2026, the agency was elevated to a cabinet-level department and its name changed from "Tennessee Emergency Management Agency" to "Tennessee Emergency Management Authority" (the TEMA acronym is unchanged). Headquartered in Nashville, with regional offices in Nashville, Jackson, Knoxville, and Chattanooga. Main phone: **(615) 741-0001**. Source: tn.gov/tema (verified live).

Public alerting reaches residents primarily through the federal **Wireless Emergency Alerts (WEA)** system, delivered via the **Integrated Public Alert and Warning System (IPAWS)**, which TEMA and local emergency managers use to issue warnings for tornadoes, flash floods, and other imminent threats. No single statewide opt-in notification brand was verified during this compile; many Tennessee counties and cities run their own local alert sign-up systems (e.g., through the county emergency management agency) — check with your county EMA for local enrollment options. Source: tn.gov/tema.

## 3. Weather alerts

Tennessee is covered by **three NWS Weather Forecast Offices**:

- **Nashville (OHX)** — Middle Tennessee
- **Memphis (MEG)** — West Tennessee and the Mid-South
- **Morristown (MRX)** — East Tennessee

Start at **weather.gov** and use the office pages (weather.gov/ohx, weather.gov/meg, weather.gov/mrx) for current watches, warnings, and hazardous weather outlooks for your county. Source: weather.gov.

**NOAA Weather Radio (NWR):** broadcasts on one of seven dedicated VHF channels (162.400–162.550 MHz); the specific frequency depends on your nearest transmitter. Look yours up at **weather.gov/nwr** before you need it.

## 4. Roads & travel

Tennessee's official traveler-information resource is **TDOT SmartWay** (smartway.tn.gov), run by the Tennessee Department of Transportation, with live traffic cameras (Nashville, Memphis, Knoxville, Chattanooga metro areas), incident data, and construction/closure information. The same data is also reachable at **TN511.com**. A statewide **511** phone service is active and confirmed: dial **511** from inside Tennessee, or **1-877-244-0065** from outside the state. Source: tn.gov/tdot, smartway.tn.gov.

## 5. Shelters & mass care

Tennessee is served by the **American Red Cross Tennessee Region** (redcross.org/local/tennessee), made up of eight local chapters (East Tennessee, Heart of Tennessee, Mid-South, Mid-West Tennessee, Nashville Area, Southeast Tennessee, Northeast Tennessee, and Tennessee River) covering all 95 counties. To find an open shelter during an active disaster: check the **Red Cross Emergency app**, call **211**, or watch local news/EAS broadcasts — shelter locations open and close as an event evolves, so don't rely on a static list. Source: redcross.org/local/tennessee.

## 6. Main hazards

- **Tornadoes** — Tennessee sees tornadoes statewide, with West and Middle Tennessee facing especially high risk; both spring and (less commonly) fall/winter outbreaks occur.
- **Severe thunderstorms** — damaging straight-line winds, hail, and lightning are common statewide, especially spring through summer.
- **Flooding & flash flooding** — river flooding and flash flooding affect all regions, with East Tennessee's mountainous terrain prone to fast-rising flash floods.
- **Winter storms** — ice storms and snow can disable roads and power, with East Tennessee's higher elevations (and occasional statewide events) most affected.
- **Earthquake (New Madrid Seismic Zone)** — West Tennessee lies within the New Madrid Seismic Zone, one of the most active earthquake zones in the central/eastern U.S.; East Tennessee also has its own, less severe, seismic zone.
