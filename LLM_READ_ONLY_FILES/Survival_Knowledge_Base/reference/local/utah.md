# Local Emergency Reference — Utah

**Compiled 2026-08 — verify current details when possible.**

## 1. Read this first

This is a **light** reference sheet: short, stable facts only (agency names, websites, office locations) — not a phonebook of utilities, hospitals, or repeater frequencies, which go stale fast. Call **911** in a life-threatening emergency if phones work.

National constants that don't change by state: **911** (emergency), **988** (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline), **211** (health/human services referral), **1-800-222-1222** (Poison Control), **1-800-985-5990** (Disaster Distress Helpline), **511** (traveler/road info, where participating), and NOAA Weather Radio's seven-channel band (162.400–162.550 MHz — local transmitter determines the exact frequency).

For a fuller, deep-tier example of this document type (utilities, trauma centers, ARRL/ARES, per-metro detail), see **texas.md**, **oklahoma.md**, or **arkansas.md** in this same directory. For radio specifics see `../comms-frequencies.md`; for maps see `../maps/INDEX.md`.

## 2. State emergency management

**Utah Division of Emergency Management (DEM)**, part of the Utah Department of Public Safety — website: **dem.utah.gov**. Main office phone: **801-538-3400** (Mon–Fri business hours). Source: dem.utah.gov/contact (verified live).

Utah does not appear to run a distinct, state-branded public alerting system; public warnings go out through the standard federal **Wireless Emergency Alerts (WEA)** and Emergency Alert System (EAS), issued locally by county/city emergency management and the National Weather Service via IPAWS. Utah DEM also runs **Be Ready Utah** (beready.utah.gov), which is a preparedness/education program, not an alert-delivery system — don't confuse the two. Check with your county emergency management office for any local opt-in notification service. Source: dem.utah.gov.

## 3. Weather alerts

Utah is covered by **three NWS Weather Forecast Offices**:

- **Salt Lake City (SLC)** — primary office, covering most of Utah plus southwest Wyoming
- **Grand Junction, CO (GJT)** — covers a slice of eastern Utah
- **Las Vegas, NV (VEF)** — covers southwestern Utah, including the St. George/Washington County area

Start at **weather.gov** and use the office pages (weather.gov/slc, weather.gov/gjt, weather.gov/vef) for current watches, warnings, and hazardous weather outlooks for your county. Source: weather.gov.

**NOAA Weather Radio (NWR):** broadcasts on one of seven dedicated VHF channels (162.400–162.550 MHz); the specific frequency depends on your nearest transmitter. Look yours up at **weather.gov/nwr** before you need it.

## 4. Roads & travel

Utah's official road-conditions resource is **UDOT Traffic** (udottraffic.utah.gov), run by the Utah Department of Transportation, with live cameras, incident data, road-weather alerts, and a companion app; a general UDOT site also exists at udot.utah.gov. **511** is available statewide in Utah for automated road-condition information (dial 511 from any Utah phone, or 1-866-511-UTAH from outside the state). Source: udottraffic.utah.gov, udot.utah.gov.

## 5. Shelters & mass care

Utah is served by the **American Red Cross Utah Region**, delivered through local chapters including the **Greater Salt Lake Area Chapter**, the **Northern Utah Chapter**, and the **Central & Southern Utah Chapter** (with an additional office in St. George). To find an open shelter during an active disaster: check the **Red Cross Emergency app**, call **211**, or watch local news/EAS broadcasts — shelter locations open and close as an event evolves, so don't rely on a static list. Source: redcross.org/local/utah.

## 6. Main hazards

- **Earthquakes** — the Wasatch Fault runs along Utah's most populated corridor (Salt Lake City to Provo/Ogden); the state has a documented history of magnitude 6.5+ quakes and remains a significant seismic risk.
- **Wildfire** — dry summers and extensive wildland-urban interface make wildfire a statewide seasonal risk, especially in drought years.
- **Winter storms & avalanche** — heavy mountain snowfall brings avalanche danger in backcountry terrain and can disrupt travel over mountain passes.
- **Drought** — recurring drought affects water supply, agriculture, and wildfire risk across much of the state.
- **Flooding & debris flows** — flash flooding (including slot canyons) and post-wildfire debris flows are a recurring hazard identified in the state's hazard mitigation plan.
