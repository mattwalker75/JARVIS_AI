## 1. Read this first

This is a **light-tier** reference: short, stable facts only — no per-utility phone numbers, no hospital lists, no repeater frequencies (those go stale). Compiled 2026-08 — verify current details when possible. In a life-threatening emergency, call **911** if phones work.

National constants worth memorizing: **911** (emergencies), **988** (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline), **211** (health/human services referrals), **1-800-222-1222** (Poison Control), **1-800-985-5990** (Disaster Distress Helpline), **511** (traveler/road info, in participating states — see note below for Washington), and NOAA Weather Radio (one of 162.400–162.550 MHz, seven channels — local transmitter determines which; look yours up at weather.gov/nwr).

For a fuller, deeper-tier model of this kind of sheet, see `../local/texas.md`, `../local/oklahoma.md`, and `../local/arkansas.md`. For radio specifics see `../comms-frequencies.md`; for maps see `../maps/INDEX.md`.

## 2. State emergency management

**Washington Emergency Management Division (EMD)**, part of the Washington Military Department, is the state's lead emergency management agency. Website: **mil.wa.gov/emergency-management-division**. No phone number is published on the main EMD page that could be verified as current — use the site's contact page rather than a memorized number.

Washington issues public alerts through **Wireless Emergency Alerts (WEA)** and, per the EMD site, the **Washington Integrated Public Alert & Warning System (WA-PAWS)**, the state's implementation of FEMA's national IPAWS platform. Counties and tribes also operate their own opt-in notification systems layered on top — check your local county emergency management page for sign-up options.

## 3. Weather alerts

Washington is covered by four National Weather Service Weather Forecast Offices — no single office serves the whole state:

- **NWS Seattle/Tacoma, WA** (weather.gov/sew) — Puget Sound, the Olympic Peninsula, and western Washington north of the Portland office's area.
- **NWS Spokane, WA** (weather.gov/otx) — northeast and east-central Washington, and the Idaho Panhandle.
- **NWS Portland, OR** (weather.gov/pqr) — covers southwest Washington in addition to northwest Oregon.
- **NWS Pendleton, OR** (weather.gov/pdt) — covers part of southeast Washington in addition to northeast Oregon.

Check current watches/warnings at weather.gov (enter your town or zip). NOAA Weather Radio broadcasts on one of seven frequencies between 162.400 and 162.550 MHz; find your local transmitter and frequency at weather.gov/nwr.

## 4. Roads & travel

The **Washington State Department of Transportation (WSDOT)** publishes real-time traffic, mountain pass, ferry, and camera conditions at **wsdot.com/travel/real-time/** (also reachable via wsdot.wa.gov). Note: Washington **discontinued its 511 phone service on May 19, 2023** after roughly 20 years, citing declining call volume and aging technology — dialing 511 in-state will not reach WSDOT traveler information. Use the website or the WSDOT mobile app instead; separate phone lines exist for WSDOT ferries and tolling, listed on the WSDOT site.

## 5. Shelters & mass care

Most of Washington is served by the **American Red Cross Northwest Region**, which the Red Cross describes as serving Washington State and five northern counties of Idaho (redcross.org/local/washington.html); southwest Washington falls within the reach of the Red Cross's Oregon-based **Cascades Region** chapters as well. To find open shelters during a disaster, use the Red Cross Emergency App, call 211, or check local news outlets — shelter locations are event-specific and not listed here because they change with every incident.

## 6. Main hazards

- **Earthquakes** — Washington averages over 1,000 quakes a year; risks include the offshore Cascadia Subduction Zone (capable of a major/great quake) and shallow crustal faults such as the Seattle Fault.
- **Volcanic hazards** — five major Cascade volcanoes (including Mt. Rainier, Mt. St. Helens, and Mt. Baker) pose eruption and lahar (volcanic mudflow) risks even during long dormant periods.
- **Wildfire** — a major and growing hazard, especially in eastern Washington's drier forests and grasslands.
- **Flooding** — the state EM agency notes flood damage exceeds that from all other natural hazards combined, driven by heavy rain and snowmelt in western Washington river valleys.
- **Landslides & winter storms** — steep, wet terrain makes landslides a recurring risk (e.g., the 2014 Oso disaster); mountain passes are subject to seasonal closures from snow and avalanche danger.
