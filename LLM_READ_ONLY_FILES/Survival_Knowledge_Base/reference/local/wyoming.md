## 1. Read this first

This is a **light-tier** reference: short, stable facts only — no per-utility phone numbers, no hospital lists, no repeater frequencies (those go stale). Compiled 2026-08 — verify current details when possible. In a life-threatening emergency, call **911** if phones work.

National constants worth memorizing: **911** (emergencies), **988** (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline), **211** (health/human services referrals), **1-800-222-1222** (Poison Control), **1-800-985-5990** (Disaster Distress Helpline), **511** (traveler/road info, participating states), and NOAA Weather Radio (one of 162.400–162.550 MHz, seven channels — local transmitter determines which).

For a fuller, deeper-tier model of this kind of sheet, see `../local/texas.md`, `../local/oklahoma.md`, and `../local/arkansas.md`. For radio specifics see `../comms-frequencies.md`; for maps see `../maps/INDEX.md`.

## 2. State emergency management

**Wyoming Department of Homeland Security (WYDHS)** — formerly known as the Wyoming Office of Homeland Security, site still hosted at **hls.wyo.gov** — is the state's lead emergency management agency, headquartered in Cheyenne. Main line: **(307) 777-4900**.

Wyoming issues public alerts through **Wireless Emergency Alerts (WEA)** and the broadcast **Emergency Alert System (EAS)**, both routed through FEMA's national **IPAWS** platform (WYDHS lists IPAWS among its programs). There is no separate statewide-branded public-alert app; several counties layer their own notification systems (e.g., CodeRed or Everbridge-based county alerts) on top of WEA/EAS — check your county's emergency management page for local sign-up.

## 3. Weather alerts

Wyoming is large, sparsely populated, and split among **five** NWS Weather Forecast Offices — no single office covers the whole state:

- **NWS Riverton, WY** (weather.gov/riw) — western and central Wyoming.
- **NWS Cheyenne, WY** (weather.gov/cys) — southeastern Wyoming (joint office, also covers the western Nebraska Panhandle).
- **NWS Billings, MT** (weather.gov/byz) — northern Wyoming (e.g., Sheridan area).
- **NWS Rapid City, SD** (weather.gov/unr) — northeastern Wyoming (Black Hills/Crook-Weston county area).
- **NWS Salt Lake City, UT** (weather.gov/slc) — southwestern Wyoming (e.g., Evanston area).

Check current watches/warnings at weather.gov (enter your town or zip — it will route you to the correct office automatically). NOAA Weather Radio broadcasts on one of seven frequencies between 162.400 and 162.550 MHz; find your local transmitter and frequency at weather.gov/nwr.

## 4. Roads & travel

The Wyoming Department of Transportation (WYDOT) publishes real-time road conditions, closures, and camera feeds at **wyoroad.info** (also linked from dot.state.wy.us). **511** works statewide in Wyoming — dial **511** or use the Wyoming 511 app/website for the same data, including a text/email "511 Notify" alert service. Given Wyoming's long distances between towns and frequent mountain-pass closures, check road conditions before any winter travel.

## 5. Shelters & mass care

Wyoming is served by the American Red Cross **Colorado & Wyoming Region** (redcross.org/local/wyoming.html), with a Wyoming chapter office in Cheyenne. To find open shelters during a disaster, use the Red Cross Emergency App, call 211, or check local news outlets — shelter locations are event-specific and not listed here because they change with every incident.

## 6. Main hazards

- **Severe winter storms & blizzards** — high winds combined with snow can produce whiteout conditions and road closures for hours to days, especially on I-80 and mountain passes, roughly October through April.
- **Extreme cold** — prolonged sub-zero temperatures, especially at higher elevations and in the Wind River/Bighorn basins, raising hypothermia and frostbite risk and stressing vehicles and utilities.
- **High wind** — Wyoming is one of the windiest states; sustained wind and gusts regularly close highways to high-profile vehicles and can down power lines independent of any storm.
- **Wildfire** — grass, sagebrush, and forested areas are at high risk in summer and early fall, particularly during drought years.
- **Flash flooding** — sudden, intense summer thunderstorms can produce flash floods in canyons and low-lying areas with little warning.
- **Remote/high-elevation travel hazards** — long distances between towns and services, high mountain passes, and limited cell coverage mean vehicle breakdowns or accidents can become survival situations; carry a winter car-safety kit.
