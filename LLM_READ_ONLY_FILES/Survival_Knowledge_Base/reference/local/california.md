# Local Emergency Reference — California

## 1. Read this first

This is a **light-tier** sheet: short, stable facts only, no phone numbers/frequencies/hospital lists that go stale fast. **Call 911 in a life-threatening emergency if phones work.** National constants that don't change: **988** (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline), **211** (health/human services), **1-800-222-1222** (Poison Control), **1-800-985-5990** (Disaster Distress Helpline), **511** (traveler/road info, where participating). NOAA Weather Radio broadcasts on one of seven fixed VHF channels (162.400–162.550 MHz); your local transmitter determines which. For the fuller "deep" format with per-utility numbers and specific frequencies, see `texas.md`, `oklahoma.md`, or `arkansas.md` in this same directory. See also `../comms-frequencies.md` for radio guidance and `../maps/INDEX.md` for maps.

## 2. State emergency management

California's state emergency management agency is the **Governor's Office of Emergency Services (Cal OES)** — official site **caloes.ca.gov**. Cal OES runs the **California State Warning Center**, the 24/7 point that coordinates alerting authorities statewide, and the **California Earthquake Early Warning Program** (ShakeAlert-powered, delivered via the MyShake app and Wireless Emergency Alerts) — see **earthquake.ca.gov**. Public alerts reach residents primarily through **Wireless Emergency Alerts (WEA)** pushed to cell phones (no signup needed, works even without a data plan as long as the local tower is up) plus the national **Emergency Alert System (EAS)** on broadcast TV/radio; Cal OES supports local counties/cities, which are the actual "alerting authorities" issuing most evacuation and shelter-in-place orders. Source: caloes.ca.gov.

## 3. Weather alerts

California is large enough to be covered by multiple **NWS Weather Forecast Offices**, each responsible for watches/warnings in its area (source: weather.gov):

- **Los Angeles/Oxnard** — southern coast/LA metro
- **San Diego** — far southern coast/border area
- **San Francisco Bay Area/Monterey** — central coast, Bay Area
- **Sacramento** — Central Valley, Sierra foothills
- **Hanford (San Joaquin Valley)** — southern Central Valley
- **Eureka** — north coast

The far eastern edges of the state (e.g., eastern Sierra, Death Valley) are served by neighboring-state offices (Reno, NV and Las Vegas, NV). Find your local forecast at **weather.gov** by ZIP or city. **NOAA Weather Radio** covers the state via local transmitters on the standard 7-channel band; look up your specific transmitter/frequency at **weather.gov/nwr**.

## 4. Roads & travel

Caltrans publishes real-time statewide road conditions, closures, and chain-control info at **QuickMap** (**quickmap.dot.ca.gov**, part of **dot.ca.gov**). **511 traveler information is available in California**, though it is organized regionally rather than as one single statewide system — e.g., **511.org** for the Bay Area, with similar regional 511 services (SoCal, San Diego, Sacramento, and others) elsewhere in the state. If unsure which applies to your area, QuickMap and dot.ca.gov are the statewide fallback.

## 5. Shelters & mass care

The **American Red Cross** serves California through several regions (source: redcross.org): **Northern California Coastal**, **Central California**, **Los Angeles**, and **California Gold Country**. To find an open shelter during an active disaster, use the **Red Cross Emergency app**, call **211**, or check local news/EAS broadcasts — shelter locations open and close as an event evolves, so confirm before traveling to one.

## 6. Main hazards

- **Earthquakes** — statewide risk, especially along the San Andreas and other major fault systems; know how to Drop, Cover, Hold On and consider signing up for ShakeAlert-based alerts.
- **Wildfire** — year-round risk in many areas, worst in late summer/fall during dry, windy conditions; know your evacuation zone and route.
- **Drought & water restrictions** — recurring, multi-year cycles affect water supply and wildfire fuel loads statewide.
- **Flooding & atmospheric rivers** — Pacific storms can bring intense rain and rapid flooding, particularly to the Central Valley and burn-scar areas.
- **Mudslides/debris flows** — a major risk after wildfire strips hillside vegetation, especially during the first heavy rains that follow a burn.
