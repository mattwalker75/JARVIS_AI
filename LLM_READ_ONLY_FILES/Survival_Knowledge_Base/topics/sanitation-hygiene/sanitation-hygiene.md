# Emergency Sanitation and Hygiene

## Read this first (the 30-second version)
1. **When toilets stop flushing, human waste becomes the #1 threat to your health** — more people die from disease caused by poor sanitation and dirty hands than from almost any other emergency hazard except unsafe water.
2. **If you still have some water:** flush manually by pouring 1–2 gallons of water fast and hard into the bowl — this is your bridge solution while you set up something longer-term.
3. **If water is scarce or sewage lines are broken:** build a **twin-bucket toilet** (a bucket with a bag liner, used with a scoop of cover material after every use) as your main system, and know how to dig a **cat-hole** or **latrine** outdoors.
4. **Wash your hands** with soap and water (20 seconds) after every toilet use and before handling food — this single habit prevents more illness than almost anything else you can do.
5. **Keep waste, garbage, and sick people separated from your clean water, food, and living space.** Distance and containment are your main tools when you have no sewer system.

The rest of this guide walks a total beginner through every step: manual flushing, building emergency toilets, digging latrines correctly, handwashing without running water, washing bodies/dishes/laundry with little water, garbage and pest control, menstrual hygiene, isolating a sick person, making basic soap, and handling greywater.

---

## Why this matters
After unsafe drinking water, **poor sanitation is the next biggest killer in a disaster**. When toilets stop working, people are tempted to just "go" somewhere convenient — behind the shed, in a corner of the yard, in a bucket that never gets covered. Within days, flies move between waste and your food, contaminated water runs into wells or streams, and hands carry germs from waste to mouths. The result is outbreaks of diarrhea, cholera, hepatitis A, and other fecal-oral diseases — exactly the illnesses that dehydrate people fastest, at the moment clean water and medical care are hardest to get. Small children, the elderly, and anyone already sick or injured are hit hardest. Almost all of this is preventable with three cheap habits: **contain waste, wash hands, keep flies away from food and waste.** This guide exists because sanitation is unglamorous and gets skipped — and that is exactly why it kills.

## Key terms (plain language)
- **Greywater** — used household water from washing (dishes, laundry, bathing) that has NOT touched toilet waste. Less dangerous than blackwater but still needs care.
- **Blackwater** — water contaminated with human feces (toilet waste, sewage). Always treat as infectious.
- **Cat-hole** — a small, individual hole dug for a single use of feces, then covered immediately. The basic wilderness/short-term method.
- **Latrine** — a larger, dug pit designed for repeated/group use over days to weeks, with a cover and often a seat or slab.
- **Cover material** — dry material (sawdust, dry leaves, ash, dirt, kitty litter, lime) added after each use to reduce smell, flies, and visible waste.
- **Fecal-oral route** — the way disease spreads: germs from feces get onto hands, food, water, or objects, and then into another person's mouth. Breaking this chain is the entire point of sanitation and handwashing.
- **Vector** — a living carrier of disease, mainly flies and rodents in this context. They move germs from waste/garbage onto food and surfaces.
- **Potable water** — water that is safe to drink. Never let it mix with greywater or blackwater.
- **Tippy-tap** — a simple hands-free handwashing device (hanging water container tipped by a foot pedal or string) that uses very little water and avoids contaminating the water supply with dirty hands.

## What you need
Most of this can be improvised from things already in a typical home or vehicle.
- **For toilets:** 5-gallon buckets (2 or more), heavy-duty plastic trash bags or contractor bags, a pool-noodle or foam "seat" (optional comfort item), a lid that seals, duct tape.
- **Cover material:** sawdust, dry leaves/grass, dry soil, wood ash, unscented clay-based kitty litter, or hydrated lime.
- **Digging tools:** shovel, entrenching tool, or a sturdy stick/garden trowel if nothing else is available.
- **Hand hygiene:** soap (bar or liquid), alcohol-based hand sanitizer (60%+ alcohol), a jug or tippy-tap setup, nail brush (optional).
- **Cleaning/disinfecting:** unscented household chlorine bleach, or calcium hypochlorite pool shock (unscented, no added algaecides) — pool shock is much more concentrated than household bleach and must **not** be measured using the household-bleach ratios in Section 8; see the stock-solution note there — rubber gloves, a dedicated "dirty" bucket and rag never used for food.
- **Garbage/pest control:** lidded trash containers, more trash bags, duct tape, screen material or mesh.
- **Menstrual hygiene:** pads/tampons/menstrual cup if stocked, plus cloth, water, and a way to wash/dry cloth privately.
- **Isolation supplies:** a separate room or tent area, dedicated dishes/linens for the sick person, gloves, mask if available, extra bleach solution.
- **Soap-making (optional/advanced):** lye (sodium hydroxide) OR hardwood ash, animal fat or vegetable oil, heat-safe (non-aluminum) containers, eye protection, gloves.

## 1. Manually flushing a toilet with a bucket
If the sewer line is intact but the pump/electric water supply is out, your toilet can usually still be used — you just have to supply the flush water yourself. This is the fastest bridge solution and buys you time before building a dedicated emergency toilet.

1. **Check first:** if you know or suspect sewer lines are broken (earthquake, major flood, city notice to stop flushing), **do not** flush into the system — skip to the bucket-toilet sections below instead.
2. Fill a bucket with **1–2 gallons of water** — greywater (used dish or bath water) works fine for this; save clean water for drinking.
3. Pour the water into the bowl **quickly and all at once**, aiming into the bowl opening, not around the rim. A fast, forceful pour creates the siphon action that carries waste down, the same way the tank normally does.
4. If it doesn't clear fully, add another 1–2 gallons the same way.
5. Do **not** pour water into the tank — pouring directly into the bowl is what makes the flush work.
6. Keep a lid on the toilet between uses to cut down on smell and flies.

## 2. Building the twin-bucket emergency toilet
When there's no working sewer connection at all, this is the standard, low-cost, low-water system used in shelters and homes worldwide.

1. **Bucket #1 — the toilet.** Line a clean 5-gallon bucket with a heavy-duty trash bag (double-bag if your bags are thin). Add an inch of cover material (sawdust, kitty litter, ash, or dry dirt) to the bottom of the bag before first use.
2. **After every use** (urine or feces), add a scoop of cover material over the waste — this is the single most important habit. It controls odor, keeps flies from finding the waste, and starts drying it out.
3. Keep the lid **closed** between uses.
4. **Bucket #2 — backup/rotation.** When bucket #1 is about two-thirds full, carefully tie off the liner bag (twist and knot it, or tape it shut) and set it aside for disposal. Swap in bucket #2 (or a fresh liner) so the toilet is never out of service.
5. **Disposal:** store sealed bags in a covered outdoor container away from living space, water sources, and food storage until they can be picked up by waste services, buried (see latrine burial depth below), or handled per local emergency guidance. Never put sealed human-waste bags in general household recycling.
6. **Optional comfort upgrade:** cut a hole in a foam pool noodle sliced lengthwise to make a padded seat rim for the bucket.
7. Wash your hands immediately after handling the bucket, liner, or lid (see Section 4).

> Tip: keep urine separate if possible (a second, smaller bucket or a funnel/jug for urine only) — urine is normally sterile from a healthy person and produces far less odor and volume than mixed waste, so separating it can extend how long your solid-waste bucket lasts.

## 3. Digging a cat-hole (single-use, short-term)
Use a cat-hole when you or one person need a one-time bathroom trip and are away from a fixed camp — for example, during evacuation or while a fixed latrine is being built.

1. **Choose the site:** at least **200 feet (about 70 adult steps)** from any water source — stream, pond, well, or standing water — and away from trails, campsites, and food storage.
2. Pick ground that is **downhill and downwind** of your living/camp area if possible, so smell and any runoff move away from you, not toward you.
3. Dig a hole **6–8 inches deep** (about the length of a small garden trowel blade) and 4–6 inches wide. This depth sits in the biologically active topsoil layer where decomposition happens fastest.
4. Use the hole, then immediately **cover completely** with the loose dirt you removed, and pack it down lightly.
5. Disguise the spot (leaves, brush) so it isn't reused by mistake, and note it if others are using the same area so a new hole is dug for the next person, spaced well apart from prior ones.
6. **Toilet paper:** bury it in the hole with the waste, or pack it out in a sealed bag if you're in a sensitive or shared area — never leave it on the surface.

## 4. Digging a slit trench or deep latrine (group/longer-term use)
When more than one or two people will need a toilet over several days or weeks, a dug latrine is more practical than repeated cat-holes.

1. **Site selection (same rules, bigger stakes):**
   - At least **200 feet (70+ steps)** from any well, stream, pond, or stored water.
   - **Downhill** of tents/shelter and any water sources, so nothing can drain toward them.
   - **Downwind** of the living area (check the prevailing wind direction).
   - Away from where food is stored or prepared, and away from where rainwater visibly collects or flows.
2. **Slit trench (short group use, days):** dig a trench about **1 foot wide, 1 foot deep, and 3–4 feet long** per expected group of users. Straddle it to use; shovel a little soil over waste after each use from a pile left at one end.
3. **Deep/pit latrine (longer use, weeks):** dig a pit roughly **3 feet long, 1 foot wide, and 4 feet deep** (deeper for more people or longer duration). Build a simple cover — planks or a slab with a hole — over the pit so people don't step in it, and add a seat if materials allow.
4. **Add cover material after every use**, same as the bucket toilet — a scoop of ash, dirt, or lime keeps odor and flies down and speeds decomposition.
5. **Keep a lid or cover** over the opening between uses (a board, tarp flap, or lid) to block flies and rain.
6. **When a pit is within about 1 foot of full,** stop using it: backfill the remaining space with soil, mound and pack the earth on top, and mark the spot clearly (stake, rocks) so it isn't dug into later. Start a new pit in a fresh location following the same siting rules.
7. **Handwashing station** goes right next to the latrine, never inside it — see Section 5.

## 5. Hand hygiene — the single most protective habit
1. **Handwashing steps (with water):**
   1. Wet hands with clean water.
   2. Apply soap and rub **all** surfaces — palms, backs of hands, between fingers, under nails, wrists — for at least **20 seconds** (about the time it takes to say the alphabet slowly).
   3. Rinse under running or poured water (don't dip hands back into a shared basin of standing water).
   4. Dry with a clean cloth or air-dry; a dirty towel can re-contaminate hands.
2. **When to wash, no exceptions:** after using the toilet/bucket/latrine, before and after preparing or eating food, after touching garbage or a sick person, after handling animals, after cleaning up any waste.
3. **No running water? Build a tippy-tap:** hang a water-filled jug (with a small hole or the cap loosely on) from a tripod of sticks or a tree branch, tied so a foot pedal (a stick tied to the jug with cord) tips it to pour a thin stream over your hands. This lets you wash hands touch-free with very little water (a liter can serve many washes) and keeps dirty hands from contaminating the water source.
4. **No soap? Use wood ash.** Clean hardwood ash (not from treated lumber, charcoal briquettes, or trash) has a mild alkaline/lye effect that helps lift grease and germs — wet hands, rub in a small amount of ash like you would soap, then rinse thoroughly. It's a real fallback, not as good as soap, but far better than water alone.
5. **Hand sanitizer** (60%+ alcohol) is a reasonable backup when hands aren't visibly dirty and water is very scarce, but it does **not** work well on visibly soiled or greasy hands — wash with soap and water first whenever you can.
6. **Keep food and water areas clean:** designate one surface/area only for food prep, wash it between tasks, keep it away from the toilet/latrine area, and always wash hands before touching food or water containers.

## 6. Washing bodies, dishes, and laundry with minimal water
1. **Body washing:** a "sponge bath" uses far less water than a shower — wet a cloth, add a little soap, wash and rinse in sections (face, underarms, groin, feet first — these matter most for skin infections). A liter or two can wash one person adequately.
2. **Dishes (three-basin method, or improvised version):**
   1. **Wash** in soapy water.
   2. **Rinse** in a second basin of plain water.
   3. **Sanitize** in a third basin with a **capful of unscented bleach per gallon of water**, or air-dry in direct sunlight (UV helps kill germs) after rinsing.
   - No basins to spare? Wash, then pour a bleach-water rinse over the dishes and air-dry — don't towel-dry with a shared cloth.
3. **Laundry:** soak soiled clothes in soapy water, agitate/scrub by hand, wring out, rinse, and hang to dry in sunlight. For clothing soiled with waste or vomit/illness, add a bleach soak (see Section 8 dilution) before regular washing and wash separately from other laundry.
4. **Reuse water thoughtfully:** rinse water from a *clean* wash can often be reused for the next wash cycle or for flushing (see Section 1) — this is greywater, handle per Section 10.

## 7. Garbage and pest control (flies and rodents)
1. **Contain garbage immediately:** use lidded containers or tightly tied bags; don't let food scraps sit open.
2. **Separate wet food waste from dry trash** where possible — wet scraps attract flies and rodents fastest.
3. **Empty/seal trash daily** in hot weather; store sealed bags away from living and sleeping areas until they can be taken away or buried away from water sources.
4. **Flies breed in exposed waste within days** — the cover-material habit in Sections 2 and 4 is your main defense at the toilet; keep trash lidded for the same reason.
5. **Rodents** are drawn to any accessible food or garbage — store food in hard, sealed containers, elevate off the ground where possible, and keep the area around your shelter clear of loose trash and standing scraps.
6. **Simple fly control:** screens or mesh over openings, fly strips/tape if available, and — most effective of all — **removing what attracts them** (open waste, food scraps, standing greywater).

## 8. Disinfecting surfaces and gear
1. **General-purpose bleach solution:** 1 tablespoon (about 15 mL) unscented household bleach per gallon of water for everyday surface disinfecting.
2. **Stronger solution for waste spills/sick-room surfaces:** about 1 cup bleach per gallon of water. Apply, let sit at least 10 minutes, then wipe or rinse.
3. **If using calcium hypochlorite pool shock instead of household bleach, do not use the ratios above directly** — pool shock is roughly 65–73% available chlorine, several times stronger than the 6–8% in household bleach. First make a dilute stock solution (dissolve a small measured amount of the powder/granules in a larger volume of water, following the product label), then use that stock solution — not the dry powder or a guessed scoop — in place of "bleach" in the ratios above, adjusting downward and testing on a small area first. When in doubt, prefer household bleach, which is easier to dose safely.
4. Always wear gloves when cleaning up waste or vomit; wash hands after removing gloves.
5. Never mix bleach with ammonia or other cleaners — this produces dangerous toxic gas.

## 9. Menstrual hygiene
1. **Supplies:** stock pads, tampons, or a reusable menstrual cup ahead of time if possible — these keep working with no water needed for the cup after the initial boil-clean.
2. **Cloth as a fallback:** clean, soft, absorbent cloth (old t-shirts, flannel) folded as a pad works when commercial supplies run out.
3. **Washing reusable cloth/cups:** rinse in cold water first (hot water sets blood stains), then wash with soap in warm water; boil a menstrual cup for a few minutes to sanitize between cycles if you have a way to boil water.
4. **Drying:** dry cloth fully in sunlight when possible (UV and heat help sanitize); a damp pad reused too soon can cause skin irritation or infection.
5. **Disposal:** wrap used disposable products before placing in sealed garbage (see Section 7); never flush pads/tampons/wipes even into a working toilet, and never put them in a bucket toilet meant for composting-style disposal without a separate wrapped-waste plan.
6. **Privacy and dignity matter** for mental health in a crisis — set aside a private space and make sure supplies are distributed discreetly if managing this for a group.

## 10. Isolating a sick person to stop disease spread
1. **Separate space:** put the sick person in a room, tent, or corner physically apart from where others sleep and eat, with airflow if possible.
2. **Dedicated items:** the sick person gets their own cup, dish, utensil, towel, and bedding — wash these separately (hot water/bleach rinse) and don't let others use them.
3. **One caregiver if possible:** limiting contact to a single, healthy caregiver reduces how many people are exposed.
4. **Caregiver hygiene:** wash hands before and after every contact, wear gloves when handling waste/vomit/soiled linens, and consider a mask if respiratory illness is suspected.
5. **Dedicated toilet or bucket** for the sick person if you have enough buckets — otherwise disinfect the shared toilet/bucket area after each use by the sick person (bleach solution from Section 8).
6. **Waste from the sick person** (vomit, diarrhea, soiled linens) should be treated as more hazardous than normal: use the stronger bleach solution, gloves, and dispose of solid waste via the latrine/bucket system, never on open ground.
7. **Watch for dehydration** in the sick person — diarrhea and vomiting cause rapid fluid loss; keep offering safe water and oral rehydration if available (see the first-aid guide).
8. **When to seek outside medical help:** high fever, blood in stool/vomit, signs of severe dehydration, or symptoms spreading rapidly through the group are signals this is beyond home care — get the person to help if at all possible.

## 11. Making basic soap (optional, when supplies run out)
Soap is simple chemistry — fat plus a strong alkali (lye) — but the lye step is genuinely dangerous. Only attempt this if commercial soap is truly unavailable and unobtainable, and follow the safety warning below exactly.

1. **Making lye from wood ash (traditional, safer than handling commercial lye but slower):**
   1. Fill a container with holes in the bottom (or a barrel with a spigot) with clean hardwood ash.
   2. Slowly pour rainwater or soft water through the ash and let it filter through, collecting the liquid that drips out — this is a rough lye solution.
   3. Test strength: a fresh egg or small potato should float with a bit of it showing above the surface; if it sinks, the solution is too weak and needs to be run through fresh ash again.
2. **Or use commercial lye (sodium hydroxide)** for a more reliable, predictable result if you have it on hand — measured by weight against your fat, following a basic cold-process soap ratio (roughly 1 part lye to 6–7 parts fat by weight is a common beginner starting ratio, but exact ratios depend on the specific fat, so use a published beginner soap recipe/lye calculator if at all possible before this becomes your only plan).
3. **Combine:** slowly add the lye solution to melted fat/oil (never the reverse — see warning below), stirring continuously until it thickens to a pudding-like consistency ("trace").
4. **Pour** into a mold (a lined box or tray) and let it sit undisturbed for 24–48 hours until firm enough to cut.
5. **Cure** the cut bars in a dry, ventilated spot for **4–6 weeks** before use — fresh soap is still highly alkaline and will irritate or burn skin if used too soon.

> ⚠️ **LYE SAFETY WARNING:** Lye (sodium hydroxide, and the solution leached from wood ash) is **caustic** — it burns skin and eyes on contact and gives off dangerous fumes when mixed with water. **Always add lye to water/liquid, never water to lye** (adding water to concentrated lye can cause violent, dangerous splattering and boiling). Wear rubber gloves and eye protection, work in a ventilated area, keep children and pets away, and never use aluminum containers or utensils (lye reacts with aluminum). **If lye contacts skin or eyes, flush immediately and continuously with plenty of plain water for at least 15 minutes (30 minutes for eyes).** Do **not** try to neutralize a lye/base burn with vinegar or any acid — the reaction generates heat and will worsen the injury. Seek medical help for any eye contact or significant burn.

## 12. Greywater — using it safely
1. **What counts as greywater:** water from dishwashing, bathing, and laundry that has not touched toilet waste.
2. **Reuse it for:** manually flushing toilets (Section 1), watering ornamental (non-edible) plants, or as the "wash" step in a repeat cleaning cycle.
3. **Don't:** let greywater pool or stand more than a day (it breeds flies and smells), let it run toward a well or water source, or use it on food crops close to harvest, since soap and food particles in it can carry germs.
4. **Simple disposal:** pour into a shallow trench or gravel-filled soak pit away from the house, water sources, and food-growing areas, letting it soak into the ground rather than run across the surface.

## Regional & scenario variants

**North Texas (heat, humidity, and flies):** Summer heat here (routinely 95–105°F) turns any uncovered waste or garbage into a serious fly and odor problem within hours, not days — cover material after *every single use* is not optional, it's the difference between a manageable bucket toilet and a health hazard. Dig cat-holes and latrines a little deeper if the ground allows, since the top few inches of clay soil bake hard in summer and dry out fast, which actually slows decomposition — mixing in looser soil or ash with the waste helps. Keep garbage sealed and shaded; the combination of heat and humidity after storms accelerates both odor and pest breeding. Watch for fire ants when digging outdoor latrines/cat-holes in North Texas yards and fields — check the site before kneeling or digging.

**Urban/apartment settings:** No yard for a latrine means the twin-bucket system (Section 2) is usually your only realistic option — stock extra bags and cover material in advance. Balconies or fire escapes can work for temporary sealed-bag storage away from living space; coordinate with neighbors on a shared disposal plan since municipal pickup may be disrupted building-wide.

**Suburban:** A yard usually allows a proper cat-hole or small latrine if siting rules (200 ft from any well or water source) can be met — check lot size and property lines first; if the yard is too small, default to the bucket system.

**Rural/larger property:** Latrines are most practical here — you likely have room to meet the distance/downhill/downwind rules and can rotate pit locations over a longer emergency. Still keep pits well clear of any well, pond, or livestock water source.

## ⚠️ Safety
- **Never** site any latrine, cat-hole, or waste storage within 200 feet (about 70 steps) of a well, stream, pond, or any water source — contaminated groundwater can spread disease to an entire household or neighborhood.
- **Never** mix bleach with ammonia or other cleaning chemicals — this creates toxic gas.
- **Never** add water to concentrated lye — always add lye to water/liquid, and always wear gloves and eye protection when making soap.
- **Never** let raw sewage or waste-bucket contents drain toward your home, garden, or any water source.
- **Always** wash hands after handling waste, garbage, buckets, or a sick person's items, even when water feels too precious to "waste" on washing — it isn't wasted, it's protecting your whole group.
- **Always** keep a dedicated "dirty" bucket, gloves, and cloth for waste/vomit cleanup that never touches food-prep areas.
- Watch children closely around uncovered pits/latrines and bucket toilets; keep lids closed and consider blocking access when not supervised.
- If sewer lines are known or suspected to be broken (major earthquake, flood, utility notice), **stop flushing into the system** even manually — switch fully to the bucket/latrine method until authorities confirm the sewer system is intact.

## Troubleshooting
- **Bucket toilet smells bad already:** you're likely skipping cover material on some uses, or the liner bag isn't sealed between uses — add a full scoop every time and keep the lid closed.
- **Flies swarming the latrine/bucket:** cover material layer is too thin or missing, or the lid/cover slab is being left open — thicken the cover layer and enforce "close it every time."
- **Latrine pit filling faster than expected:** likely under-sized for your group — start a second pit early rather than waiting until the first is completely full, and add cover material more generously to slow the fill rate.
- **No cover material on hand:** dry dirt works in a pinch even without ash or sawdust; just make sure it's dry, not damp, or it won't control odor/flies well.
- **Hands still feel dirty after ash-washing:** ash is a real fallback but weaker than soap — combine with more water and more scrubbing time, and switch to soap or sanitizer the moment any is available.
- **Soap won't reach "trace"/thicken:** lye solution may be too weak (from under-strength ash lye) or fat too cool — for wood-ash lye, test with the egg-float method and re-run through fresh ash if it's too weak; for melted fat, keep temperatures similar between the lye solution and fat when combining.
- **Someone in the group is now showing symptoms after using a shared toilet:** isolate them immediately (Section 10), switch to a dedicated bucket/toilet for them if possible, and step up disinfection of all shared touch points.

## Quick-reference checklist
- [ ] Know how to manually flush with 1–2 gallons poured fast into the bowl (only if sewer lines are intact).
- [ ] Twin-bucket toilet set up: liner bags, cover material, sealed lid, rotation plan.
- [ ] Cat-hole rule memorized: 6–8 in deep, 200 ft / 70 steps from water, downhill/downwind, cover immediately.
- [ ] Latrine rule memorized: same siting rules, bigger pit, cover after every use, backfill and mark when full.
- [ ] Soap, hand sanitizer, and a tippy-tap or pour method ready for handwashing without running water.
- [ ] Know the wood-ash handwashing fallback if soap runs out.
- [ ] Bleach on hand for surface disinfection (1 tbsp/gal general; ~1 cup/gal for waste spills) — never mixed with ammonia.
- [ ] Garbage kept lidded, sealed, and separated from food/water areas; fly and rodent control in place.
- [ ] Menstrual supplies stocked, with a cloth/cup fallback plan and private washing/disposal method.
- [ ] Isolation plan ready: separate space, dedicated dishes/linens, one caregiver, extra disinfectant.
- [ ] Lye-safety rules understood before attempting any soap-making: lye into water (never reverse), gloves, eye protection, no aluminum containers.
- [ ] Greywater reuse and disposal plan that never crosses paths with drinking water.
