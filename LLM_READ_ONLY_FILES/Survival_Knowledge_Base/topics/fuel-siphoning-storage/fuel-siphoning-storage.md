# Fuel — Siphoning, Storage, Shelf Life & Alternative Sources

Fuel runs your vehicle, your generator, and often your heat. In a long outage or evacuation, the
problem is rarely "how do I burn it" — it's **how do I safely move it, keep it, and tell if it's still
good.** This guide assumes no internet and no special equipment. A person who has never siphoned or
stored fuel can follow it.

> ⚠️ **THE FOUR RULES THAT KEEP YOU ALIVE — read before doing anything with fuel:**
> 1. **NEVER siphon fuel by sucking on the hose with your mouth.** Swallowing gasoline, or breathing
>    it into your lungs (aspiration), causes chemical pneumonia and poisoning that can kill. Use a pump
>    or a self-priming (jiggler) siphon — never your lungs. If someone swallows or inhales fuel, call
>    **Poison Control 1-800-222-1222** and **911** if they are coughing, choking, or short of breath;
>    do **NOT** make them vomit (see [First Aid](../first-aid/first-aid.md)).
> 2. **Gasoline vapor — not the liquid — is what explodes.** Vapor is heavier than air, spreads along
>    the ground, and finds ignition sources yards away. Work **outdoors only**, never near a flame,
>    pilot light, cigarette, running engine, or anything that sparks.
> 3. **Control static electricity.** Fuel flowing through a hose or into a plastic container builds a
>    static charge that can spark. Always set the container **on the bare ground** (not in a truck bed,
>    on a car seat, or on plastic) while filling, and keep the hose/nozzle touching the container.
> 4. **Never store fuel indoors, in a living space, or near an ignition source,** and keep a **Class B
>    (flammable-liquid) fire extinguisher** within reach whenever you handle it.

# PART 1 — Fuel safety fundamentals (the non-negotiables)

- **Right container, right color.** Use only approved, sealed fuel containers: **red = gasoline**,
  **yellow = diesel**, **blue = kerosene**. Never store fuel in water jugs, milk jugs, or unmarked
  containers — people die from drinking mislabeled fuel, and thin plastic degrades and leaks.
- **Leave room and vent heat.** Don't fill a can to the very top — fuel expands with heat; leave ~5%
  air space. Store cans out of direct sun and away from temperature swings.
- **Ventilation and ignition.** Handle and store fuel where vapor can disperse (detached shed, garage
  with the door open, outdoors) — never near a furnace, water heater, generator, grill, or any pilot
  light.
- **Keep it off the water supply.** If you repurpose any hose or pump for fuel, it is **fuel-only
  forever** — gasoline residue and additives make it unsafe for drinking water even after rinsing.
- **Quantity/legal note.** As a rough safety and code guideline (NFPA 30), a home should keep no more
  than about **25 gallons of gasoline** in approved containers, stored away from living space. More
  than that belongs in purpose-built tanks. Diesel and kerosene are far less volatile and safer to
  store in larger amounts.

# PART 2 — Siphoning fuel

Siphoning means moving fuel from one tank/container to another. There are two honest realities to
understand **before** you try.

## 2.1 The modern-vehicle catch (read this first)
**Most vehicles built since roughly the mid-2000s — and nearly all since ~2010 — are designed to stop
you from siphoning through the fuel filler neck.** They have an **anti-siphon screen, a small
spring-loaded flapper door, and rollover/anti-spill valves** a few inches down the neck. A siphon hose
usually **hits that flap and will not pass into the tank.** This is deliberate (theft prevention and
crash safety).

- **Do not force a hose past it** — you can damage the neck or jam the hose.
- Newer vehicles (this includes a modern truck like a **2019 Tacoma**) commonly cannot be siphoned
  through the filler neck at all. Assume this is the case and plan around it.

**If the filler neck is blocked, your realistic options are:**
1. **Siphon from an OLDER vehicle** (roughly pre-2005) or from equipment that has an open tank — lawn
   mowers, older trucks, tractors, boats, ATVs, generators — where a hose reaches the fuel freely.
2. **Use the vehicle's own fuel system** (advanced, mechanically-inclined only): relieve fuel-rail
   pressure, disconnect the fuel line at the rail or filter, put that line into a container, and
   briefly power the electric fuel pump to push fuel out. This involves the pressurized fuel system and
   is a real fire/injury risk — only for someone who understands their vehicle. When in doubt, don't.
3. **Drop or tap the tank** (mechanic-level) — beyond the scope of an emergency for most people.

## 2.2 The safe methods (when the tank IS reachable)
**Best tool: a self-priming "jiggler"/shaker siphon or a hand pump** (cheap, worth keeping in your
kit). It starts the flow with no mouth suction.

**Hand-pump or jiggler siphon (preferred):**
1. Work **outside**, engines off, no ignition sources, container on the **bare ground** *below* the
   fuel source (siphons need the destination lower than the source, or a pump).
2. Feed the **source hose** down into the fuel until it's submerged.
3. **Jiggler siphon:** move the hose up and down sharply a few times to start the flow, then hold it
   steady and let gravity pull fuel into your container. **Hand/bulb pump:** squeeze the pump until
   fuel flows, then let it run.
4. To stop, **lift the source hose out of the fuel** or raise the container above the source.

**Gravity siphon with a pump primer — never mouth-start.** If you only have a plain hose and no pump,
you can start a siphon by fully submerging the hose to fill it with fuel, capping the low end with your
thumb, and lowering it — but this is messy and risky. **A $10 jiggler siphon removes all of this
danger; keep one in every vehicle and with your generator.**

**Hose material:** use **fuel-rated hose** if you have it. A garden/water hose will work briefly but
gasoline degrades rubber and some plastics — it can swell, harden, or leach, so treat a water hose as a
one-time improvisation and never reuse it for water.

## 2.3 Getting siphoned fuel into your tank/engine
- Pour from the container through a **funnel** into your gas can or, for a modern anti-siphon vehicle,
  directly into the filler neck (the flap opens **inward** for a proper nozzle/funnel spout, even
  though it blocks a siphon hose going the other way).
- Filter questionable fuel through a clean cloth or a fine funnel screen to catch rust/debris.

# PART 3 — Storing fuel

- **Containers:** approved, color-correct, tightly sealed, labeled with the **fill date**.
- **Location:** a detached shed or shaded outdoor spot; cool, stable temperature; never in the house,
  never near an ignition source, never in direct sun.
- **Fill level:** leave a little expansion room, but for **long storage a nearly full sealed can lasts
  longer** than a half-empty one (less air = less oxidation and less water condensation).
- **Stabilizer:** add a fuel stabilizer (e.g., STA-BIL) to any gasoline you plan to keep more than a
  few weeks. Follow the dose on the bottle (commonly ~1 oz per 2.5 gallons). Stabilizer dramatically
  extends gasoline life (see Part 4).
- **Rotation is the real secret:** don't just hoard fuel and forget it. **Use the oldest fuel in your
  vehicle or generator every few months and refill the can with fresh.** Rotated, stabilized fuel is
  always ready; forgotten fuel turns to varnish and clogs carburetors and injectors.

See [Emergency Power & Off-Grid Energy](../power-energy/power-energy.md) for generator refueling safety
(shut down and cool before refueling) and [Supplies & Inventory Rotation](../supplies-inventory-rotation/supplies-inventory-rotation.md) for building rotation into your routine.

# PART 4 — Shelf life & how to tell if fuel has gone bad

Fuel does **not** last forever. These are working shelf-life estimates for fuel stored in a sealed,
approved container in cool conditions:

| Fuel | Untreated shelf life | With stabilizer | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Gasoline (pure, no ethanol)** | ~6 months | up to ~1–3 years | Rare at US pumps; lasts longer than blended |
| **Gasoline (E10, normal US pump gas)** | **~3 months** | up to ~1–2 years | Ethanol absorbs water and can **phase-separate** |
| **Diesel** | ~6–12 months | 1 year+ (with biocide) | Prone to microbial "algae" growth if water gets in |
| **Kerosene** | ~2–5 years | longer sealed | Very stable; good for lamps/heaters |
| **Propane (LP)** | **Indefinite** | n/a | The fuel itself never degrades — best long-term store |

**How to tell gasoline has gone bad (any one of these = suspect it):**
- **Smell:** fresh gas is sharp and solvent-like; **old gas smells sour, stale, or like varnish/paint
  thinner.**
- **Color:** fresh gas is clear-to-pale; **old gas turns darker/amber or muddy.**
- **Separation:** with ethanol blends, water can pull the ethanol to the bottom (**phase separation**) —
  you'll see distinct layers. Separated fuel is **ruined**; do not run it.
- **Residue:** gummy, sticky, or varnish film in the container.

**Can you still use old fuel?**
- **Mildly stale, not separated:** you can often use it by **diluting it heavily with fresh fuel** —
  e.g., pour a small amount into a nearly full tank of good gas so the engine never runs on more than a
  fraction of old fuel. Best burned in a tolerant engine (older car, lawn mower) rather than a modern
  fuel-injected vehicle or generator.
- **Phase-separated, dark, or foul:** **do not use it** — it will run poorly and can damage the engine.
  Set it aside for proper disposal (a hazardous-waste site when services return); never pour fuel on the
  ground, down a drain, or into water.

# PART 5 — Alternative fuel sources in a prolonged outage

When you can't refill at a station, look around — usable fuel is often already on your property or
neighborhood (get permission where you can; in a true life-safety emergency, document what you take):

- **Other vehicles and equipment** (siphon per Part 2): cars, trucks, boats, ATVs, motorcycles, RVs.
- **Lawn/garden equipment:** mowers, trimmers, chainsaws, leaf blowers, pressure washers — small but
  open, easy-to-reach gas tanks.
- **Generators and pressure washers:** their tanks and any stored fuel cans nearby.
- **Propane:** BBQ grill tanks, RV tanks, and 1-lb camp cylinders — great for cooking and some heaters,
  and it stores indefinitely. See cooking/heating options in
  [Emergency Power & Off-Grid Energy](../power-energy/power-energy.md).
- **Diesel:** farm equipment, tractors, commercial trucks, home heating-oil tanks (heating oil ≈
  off-road diesel and can run diesel engines in a pinch).
- **Kerosene:** lamp and heater fuel; stable and long-lasting.

---

# PART 6 — Match the fuel to the engine (do NOT mix)

Putting the wrong fuel in an engine can destroy it or strand you:
- **Gas engine → gasoline only.** Diesel in a gas engine won't run.
- **Diesel engine → diesel only.** **Gasoline in a diesel engine is very damaging** — do not do it.
- **Two-stroke engines** (many chainsaws, older trimmers, some generators) need **gas pre-mixed with
  two-stroke oil** at the ratio on the equipment (e.g., 40:1 or 50:1) — straight gas will seize them.
- **Diesel ≈ heating oil / off-road diesel** are close enough to substitute in an emergency; **kerosene
  can be blended into diesel in small amounts** in cold weather but isn't a full substitute.
- **Flex-fuel** vehicles are the exception that can take E85; ordinary vehicles should stick to regular
  pump gasoline.

# Quick reference

- **Siphon:** never by mouth — use a jiggler siphon or hand pump. Modern vehicles usually **can't** be
  siphoned through the filler neck (anti-siphon flap) — use older vehicles/equipment or the fuel-pump
  method (advanced).
- **Store:** approved color-coded sealed cans, dated, outside/cool, away from ignition, ≤ ~25 gal gas
  at home, stabilizer for anything kept > a few weeks.
- **Shelf life:** E10 gas ~3 months (≤ ~1–2 yrs stabilized); diesel ~6–12 months; propane indefinite.
  Bad gas = sour/varnish smell, dark color, or layered separation → don't run it.
- **Rotate** fuel through your vehicle/generator every few months and refill fresh.
- **Match fuel to engine**; never put gas in a diesel.
- **Safety:** work outdoors, container on the ground (static), Class B extinguisher handy, no flames or
  running engines nearby. Fuel swallowed/inhaled → Poison Control **1-800-222-1222**, don't induce
  vomiting.
