# Psychological First Aid & Resilience in a Crisis

## Read this first
1. **Panic, not the disaster itself, kills people who otherwise would have survived.** Clear thinking is a skill you can learn and practice, not a personality trait you either have or don't.
2. **When your mind goes blank or races, use STOP: Stop, Take a breath, Observe, Plan.** This one sequence, done out loud if needed, interrupts panic faster than anything else in this guide.
3. **To help someone else in shock, use Look, Listen, Link:** look for immediate danger and who needs help first; listen without pushing them to talk; link them to practical needs, people, and information.
4. **Do not force anyone — adult or child — to "talk about it."** Being present, calm, and available matters more than getting someone to open up. Talking helps only when the person is ready.
5. **Children take their emotional cues from the calmest adult in the room.** Your steady voice and face are a bigger intervention than any explanation.
6. **Grief has no fixed timeline and no wrong way to do it**, as long as the person is not a danger to themselves or others.
7. **Know the red flags for suicide risk** (see the Safety section) and take them seriously every time — a person expressing a specific plan and means to end their life is an emergency, not a mood.
8. **You are not a therapist, and you don't need to be one.** Psychological first aid is about safety, stabilization, and connection — not diagnosis or treatment.

---

## Why this matters
In a disaster or wilderness emergency, your body and brain react to danger with the same tools evolution gave every human: a flood of stress hormones that sharpen focus for a few seconds and then, if unmanaged, tip into panic, tunnel vision, or freezing. Rescue and military experience across decades of disasters, shipwrecks, and combat consistently shows the same pattern: **people with equal training, supplies, and physical condition survive at very different rates, and the difference is usually psychological.** Those who keep functioning share three habits — they interrupt panic quickly, they break overwhelming problems into one small next step, and they stay connected to other people instead of isolating.

The reverse is also true. Untreated panic causes people to run the wrong direction, forget basic skills they know cold, freeze during a critical window, or make impulsive decisions (jumping into cold water, leaving a group, refusing to eat or drink) that directly cause death. Despair — the belief that nothing you do will matter — is just as dangerous as panic because it stops people from doing the small, repeated actions (finding water, signaling, checking on each other) that add up to survival.

This guide is not about being fearless. Fear is useful information. It is about **staying functional while afraid** — for yourself, and for the people depending on you, including children, elders, and anyone freshly grieving a loss. A person who is psychologically stable is also far more able to give first aid, ration food fairly, keep watch, and make good decisions about resources — so this skill underpins every other chapter in this knowledge base.

## Key terms
- **Survival mindset** — the deliberate practice of staying calm, task-focused, and hopeful under extreme stress; a trainable skill, not an innate trait.
- **Acute stress reaction** — the normal, temporary fight-flight-freeze response to danger (racing heart, tunnel vision, shaking, blank mind). Not a disorder; it usually passes within minutes to hours once safety is restored.
- **Panic** — an acute stress reaction that has taken over behavior, causing actions that increase danger (running blindly, refusing help, hyperventilating to the point of fainting).
- **Grounding technique** — a simple sensory exercise (naming things you can see/hear/touch) that pulls attention out of racing thoughts and back into the present moment.
- **Psychological First Aid (PFA)** — a structured, non-clinical way of supporting someone in acute distress: ensure safety, offer calm presence, listen without pressuring, connect them to practical help. Not therapy, not counseling, not "processing the trauma."
- **Look, Listen, Link** — the three-step PFA model used in this guide: assess safety and immediate needs (Look), be present without forcing disclosure (Listen), connect the person to information, services, and social support (Link).
- **Grief** — the normal emotional response to loss (of a person, a home, a way of life); can include sadness, anger, guilt, numbness, or relief, often in a shifting mix.
- **Morale** — a group's shared sense that effort is worthwhile and things can improve; degrades under fatigue, unfairness, boredom, and uncertainty, and is actively rebuildable through routine, purpose, and small wins.
- **Compassion fatigue** — the exhaustion that helpers feel from repeatedly supporting others in distress; a real risk for anyone playing a caretaker role for days or weeks.
- **Suicidal ideation** — thoughts of ending one's own life, ranging from passive ("I wish I weren't here") to active with a specific plan and means; the latter is always an emergency.

## What you need
Psychological first aid requires almost no equipment — its "supplies" are mostly skills and attention:
- **Nothing physical is strictly required.** Your calm voice, presence, and attention are the primary tools.
- A **watch or any way to track time** — useful for pacing breathing exercises and for structuring routine (see Morale section).
- **Paper and pencil** (optional but valuable) — for journaling, writing down a plan during STOP, or for children to draw feelings they can't yet say in words.
- **A quiet, safe-feeling physical space**, even if small — a corner, a tent, a car — where a distressed person can sit without an audience.
- **Water and simple food** — dehydration and low blood sugar make emotional regulation much harder; offering a drink or snack is itself a PFA action.
- **A list of names/roles** in your group (if any) — knowing who has any relevant background (medical, counseling, clergy, teaching) helps you route people to the best available support.
- **This guide, memorized or in reach** — the STOP sequence and Look-Listen-Link model work best when they are automatic, so read them more than once before you need them.

## The survival mindset: staying calm and thinking clearly

### 1. Understand what is happening to your brain
Under acute stress, the amygdala (the brain's alarm system) takes over from the prefrontal cortex (the part that reasons and plans). This is why people report "going blank" or "not being able to think." It is a normal, temporary, and reversible state — not weakness, not a character flaw. Knowing this in advance makes it far less frightening when it happens to you.

### 2. Use the STOP technique the moment you notice panic rising
Signs you need STOP: racing heart, tunnel vision, shaking hands, thoughts jumping in circles, urge to run or freeze.

1. **S — Stop.** Physically stop moving. Sit or stand still. Say "stop" out loud if it helps interrupt the spiral.
2. **T — Take a breath.** Do at least one round of controlled breathing (see step-by-step below) before deciding anything.
3. **O — Observe.** Scan: Am I safe right now, in this exact second? What do I actually see, hear, and know to be true (not what I fear might be true)? Who else is here and how are they doing?
4. **P — Plan.** Choose exactly one next action — the smallest useful step, not the whole solution. Do that one thing, then repeat STOP if needed.

### 3. Controlled breathing, step by step
Use "box breathing" — simple to teach, works for adults and children age 6+:
1. Breathe in slowly through your nose for a count of **4**.
2. Hold the breath gently for a count of **4** (don't strain).
3. Breathe out slowly through your mouth for a count of **4**.
4. Hold empty for a count of **4**.
5. Repeat for **4 to 8 full cycles** (about one to two minutes). Trace a square shape with a finger in the air or on your leg if it helps you keep count.

If counting itself feels too hard in the moment, simplify to: **in for 4, out for 6** — a longer exhale than inhale calms the nervous system fastest.

### 4. Grounding technique: 5-4-3-2-1
Use this when thoughts are racing or a person feels detached/unreal ("dissociated"). Name out loud (or silently if alone):
1. **5** things you can see.
2. **4** things you can physically feel (ground under feet, fabric, temperature of air).
3. **3** things you can hear.
4. **2** things you can smell (or remember smelling).
5. **1** thing you can taste (or your saliva/mouth right now).

This forces attention into the present moment and out of the "what if" spiral, and it works even when a person is too overwhelmed to talk.

### 5. Positive, realistic self-talk
Replace catastrophic thoughts with true, workable statements. This is not "toxic positivity" — it is choosing the accurate thought that helps you function over the accurate-sounding thought that doesn't.
- Instead of "We're going to die out here" → **"I am alive right now, I have already survived the worst moment, and I know the next thing to do."**
- Instead of "I can't do this" → **"I don't have to do all of it. I only have to do the next small step."**
- Instead of "This is hopeless" → **"Hopeless is a feeling, not a fact. People have survived worse than this with less than I have."**

### 6. One task at a time
Overwhelm comes from the brain trying to solve every problem simultaneously (water, shelter, injuries, family members elsewhere, food, tomorrow, next week). Counter this deliberately:
1. Say out loud: "Right now I am only doing ___."
2. Pick the single most urgent, most doable task (usually: stop bleeding, get warm/dry, get to physical safety, get water).
3. Finish or stabilize it before consciously shifting to the next.
4. Write a short list if paper is available — crossing off even one item measurably improves mood and focus.

## Psychological First Aid for others: Look, Listen, Link

This model, drawn from disaster mental-health practice, is built for non-professionals helping people in acute distress. It is deliberately **not** about probing for trauma details or diagnosing anyone.

### LOOK
1. Scan for immediate physical danger to the person and yourself (fire, traffic, structural collapse, violence) — safety always comes before comfort.
2. Identify who is most distressed or most at risk (a child alone, someone visibly shaking or unresponsive, someone with an injury).
3. Note basic needs in view: are they injured, cold, wet, separated from family, disoriented?

### LISTEN
1. Approach calmly, at their eye level if possible, introduce yourself and your intent ("I'm here to help, can I sit with you?").
2. Let them decide whether to talk. Silence and presence are acceptable and often preferred.
3. If they do talk, **listen without correcting, minimizing, or rushing to fix.** Reflect back what you hear: "That sounds terrifying" rather than "It'll be fine."
4. Never say "I know how you feel," "at least you still have...", or "everything happens for a reason" — these minimize real pain even when well-intentioned.
5. Do not pressure anyone to recount details of what happened. Re-telling a traumatic event before someone is ready can re-traumatize rather than heal.

### LINK
1. Identify and meet practical needs first: water, warmth, a place to sit, information about loved ones.
2. Connect them to people (family, group members, someone with relevant skills) and to accurate information — uncertainty fuels fear more than bad news does.
3. If professional help (medical, mental health, clergy) is reachable, offer to help them access it — but don't insist if they decline, unless safety-relevant red flags are present (see Safety section).
4. Check back in later. A single contact is good; a follow-up is better.

```
   LOOK                LISTEN               LINK
 ┌─────────┐        ┌─────────┐        ┌──────────────┐
 │ Safety? │  --->  │ Presence │  --->  │ Needs, people,│
 │ Who needs│       │ not      │        │ information,  │
 │ help now?│        │ pressure │        │ follow-up     │
 └─────────┘        └─────────┘        └──────────────┘
```

## Helping children cope

Children regulate their emotions largely by watching the adults around them — your calm is contagious, and so is your fear.

1. **Stay physically close and at their eye level.** Touch (a hand held, an arm around shoulders) is calming if the child is comfortable with it.
2. **Give honest information at their level.** Do not lie ("everything is fine") if it isn't, but do not overload them with adult-level detail either. "There was a big storm and some houses got hurt. We are safe here and the grown-ups are taking care of things" is honest and bounded.
3. **Keep or rebuild routine as fast as possible** — regular mealtimes, a bedtime ritual, simple chores. Predictability is one of the strongest anti-anxiety tools for children.
4. **Limit exposure to frightening sights, sounds, and adult conversations**, including news/radio replays of the event. Repeated exposure to disaster imagery is linked to worse outcomes in children.
5. **Let them ask questions more than once.** Young children process through repetition — answering the same question calmly the fifth time is normal and helpful.
6. **Allow play and drawing.** Play-acting the event or drawing it is a normal, healthy way children process fear; it is not something to shut down unless it becomes constant, escalating distress rather than working-through.
7. **Watch for regression** (bedwetting, thumb-sucking, clinginess in a child who had outgrown these) — normal short-term, but reassure rather than punish it.
8. **Reassure them concretely about safety**: name the specific adult who is in charge of keeping them safe, and where their family members are or when they will be reunited.

## Helping the elderly cope

1. **Preserve independence and dignity wherever possible.** Ask before assisting; let them do what they can do themselves.
2. **Account for sensory and mobility limits.** Speak clearly, face them so they can see your expression/lips, and don't rush physical movement.
3. **Medication and routine disruption matter more here.** Losing access to regular medication, sleep schedule, or mobility aids can cause confusion that looks psychological but is partly physical — flag this for anyone with medical knowledge.
4. **Loss hits differently.** An older adult may be grieving a lifetime home, independence, or a spouse — avoid comparing their loss to others' or rushing them toward "moving on."
5. **Give them a role.** Elders often have deep practical skills (mending, cooking, calming children, historical knowledge of the area) — purposeful contribution supports morale at any age.
6. **Watch for withdrawal or sudden confusion**, which can indicate untreated pain, dehydration, medication issues, or depression, not just "old age."

## Grief and loss

1. **There is no fixed timeline and no single correct way to grieve.** Sadness, anger, numbness, guilt, and even relief can all appear, sometimes within the same hour.
2. **Don't rush anyone toward "moving forward"** or acceptance. Presence and patience matter more than progress.
3. **Rituals help, even improvised ones** — a moment of silence, sharing a memory, marking a grave or site if possible. Humans across every culture use ritual to process loss; a small, sincere one is better than none.
4. **Watch for grief that stops functioning entirely** (not eating/drinking, not sleeping at all, talking about wanting to die) — this crosses from normal grief into a safety concern (see Safety section below).
5. **Let children grieve too**, in their own way and language — they should not be shielded from the fact of a loss, only from unnecessary graphic detail.

## Maintaining morale in a group

Morale is not a fixed resource — it can be actively built and rebuilt, even in bad conditions.

1. **Establish routine fast.** Fixed times for waking, meals, work, and rest reduce anxiety by making the day predictable.
2. **Give everyone a purpose.** Assign real tasks matched to ability — idle hands and idle minds turn toward fear and conflict. Rotate undesirable jobs fairly.
3. **Celebrate small wins out loud.** Finished a shelter repair, found clean water, a child slept through the night — name it. Progress markers combat despair.
4. **Be visibly, consistently fair** with food, work, and rest — perceived unfairness is one of the fastest morale killers in any group under stress.
5. **Clear, calm leadership matters.** One person (or a small rotating team) should be recognized as making final calls on safety-critical decisions, reducing the paralysis of arguing in a crisis. Leadership should explain reasoning when possible and welcome input, not command by fear.
6. **Use appropriate humor.** Shared laughter, even brief and situational, lowers stress hormones and rebuilds a sense of normalcy — read the room, but don't suppress it entirely out of misplaced solemnity.
7. **Protect sleep and rest aggressively** (see below) — exhausted people cannot maintain morale or judgment no matter how good their attitude.
8. **Address conflict early and privately**, not by ignoring it. Unresolved friction under stress escalates fast; a brief, calm, private conversation ("I noticed tension between you two, let's sort it out") prevents it from infecting the whole group.
9. **Give people a way to contribute to decisions** that affect them, even small ones (when to eat, how to divide a chore) — a sense of control is one of the strongest protective factors against despair.

## Managing sleep and rest

1. **Protect a minimum sleep window for everyone** — adults need close to 7 hours, children more, even in poor conditions; chronic sleep loss degrades judgment faster than almost any other factor and mimics or worsens depression/anxiety symptoms.
2. **Rotate watch/duty schedules** so no one person is chronically sleep-deprived; stagger shifts rather than relying on the same person every night.
3. **Create a wind-down routine** even without normal comforts — dim light, quiet voices, a consistent "last task before sleep" signal.
4. **Nap strategically** (20–30 minutes) if a full night isn't possible; avoid longer naps that cause grogginess ("sleep inertia").
5. **Separate sleeping and worrying.** If someone can't sleep because their mind is racing, have them do a brief STOP + box-breathing cycle, or write worries down to "deal with tomorrow" rather than lying awake replaying them.

## Group dynamics and conflict

1. **Expect stress to shorten tempers** — treat early irritability as a normal signal, not a character flaw, and address it with rest, food, and water before assuming a personal conflict.
2. **Set a few simple, explicit shared rules** (how food is divided, quiet hours, decision-making process) early, so conflicts have a fair reference point instead of becoming personal.
3. **Mediate disputes privately and briefly** — let each side state their view without interruption, restate the shared goal (everyone's safety and survival), and agree on one concrete next step.
4. **Watch for scapegoating** — groups under stress sometimes unfairly blame one person; a good leader names this pattern directly and stops it.
5. **Rotate hard or unpleasant jobs** so resentment doesn't concentrate on one person.

## Regional & scenario variants
- **Mass-casualty or disaster-scale events (earthquake, flood, active shooter aftermath):** Triage psychological needs like physical ones — focus first on anyone in active danger, then the most acutely distressed, then general reassurance to the group. Expect responders and helpers to also need PFA after the acute phase.
- **Long-duration isolation (extended power outage, wilderness stranding, multi-week shelter-in-place):** Morale maintenance (routine, purpose, small wins) becomes more important relative to acute panic control, which fades as the immediate danger passes. Watch especially for slow-building despair and cabin fever.
- **Single survivor / solo scenario:** All "helping others" tools turn inward — talk to yourself out loud as you would coach someone else; keep a simple written log to externalize thoughts and track small wins over days.
- **Mixed-language or mixed-culture groups:** Calm tone, facial expression, and physical presence communicate safety even across language barriers; use simple words, short sentences, and repeat key safety information.
- **Combat/violence-adjacent trauma:** Be especially careful not to pressure disclosure of violent details; safety and stabilization needs (physical safety, water, warmth) come well before any conversation about what happened.
- **Religious or culturally-specific grief rituals:** Support existing rituals wherever safely possible rather than substituting your own — mourning practices are protective when they are familiar to the person grieving.

## ⚠️ Safety
**Recognize when someone (including you) needs urgent professional help, not just peer support.**

Warning signs of a mental health crisis or severe breakdown:
- Talking about wanting to die, disappearing, or being "better off gone" — even said as a joke or "just venting."
- Describing a **specific plan and means** to end their life (this is always an emergency — do not leave the person alone, remove access to means if safely possible, and get them to any available professional or emergency help immediately).
- Giving away possessions, saying goodbyes, or sudden calm after a period of severe distress (can indicate a decision has been made).
- Complete inability to function: not eating or drinking for over 24 hours, unable to sleep at all for multiple nights, unresponsive to comfort or their own name.
- Severe dissociation (seeming "not there," unresponsive to surroundings) that does not improve with grounding techniques after repeated attempts.
- Psychotic symptoms — hearing voices, seeing things that aren't there, or paranoid beliefs disconnected from the actual situation.
- Extreme, sustained aggression or loss of control that threatens the safety of others.

**What to do:**
1. Do not leave the person alone if they express suicidal intent with a plan.
2. Remove immediate means of harm if you can do so safely (weapons, medications, sharp tools).
3. Stay calm, stay present, and say plainly: "I'm concerned about you and I want you to be safe. I'm not leaving you alone right now."
4. Get them to professional help as soon as physically possible — a doctor, mental health professional, crisis line, clergy member, or emergency responder. In a true no-communication scenario, prioritize physically moving toward help over waiting it out.
5. If a crisis line or connectivity becomes available at any point, use it — in the US, 988 is the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline.
6. This guide is not a substitute for professional mental health treatment. Its purpose is to keep people safe and stable until real help is reached — never to replace it once reachable.
7. **Helpers need support too.** If you are the one providing PFA to others for days or weeks, watch yourself for the same warning signs and hand off caretaking duties periodically — compassion fatigue is real and reduces your ability to help anyone, including yourself.

## Troubleshooting
- **"STOP isn't working, my mind still won't stop racing."** Repeat the breathing step alone for several more cycles before trying to observe/plan again — you may need to lower your body's stress chemistry further before your thinking brain comes back online. Physical movement (a short walk, shaking out your hands) can help discharge stress hormones first.
- **"A child won't stop asking the same scary question."** This is normal processing, not defiance — answer briefly and calmly each time rather than showing frustration; the repetition itself is often what settles them.
- **"Someone refuses to talk to me at all."** Respect it. Silent presence is still psychological first aid. Offer practical help (water, a blanket, sitting nearby) instead of pushing conversation, and check back later.
- **"The group is turning on one person."** Name the pattern directly ("we're all under a lot of stress, let's not take it out on one person") and redirect energy toward a shared task.
- **"I said the wrong thing and made it worse."** Acknowledge it simply ("I'm sorry, that came out wrong, what I meant was I'm here for you") and return to listening rather than trying to fix the mistake with more words.
- **"I'm the one falling apart and there's no one to help me."** Use STOP and box breathing on yourself exactly as taught here; write your thoughts down if possible; if any way to reach outside help exists, use it — leaders and helpers are allowed to need help too.
- **"A person seems 'checked out' and grounding isn't bringing them back."** Keep them physically safe, keep trying gentle grounding, and treat this as a signal to get professional help as soon as it's reachable — prolonged unresponsiveness is beyond peer-level PFA.

## Quick-reference checklist
- [ ] Notice rising panic early: racing heart, tunnel vision, blank mind.
- [ ] Run **STOP**: Stop – Take a breath – Observe – Plan.
- [ ] Box breathing: in-4, hold-4, out-4, hold-4, repeat 4–8 times.
- [ ] Grounding when overwhelmed: 5 seen, 4 felt, 3 heard, 2 smelled, 1 tasted.
- [ ] One task at a time — say it out loud, do it, then choose the next.
- [ ] Helping others: **Look** (safety/needs) – **Listen** (presence, no pressure) – **Link** (practical help, people, follow-up).
- [ ] With children: stay calm, be honest at their level, keep routine, limit scary exposure.
- [ ] With elders: preserve dignity, give a role, watch for confusion vs. depression.
- [ ] With grief: no timeline, no comparisons, allow ritual, don't rush "moving on."
- [ ] Group morale: routine, purpose, fairness, visible small wins, humor, calm leadership.
- [ ] Protect sleep for everyone; rotate duty shifts.
- [ ] Know the red flags: specific suicide plan/means, giving away belongings, total inability to function, non-responsive dissociation — treat as emergencies, get professional help fastest possible way.
- [ ] Check on yourself too — helpers can burn out and need the same tools taught here.
