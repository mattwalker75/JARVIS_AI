# Documents, Money & Emergency Administration

## Read this first (the 30-second version)
1. **Make a document kit today, before anything happens.** Photocopy or scan every ID, insurance policy, deed/title, and medical record; put copies in a **waterproof, portable folder** you can grab in 60 seconds, plus a **second copy** stored digitally (encrypted USB and/or cloud) and a **third copy** with someone out of state.
2. **Keep emergency cash on hand** — small bills, in a fireproof/waterproof container, because ATMs and card readers fail when the power or network is down.
3. **Write a medical card** for every household member: conditions, allergies, medications (with dosages), doctor names, blood type, insurance ID — carry it in a wallet, and keep a full copy in the kit.
4. **Photograph your home and belongings NOW**, room by room, with a phone — this is your proof for an insurance claim later, and it is nearly impossible to reconstruct from memory after a disaster.
5. **After a disaster:** photograph damage before you touch or discard anything, call your insurer within 24–48 hours, keep every receipt, and if it's a federally declared disaster, register with FEMA at disasterassistance.gov or 1-800-621-3362.
6. **Keep a paper contact list** — phone numbers do not survive a dead phone battery, and you may not have your phone at all.

This guide gives you a full checklist to build the kit, explains what to record for each document type, and walks through documenting damage and filing claims step by step. **A person who has never done any of this can follow it.**

---

## Why this matters
When a disaster hits — a house fire, a tornado, a flood, a hurricane evacuation — you may have **minutes, not hours**, to leave, and you may never get back into the building. Without your papers you cannot prove who you are, prove you own your home, get your prescriptions refilled, collect an insurance payout, or receive disaster aid — right when you need all of that most. People who lose their identification and records on top of losing their home often spend **months** untangling bureaucracy during the worst period of their lives. A $10 waterproof folder, a few hours of photographing your house, and a spare $100–$300 in cash prevents almost all of that. This guide is not legal or insurance advice — it is a practical checklist to reduce paperwork chaos when you're already overwhelmed.

## Key terms (plain language)
- **Document kit / go-kit** — a portable folder or bag holding copies of your most important papers, ready to grab during an evacuation.
- **Vital records** — birth/marriage/death certificates, Social Security cards, passports, and similar government-issued core documents.
- **Deed** — the legal document proving who owns a piece of real property (land/house).
- **Title** — the legal document of ownership for a vehicle (or, informally, other property).
- **Policy declarations page ("dec page")** — the 1–2 page summary at the front of an insurance policy showing coverage limits, deductible, and policy number; the single most useful page to have copied.
- **Deductible** — the amount you pay out of pocket before insurance starts paying on a claim.
- **Claims adjuster** — the person sent by your insurance company (or hired independently) to inspect damage and estimate what the insurer will pay.
- **Proof of loss** — a formal, signed statement of what you lost and its value, which your insurer may require to pay a claim.
- **FEMA Individual Assistance** — federal disaster aid for individuals/households, available only after a federal disaster declaration for your county, and generally only for needs insurance doesn't cover.
- **Encryption** — scrambling digital data so it can't be read without a password/key; on modern phones and computers this is often already built in.
- **Two-factor authentication (2FA)** — a login that requires your password **plus** a second proof (a code texted to your phone, or an authenticator app).
- **Home inventory** — a room-by-room record (photos/video + a list) of your belongings and their approximate value, used to support an insurance claim.
- **EFFAK (Emergency Financial First Aid Kit)** — a free FEMA/Operation HOPE toolkit of fill-in-the-blank checklists (household ID, financial/legal documents, financial accounts, medical info, household contacts) built for exactly this purpose; this guide's Part 7 checklist covers the same ground, and the EFFAK PDF (see Sources, at the end) gives you literal printable forms if you prefer filling in someone else's template to building your own.
- **Disaster Recovery Center (DRC)** — a temporary, in-person FEMA/state site set up after a major disaster where you can apply for assistance, ask questions, and get help uploading documents if you have no internet access of your own.

**The four-step mental model (from Ready.gov's Financial Preparedness guidance) that ties this whole guide together:**
1. **Compile** — gather the documents and account info (Part 1).
2. **Review** — check that your insurance coverage, beneficiaries, and account details are actually current and adequate (Part 1B, Part 6).
3. **Safeguard** — copy, back up, and store it where a disaster can't take out every copy at once (Part 5).
4. **Update** — refresh it on a schedule and after every major life change (Part 5A, step 6).

# PART 1 — WHAT TO RECORD AND COPY

## 1A. Identification documents
**Copy/scan these for every household member:**
- Driver's license or state ID (front and back)
- Passport (photo page) and passport card
- Birth certificate
- Social Security card (see caution below)
- Marriage certificate / divorce decree (if relevant to benefits or custody)
- Military ID / DD-214 (discharge papers)
- Immigration/visa documents (green card, visa, work permit)
- Child custody or guardianship papers, adoption papers
- Recent passport-style photo of each family member (helps identify children if separated, and speeds up replacing a lost ID)

**How to copy them:**
1. Use a phone scanning app (most phone cameras have a built-in "scan document" mode in the Files/Notes app) or a flatbed scanner. Photograph in good light, flat, with all four corners visible.
2. Save as PDF where possible (one file per document, clearly named: `lastname_firstname_passport.pdf`).
3. Print **one paper copy** for the physical kit, and save the digital file per Part 3.

> ⚠️ **Social Security cards:** carrying the physical card around raises identity-theft risk if the kit is lost or stolen. Better options: photograph the number and store it **only** in the encrypted digital backup (not printed loose in the folder), or simply memorize/write the number on an index card kept with, but not loose inside, other paperwork. Never post a photo of it anywhere online or send it by unencrypted email.

## 1B. Insurance policies — what to record for each
For **every** policy (home/renters, auto, health, life, flood, umbrella), copy the **declarations page** at minimum, and ideally the full policy. Record:

| Field | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Insurer name and policy number | Needed to file any claim |
| 24-hour claims phone number and claims-office address | You may need to call before you can even get online |
| Agent name and phone number | A known human contact speeds everything up |
| Coverage limits (dwelling, contents, liability, additional living expenses) | Tells you what you can actually recover |
| Deductible amount, and whether it's a flat dollar amount or a percentage (common for wind/hail/hurricane in Texas) | A 1–2% wind/hail deductible on a $300,000 home is $3,000–$6,000 — know this **before** disaster, not after |
| Named perils vs. "all-risk" — and specific **exclusions** (flood is almost always excluded from standard home policies) | Flood damage generally requires a **separate flood policy** (NFIP or private) — most home policies do not cover it |
| Health insurance ID and group number, plus a member-services phone number | Needed to get care and refills away from home |
| Life insurance company, policy number, and beneficiary named | Needed by survivors after a death |

**Do this too:**
- Save your agent's and insurer's contact info as a **paper** entry, not just a phone contact — if your phone is dead, lost, or destroyed you still need the number.
- If you don't have flood insurance and live in or near a floodplain, this is worth addressing before the next storm season — see the [Water & Flooding/Plumbing guide](../water-flooding-plumbing/water-flooding-plumbing.md).

## 1C. Property records: deeds, titles, and loans
- **Home deed** or the closing/settlement documents from when you bought the home (proves ownership).
- **Mortgage statement** (lender name, loan number, contact info) — you may need to keep making payments or contact the lender for disaster forbearance.
- **Vehicle titles and registration**, plus auto loan/lien information.
- **Recent property tax statement** (shows the county's appraised value — useful supporting evidence, and you'll want the account number regardless).
- **HOA documents**, if applicable.
- **Home improvement/renovation records and major-appliance receipts** — these support a higher claimed value after a loss and prove the age/condition of big-ticket items (roof, HVAC, water heater).

## 1D. Medical information and the wallet card
Keep this **current** — update it whenever a prescription or diagnosis changes.

**For each family member, record:**
- Full legal name, date of birth, blood type (if known)
- Chronic conditions (diabetes, heart disease, seizure disorder, asthma, etc.)
- **Allergies** — medications, foods, insect stings — clearly marked, since this is the single most safety-critical line
- **Current medications**: drug name, dose, how often, and **why** it's prescribed (a responder or pharmacist filling in for your regular doctor needs this to substitute safely)
- Prescribing doctor(s) name and phone number, and pharmacy name/phone/location
- Health insurance ID/group number
- Emergency contact name and phone number (someone **not** in the immediate household, in case the whole household is affected together)
- Medical devices in use (CPAP, insulin pump, pacemaker, hearing aids, glasses/contacts prescription) and their model/settings

**Build a wallet card:**
1. Use an index card, or fold a sheet to wallet size; write (or print) the fields above for that one person.
2. Laminate it (packing tape works if you have no laminator) so it survives a wet pocket or bag.
3. Every household member — including kids and anyone with a caregiver — carries their own card at all times, not just during evacuations.
4. Keep a **master copy** of everyone's cards together in the document kit.

**Prescription list — keep it simple and keep it updated:**
- One combined sheet, one row per person per medication: name / drug / dose / frequency / prescribing doctor / pharmacy.
- Photograph the label of each pill bottle as a backup (shows exact name, strength, and RX number, which speeds up an emergency refill at any pharmacy).
- Most pharmacies can look up and transfer a prescription using just your name and date of birth, but having the drug name and dose in hand avoids dangerous guessing if the pharmacy's own records are unreachable (e.g., their system is down too).

> ⚠️ This guide is not medical advice. Never guess at a dose or substitute a medication without a pharmacist or doctor's input; if you run out of a critical medication (insulin, heart, seizure, or blood-thinner medication) and cannot reach your regular doctor or pharmacy, go to any pharmacy or urgent care and show your prescription list — most states allow an emergency refill during a declared disaster. See the [First Aid guide](../first-aid/first-aid.md) for medical emergencies themselves.

## 1E. Other things worth including
- Wills, powers of attorney, and advance directives/living wills, if you have them.
- Pet vaccination records and photos (many shelters require proof of rabies vaccination; a photo helps prove ownership if you're separated from a pet).
- School records/report cards and immunization records for children (schools often require these to enroll quickly elsewhere).
- A recent household inventory (Part 4) and any appraisals for high-value items (jewelry, art, collectibles).
- Employer contact info and, if self-employed, your business license/registration and key client contacts.

## 1F. If it's already lost or destroyed: how to replace each document
If a disaster already happened and you're reading this **after** the fact with nothing in hand, here is where each document actually gets replaced. Your digital backup (Part 5B) makes every one of these faster — most agencies accept a scanned/printed copy of an expired or damaged original as supporting proof even when they need to issue a new one.

| Document | Where to replace it | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Social Security card | ssa.gov (online replacement is available in most states through a **my Social Security** account) or your local Social Security office in person | You often don't need a physical card day-to-day — the number itself is usually enough; replacing the card is mostly for your records. |
| Driver's license / state ID | Your state's DMV (or equivalent) — many states offer online renewal/replacement or an expedited process specifically for declared disasters | Bring whatever ID or proof of address you do have; ask specifically about disaster-replacement policies. |
| Birth certificate | The **vital records office of the state or territory where you were born** (not a national office) | The CDC publishes a state-by-state directory of where to write for birth/death certificates if you don't know your state's office. |
| U.S. passport | Report it lost/stolen to the State Department, then apply in person for a new one (Form DS-11, or DS-64 to report the loss) | travel.state.gov has the current forms and fees; expedited service exists for verified emergencies. |
| Green card (permanent resident card) | USCIS Form I-90, filed online or by mail | uscis.gov walks through the replacement process and current filing fee. |
| Medicare card | Log into your account at medicare.gov to view/print a copy or request a mailed replacement, or call **1-800-MEDICARE** | No need to wait for a new physical card to use your coverage — your Medicare number still works. |
| Prior-year tax return copies | Free transcript via IRS.gov "Get Transcript," or a full copy via **Form 4506** (small fee) | A transcript is usually enough for loan/aid applications; a full copy takes longer. |
| Vehicle title / registration | Your state DMV | See Part 1C. |
| Home deed / recorded plat | The county clerk's office where the property is recorded (Collin County: the **Collin County Clerk** — Part 5C) | Recorded deeds are public record; a lost personal copy doesn't affect your actual ownership. |
| Damaged/mutilated paper currency | The Treasury's **Bureau of Engraving and Printing (BEP) Mutilated Currency Redemption** program — mail it with a completed **BEP Form 5283** | Free service; the BEP processes roughly 30,000 claims a year worth over $30 million combined. Note more than half of the original note (with any security features) generally must be identifiable — check bep.gov for current requirements before mailing anything. |
| Lost paper savings bonds | TreasuryDirect.gov | The Treasury no longer issues new paper bonds, but lost older ones can still be traced/replaced there. |
| Concern about identity theft after losing documents | IdentityTheft.gov (Federal Trade Commission) | Gives you a personalized recovery plan and pre-filled letters/affidavits if someone misuses your information. |

> Start this whole table from your **digital backup** (Part 5B), not from memory — having the old document number, issuing office, and expiration date on hand turns a multi-week replacement into a same-day or same-week one at most agencies.

# PART 2 — EMERGENCY CASH

## Why cash, and how much
When the power grid, cell network, or internet is down, **card readers and ATMs stop working** — this happens routinely in hurricanes, ice storms, and prolonged outages. Cash is the only payment method guaranteed to work everywhere.

- **Target amount:** commonly recommended range is **$100–$300 in a grab-and-go kit** per household, plus a separate larger reserve (many guides suggest being able to cover **a few weeks of essential expenses**, if you can manage it) kept at home for a longer outage. Scale to what you can realistically set aside — some cash beats none.
- **Use small denominations**: $1s, $5s, $10s, and a few $20s. In a real outage, stores may have no way to make change, and a single $100 bill may be useless or refused. Include a small amount of coins/rolled change for vending machines, tolls, or laundry.
- **Split it up:** don't keep all the cash in one place. Keep some in the document kit, some in a home safe, and a small amount distributed among household members (a bugout bag pocket, a wallet, a car).

## Where to keep it
- A small **fireproof/waterproof document box or bag** at home (see Part 5) is the primary spot.
- A portion in your **grab-and-go bag** — see the [Evacuation & Bug-Out Vehicle guide](../evacuation-bugout-vehicle/evacuation-bugout-vehicle.md) for how that bag is organized overall.
- Never keep 100% of your emergency cash in a bank-only account you can't reach without power/internet, and never keep the household's entire cash reserve in one single easily-lost location (one wallet, one glovebox).

> ⚠️ Cash in the open is a theft risk like anything else of value — keep amounts reasonable, don't advertise that you keep cash at home, and combine with basic home security (see the [Security & Defense guide](../security-defense/security-defense.md)).

# PART 3 — THE OFFLINE PAPER CONTACT LIST

Phones die, get lost, get destroyed, or lose signal — and most people no longer memorize phone numbers. A paper list is a low-tech backbone that never needs a battery.

**Build one page (front and back is fine) with:**
1. **Immediate family** — every household member's cell number, plus one out-of-area relative or friend as a check-in point (long-distance calls/texts often get through even when local lines are jammed).
2. **Work/school** numbers for each family member.
3. **Doctors, pharmacy, veterinarian.**
4. **Insurance agent(s) and 24-hour claims lines** (Part 1B).
5. **Utility companies** (electric, gas, water) — for reporting outages or gas leaks. See the [Electrical, Gas & Utility Safety guide](../electrical-gas-utility-safety/electrical-gas-utility-safety.md).
6. **Local emergency management office** and non-emergency police/fire line (911 is for emergencies only — keep the non-emergency number too).
7. **A designated out-of-town meeting contact** — in many family emergency plans, everyone calls or texts one agreed-upon person outside the area to report their status, since it's often easier to complete one long-distance call than to reach each other directly.

**Distribute it:** one copy in the document kit, one in each vehicle, one in each adult's wallet/bag, a laminated copy on the fridge, and one in each child's backpack with instructions to show it to a trusted adult if separated.

# PART 4 — PHOTOGRAPHING AND DOCUMENTING PROPERTY

Good documentation **before** a disaster is what turns "I think I had about $30,000 of stuff" into a claim insurers actually pay quickly.

## Before a disaster: build a home inventory
1. **Walk every room on video**, narrating out loud what you see and opening cabinets/closets/drawers. Don't rush — a slow, complete walkthrough is more useful than a fast one.
2. **Photograph high-value items individually**: electronics (with serial numbers visible), jewelry, tools, furniture, appliances, art, collectibles. For anything expensive, photograph the receipt or box/manual too.
3. **List it out** alongside the video/photos: item, approximate purchase date, approximate value, and if you have it, the receipt. A spreadsheet or even a notes app list is fine — the photos/video are your primary proof; the list makes filing faster.
4. **Photograph the exterior**: all four sides of the house, the roof if visible/safe to photograph from the ground, fences, sheds, landscaping, and any vehicles.
5. **Update it** roughly once a year, or right after any major purchase (new appliance, new furniture, home renovation).
6. **Store this like the rest of your documents** — a copy on your phone does no good if the phone is destroyed with the house. Back it up per Part 5 (cloud and/or an off-site copy).

## After a disaster: documenting damage for a claim
1. **Photograph and video everything before you clean up, move debris, or make repairs** — once you throw out a ruined couch or start ripping out wet drywall, you lose your proof. Take wide shots of each room **and** close-ups of specific damage.
2. **Make only emergency repairs** needed to prevent further damage (tarping a roof hole, shutting off a leaking pipe) — photograph the damage first, and **keep all receipts** for materials and any temporary repair labor; most policies reimburse reasonable "mitigation" costs.
3. **Do not discard damaged items** until your adjuster has seen them or told you it's fine to dispose of them — if something must be removed immediately for safety (mold, contamination, blocking an exit), photograph it thoroughly from multiple angles first.
4. **Keep a running written log**: date, what happened, what you did, who you talked to (name and reference/claim number), and how long each call took. This becomes invaluable if a claim is disputed later.
5. For structural safety of the building itself before you re-enter or let anyone else evaluate damage, see the [Home Structural Assessment guide](../home-structural-assessment/home-structural-assessment.md) — that guide covers whether the building is safe to be in; this guide covers the paperwork trail.

## Salvaging wet or fire-damaged original papers and photos (if you didn't have copies)
If originals got wet or singed and you have no backup copy, the **National Archives'** emergency-salvage guidance for flood-damaged family papers applies directly to your own documents and photos:

1. **Speed matters most in the first 48 hours.** Wet paper and photos develop mold and the ink/emulsion starts to degrade fast.
2. **Do not try to separate stuck-together wet pages or peel a photo off glass/another photo.** Handle wet photographic prints and negatives only by the edges — the surface is easily damaged by touch while wet.
3. **Do not use high heat, a hair dryer, or direct sunlight to dry anything fast.** Fast heat-drying causes pages to curl, crack, and warp permanently — the opposite of what you want.
4. **If it's just a few documents:** interleave with paper towels or unprinted newsprint, lay flat or hang, and air-dry in a cool, low-humidity space out of direct sun, changing the absorbent paper as it gets wet.
5. **If you have too much to air-dry within 48 hours:** put the wet papers/photos in a plastic bag or box and **freeze them** — freezing stops mold growth and buys you time to deal with them (or take them to a professional) later, in batches, once dried.
6. **Photographs are the most fragile and time-sensitive item.** Keep them wet and get them to a professional photo-restoration/photo-finishing lab as soon as you can — recovery odds drop the longer they sit, and some photo types have only a low chance of recovery even with prompt treatment.
7. This is for salvaging **originals you still have** — it does not replace the digital backup habit in Part 5B, which avoids needing to do any of this in the first place.

# PART 5 — BUILDING THE PHYSICAL KIT AND THE DIGITAL BACKUP

## 5A. The waterproof/grab-and-go document package
**You need:** a small waterproof, fireproof document bag or box (a dedicated fire/water-rated document bag is ideal; a heavy-duty zip-top freezer bag inside a small tote or backpack pocket is a workable improvised substitute), plus paper copies of everything in Part 1.

**Steps to assemble:**
1. Gather and copy every document from Part 1 (originals stay in a home safe or safe-deposit box where practical — the kit holds **copies**, except items you truly must carry, like current ID).
2. Organize with labeled folders or large envelopes by category: ID, insurance, property, medical, financial, contacts.
3. Place everything inside a zip-top bag (air out excess air) and then inside the fireproof/waterproof case or bag.
4. Store the kit somewhere you can reach it in the dark in under a minute — a front hall closet shelf, not a back attic corner.
5. Tell every adult in the household where it is and do a practice grab-and-go once.
6. Re-check the contents every 6–12 months (align it with a recurring date like a smoke-detector battery check) — update expired IDs, add new insurance dec pages, refresh the medical/prescription list.
7. This kit pairs with your evacuation bag — see the [Evacuation & Bug-Out Vehicle guide](../evacuation-bugout-vehicle/evacuation-bugout-vehicle.md) for how it fits into the overall grab-and-go load, and the [Supplies, Inventory & Rotation guide](../supplies-inventory-rotation/supplies-inventory-rotation.md) for how to fold this into a broader supply-check routine.

## 5B. The encrypted digital backup (kept simple for beginners)
The paper kit can still burn, flood, or be left behind. A digital copy that you can reach from **any** computer or phone, anywhere, is the safety net.

**The simple version — do these two things, and you've covered most of the risk:**

1. **A USB drive**, kept off-site (with a relative, in a work desk, in a safe-deposit box — anywhere other than your home):
   - Copy all scanned documents onto it.
   - Use built-in encryption so the drive is useless if lost or stolen: Windows has **BitLocker To Go**; macOS has **encrypted disk images** (Disk Utility → New Image → choose encryption) or an encrypted external volume via Disk Utility. Follow the OS's own prompts — you only need to set a strong password once.
   - Label it plainly on the outside as "documents" (don't write account numbers or passwords on the label).

2. **A cloud backup**, using a mainstream provider you already trust (examples: iCloud, Google Drive, Dropbox, Microsoft OneDrive):
   - Create one folder (e.g., "Emergency Documents") and upload the same scanned files.
   - Turn on **two-factor authentication (2FA)** on that account — this is the single most effective step to keep the account from being broken into, and every major provider offers it for free in account security settings.
   - For extra protection on especially sensitive files (Social Security numbers, full account numbers), zip them into a single password-protected archive before uploading, or use the cloud provider's built-in encrypted/"vault" folder feature if it has one.

**Password strategy — keep it simple:**
- Use a **long passphrase** (4+ random unrelated words, e.g., "correct-horse-battery-staple" style) rather than a short complex password — longer beats cleverer.
- Use a **different** password for your email and your cloud-storage account than for everything else; those two accounts are the keys to resetting all your other accounts.
- Consider a password manager (built into most phones/browsers already, e.g., Apple Keychain, Google Password Manager) to avoid reusing passwords — but **write down** the one master password/passphrase to that manager somewhere in your physical document kit, since forgetting it locks you out of everything at once.
- Never store the actual login passwords in the same plain file as the encrypted documents; keep the "how to get in" information separate from the "sensitive documents" content.

**A third copy for redundancy:** give a USB copy or share cloud-folder access with one trusted relative or friend **who lives in a different area** (not affected by the same regional disaster). This protects you if your home, your safe-deposit box, and your own cloud account are all somehow inaccessible at once.

## 5C. North Texas / regional notes
- **Tornadoes and severe thunderstorms** (spring/early summer) mean total-loss house fires and roof/wall failures can happen with only minutes of warning — the "grab it in 60 seconds" design of the kit matters most here; keep it in or very near your interior safe room/closet, not a detached garage or shed.
- **Flash flooding** is common on blackland prairie clay soils that shed rain fast; if your area has flooded before or sits near a creek/floodplain, check specifically whether you carry **separate flood insurance** (standard homeowner's policies sold in Texas do not cover flood) — record that policy separately if you have one, and see the [Water & Flooding/Plumbing guide](../water-flooding-plumbing/water-flooding-plumbing.md).
- **Ice storms** can knock out power for days across Collin County; that's exactly when ATMs/card readers go down and cash matters, and when a paper contact list (dead phone battery, no charging) earns its keep.
- **Grassfires/wildfire risk** in dry periods is another total-loss, minutes-of-warning scenario — same kit logic as tornadoes.
- Collin County property records (deeds) are recorded with the **Collin County Clerk**; if your originals are ever destroyed, recorded deeds and plats can typically be re-obtained from the county clerk's office — keeping your own copy just saves time.

# PART 6 — FILING A DISASTER INSURANCE CLAIM AND FEMA ASSISTANCE

## Filing an insurance claim, step by step
1. **Ensure safety first** — don't file a claim from inside an unsafe structure. Confirm the building is safe to be in or near (see the [Home Structural Assessment guide](../home-structural-assessment/home-structural-assessment.md)) before you go back in to survey damage closely.
2. **Document everything before cleanup** (Part 4) — photos/video of all damage, from multiple angles, in good light.
3. **Call your insurer's 24-hour claims line as soon as possible**, ideally within 24–48 hours. Have your policy number ready (from your document kit). Ask for and **write down**: your claim number, the adjuster's name and phone number, and the expected timeline for inspection.
4. **Make only necessary emergency repairs** to prevent further damage (tarps, boarding windows, shutting off water) and **keep every receipt** — reasonable mitigation costs are usually reimbursable, but get it in writing/confirmed with the adjuster if the amount is significant.
5. **Meet the adjuster in person if you can.** Walk them through the damage, point out anything that might not be obvious (pre-existing high-value items, hidden damage), and provide your home inventory and photos. Take your own notes and photos during their visit too — an independent record protects you if there's a dispute later.
6. **What adjusters typically want from you:**
   - Your policy number and full contact information
   - A written description of what happened and when
   - Photos/video of the damage (before and after any emergency repairs)
   - A list of damaged/destroyed items with approximate value and age (your home inventory)
   - Receipts for anything you've already repaired, replaced, or discarded
   - Access to inspect the property (and any damaged items you kept)
7. **Get the settlement offer in writing.** Read it against your policy's declarations page and coverage limits. If you disagree with the amount, you can typically ask the insurer to re-inspect, request a supplemental claim for damage found later (e.g., mold discovered after drywall is opened), or as a last resort, consult a licensed public adjuster or attorney — this guide cannot tell you whether to do so; that's a decision for a professional given your specific policy and state law.
8. **Keep a claim log**: every call, date, who you spoke with, and what was said or promised. Insurance claims for major disasters can take weeks to months; a written log prevents "he said/she said" problems.

> This is general practical guidance, not legal or insurance advice. Insurance law and specific policy language vary — when real money or a disputed claim is on the line, a licensed public adjuster, your state insurance department, or an attorney is the right next step, not this guide.

## FEMA disaster assistance — the basics
FEMA Individual Assistance is **federal aid for individuals/households**, and only becomes available after the President issues a **federal disaster declaration** covering your specific county (this is announced on the news and at fema.gov/disaster/declarations — not every storm qualifies).

1. **Check first** whether your area has an active federal declaration, at fema.gov/disaster/declarations.
2. **File your insurance claim first, or at the same time** — FEMA assistance generally cannot duplicate what insurance covers; it's meant to help with needs insurance doesn't (or for the uninsured).
3. **Apply one of four ways:** online at **disasterassistance.gov** (click "Apply Now" and create an account so you can save progress, check status, and get messages), through the **FEMA mobile app**, by phone at **1-800-621-3362**, or **in person at a Disaster Recovery Center (DRC)** if one is set up near you — useful if you have no working internet. Registration is always free — never pay anyone to "help" you register; be alert to post-disaster scams.
4. **Have ready before you start:**
   - Social Security number (at least one household member needs one and must be a U.S. citizen, non-citizen national, or qualified alien; if no one in the household has one, check the Social Security Administration's guidance first)
   - Current mailing address, the damaged property's address, phone number, and a valid email address
   - Insurance details (company, policy type — homeowners/flood/auto/mobile home — and whether you've filed a claim)
   - A description of the damage: disaster type (flood, hurricane, tornado, fire, etc.) and the type of dwelling/vehicle affected
   - **Total annual household income before taxes**, as of the date of the disaster (used to determine some types of assistance)
   - Banking information (bank name, account type, routing and account number) **only if** you want any award sent by direct deposit — this is optional
5. **Mind the deadline.** You generally have **60 days from the date of the federal disaster declaration** to apply — FEMA sometimes extends this for a specific disaster, and there is often a further late-registration window if you miss it for a documented good reason, but don't count on that; apply as soon as you can. Check disasterassistance.gov for the exact deadline for your specific disaster.
6. **Submit documents through the online "Upload Center"** once you have an account, instead of mailing paper where possible — it's faster and gives you a record that FEMA received it.
7. **FEMA may schedule a home inspection** to verify damage — this is separate from your insurance adjuster and does not replace filing with your insurer.
8. **Assistance can include** temporary housing help, basic home-repair grants, and grants for other disaster-related necessary expenses (this changes by disaster and program rules — the registration process will tell you what applies).
9. **After you apply**, check your status anytime at disasterassistance.gov (the "Check Status" button, if you made an account) — see fema.gov/assistance/individual/after-applying for what typically happens next.
10. **Keep copies of everything you submit to FEMA**, and note your FEMA registration/application number the same way you tracked your insurance claim number.

# ⚠️ Safety — the most important warnings
- **This guide is not legal, financial, or insurance advice.** For disputed claims, complex estates, custody, or immigration matters, consult a licensed professional (attorney, public adjuster, financial advisor).
- **Never carry a loose Social Security card or full SSN printout** in a bag that could be lost or stolen — keep the number in the encrypted digital backup only, per Part 1A.
- **Never guess at a medication dose** or substitute a drug based on this guide or your own memory — a pharmacist or doctor must make that call. See the [First Aid guide](../first-aid/first-aid.md) for medical emergencies.
- **Photograph damage before you clean up or discard items** — once it's gone, so is your proof for the claim.
- **Beware post-disaster scams**: legitimate FEMA registration is always free; anyone charging a fee to "help you register" or claiming to be a FEMA inspector who demands payment or your Social Security number on the spot is a scam — ask for ID and verify through official channels.
- **Do not re-enter a structurally unsafe building** just to retrieve documents — see the [Home Structural Assessment guide](../home-structural-assessment/home-structural-assessment.md) for how to judge that; documents are replaceable, you are not.
- **Standard homeowner's insurance in Texas typically excludes flood damage** — confirm your actual coverage before a flood, not after.
- **Keep passwords and passphrases separate from the sensitive documents themselves** — if someone gets the encrypted file without the password it's useless, but writing the password on the same drive/folder defeats the encryption entirely.

# Troubleshooting
| Problem | Fix |
|---|---|
| Lost all IDs in the disaster | Use your digital backup (USB/cloud copy) to print replacements from any library/copy shop; visit your state DMV/vital records office in person with whatever ID or proof of address you do have — most states have an expedited disaster-replacement process. |
| Insurance company disputes the damage amount | Provide your before/after photos, video walkthrough, and home inventory list; request a re-inspection in writing; keep your claim log; consider a licensed public adjuster if still unresolved. |
| No power/internet to reach cloud backup | Use the offline paper kit and/or the encrypted USB copy on any working computer; this is exactly why you keep both a paper and a digital copy. |
| Ran out of a critical prescription and can't reach your regular doctor | Go to any pharmacy or urgent care with your prescription list and pill-bottle photos; many states allow emergency refills of maintenance medications during a declared disaster — ask the pharmacist directly. |
| Forgot the password/passphrase to the encrypted USB or cloud vault | This is why the master passphrase is written down and kept in the physical kit — check there first; going forward, use a password manager with one memorized master password. |
| Adjuster hasn't shown up and it's been over a week | Call the claims line again, ask for a supervisor if needed, and confirm your claim number and next steps in writing; document every call in your claim log. |
| Not sure if your county qualifies for FEMA aid | Check fema.gov/disaster/declarations or call 1-800-621-3362; FEMA Individual Assistance only exists after a specific federal declaration for your area. |
| Cash on hand was in one location and got destroyed/lost with the house | Going forward, split emergency cash across the kit, a home safe, and a vehicle rather than keeping it all in one spot. |
| Paper money is torn, burned, or water-damaged | Don't tape it together or throw it out — mail it to the Treasury's Bureau of Engraving and Printing Mutilated Currency Redemption program (BEP Form 5283); it's a free service if enough of the note is identifiable. See Part 1F. |
| Original documents got wet or singed and there's no backup copy | Don't force-dry with heat or peel apart stuck pages/photos; air-dry flat in a cool, low-humidity spot away from sun, or freeze them if there's too much to handle within 48 hours; get photos to a professional lab fast. See Part 4. |
| Missed the FEMA application deadline for your disaster | Check disasterassistance.gov or call 1-800-621-3362 — a late-registration process sometimes exists for a documented good reason, but the safest move is always to apply well before the ~60-day mark from the disaster declaration. |
