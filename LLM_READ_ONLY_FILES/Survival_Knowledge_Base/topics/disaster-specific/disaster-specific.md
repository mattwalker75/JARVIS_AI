# Disaster-Specific Survival Guides

## Read this first (the 30-second version)
1. **Every disaster boils down to one decision, made early: shelter-in-place or evacuate.** Getting this decision right — and making it BEFORE conditions get bad — matters more than any gear you own.
2. **Have a plan and a kit ready before you need them.** You will not have time to think clearly, shop, or pack once the emergency starts. Build your 72-hour kit and family plan today, not during the warning.
3. **Watch = be ready, Warning = act now.** A "Watch" means conditions are favorable for a hazard (tornado, severe storm, flood) in your area — get your kit staged and monitor radio/weather alerts. A "Warning" means the hazard is happening or imminent — take protective action immediately.
4. **Water and moving vehicles are two of the biggest hidden killers.** Never drive or walk into flood water ("Turn Around, Don't Drown"), and never run a generator, grill, or camp stove indoors or in a garage (carbon monoxide is invisible and deadly).
5. **Every disaster has a BEFORE (prepare), DURING (survive the event), and AFTER (recover safely) phase.** Most deaths happen because people skip the "before" prep or take shortcuts in the "after" phase (downed power lines, contaminated water, structural damage).
6. **North Texas is a multi-hazard region.** In a normal year you may face tornadoes, severe hail/lightning storms, flash floods, a crippling ice storm, extreme summer heat, and grassfires — sometimes more than one within months of each other. This guide is weighted accordingly.
7. **If in doubt about safety, choose the more cautious action.** Go to the safe room even if the tornado misses you; leave the wildfire-threatened area even if your house survives. Being wrong in the direction of caution costs you time; being wrong the other way can cost your life.

## Why this matters
Disasters kill and injure people in very different ways — wind and debris, drowning, hypothermia, carbon monoxide, heat stroke, smoke and burns, or the slow grind of no clean water, food, or medical care during a long outage. A generic "be prepared" mindset is not enough because **the correct action for one disaster can be the wrong action for another.** Going to a basement is right for a tornado but wrong for a flash flood. Opening windows helps in some situations but can be fatal during a wildfire smoke event or a chemical release. This guide gives you the specific, correct steps for each major disaster type so you are not guessing under pressure.

North Texas (Dallas–Fort Worth and surrounding counties) sits in a particularly hazard-dense zone: it is inside "Tornado Alley," gets some of the most frequent large hail and severe thunderstorms in the country, floods fast due to clay soil and urban runoff, experiences occasional catastrophic winter storms (the February 2021 Texas blackout left millions without power and water for days in freezing temperatures, and caused hundreds of deaths, many from hypothermia and carbon monoxide poisoning), and has brutal, prolonged summer heat that is its own silent killer. Knowing the right response for each hazard — and having supplies staged in advance — is genuinely lifesaving knowledge for anyone in this region.

## Key terms
- **Watch** — conditions are favorable for the hazard to develop (tornado watch, severe thunderstorm watch, flood watch, winter storm watch). Get informed, charge devices, stage your kit, and stay alert. It has NOT happened yet.
- **Warning** — the hazard is occurring, imminent, or has been detected (radar-confirmed tornado, flash flood warning, winter storm warning). Take protective action NOW — this is not the time to keep watching TV or finish a task.
- **Shelter-in-place** — staying inside your current structure (home, workplace, vehicle) and using it for protection rather than trying to travel. Usually correct for tornadoes, severe storms, and often for winter storms and pandemics.
- **Evacuate** — leaving the area proactively, before or during a disaster, because staying is more dangerous than leaving (wildfire approaching, mandatory hurricane/flood evacuation order, structural damage making the building unsafe). Evacuating early, before roads clog or water rises, is always safer than evacuating late.
- **Bug-out bag / go-bag** — a pre-packed bag (one per person, ideally) with essentials for at least 72 hours, kept ready by the door or in the car so you can leave within minutes if evacuation is ordered.
- **72-hour kit** — the baseline supply of water, food, medication, and gear to sustain your household for three days without outside help, the minimum FEMA and Red Cross recommend for any household.
- **Turn Around, Don't Drown** — the National Weather Service's flash-flood safety slogan: never drive or walk into water of unknown depth or any moving water, ever.
- **30-30 rule** — for lightning safety: if the time between seeing lightning flash and hearing thunder is 30 seconds or less, go indoors immediately; wait 30 minutes after the last thunderclap before going back outside.
- **Defensible space** — a cleared, maintained buffer zone around a structure (typically 30–100+ feet) with reduced vegetation, used to slow or stop an advancing wildfire/grassfire from reaching the building.
- **Grid-down** — an extended loss of electrical power (hours to weeks) affecting heating/cooling, water pumping/treatment, refrigeration, communications, and fuel pumps.

## What you need — 72-hour kit / go-bag (build this before any disaster)
Keep this staged year-round; check and rotate contents every 6 months.

| Category | Items |
|---|---|
| Water | 1 gallon per person per day, minimum 3-day supply (see [Water guide](../water/water.md)); water filter/purification tablets |
| Food | 3-day supply of non-perishable, no-cook food per person; manual can opener |
| Light & power | Flashlight, headlamp, extra batteries, hand-crank or battery weather radio (NOAA), portable power bank |
| First aid | Full first-aid kit, 7–14 days of prescription medications, basic OTC meds (see [First Aid guide](../first-aid/first-aid.md)) |
| Documents | Copies of ID, insurance, medical info, cash in small bills, in a waterproof bag |
| Clothing/warmth | Change of clothes per person, sturdy shoes, rain gear, emergency/space blanket, extra layer for cold |
| Tools | Whistle, dust masks (N95), plastic sheeting and duct tape (shelter-in-place sealing), wrench/pliers to shut off utilities, local paper maps |
| Sanitation | Moist towelettes, garbage bags, plastic ties (see [Sanitation guide](../sanitation-hygiene/sanitation-hygiene.md)) |
| Family-specific | Infant formula/diapers, pet food/supplies, extra glasses, important phone numbers written on paper (phones die/lose signal) |
| Vehicle | Keep a smaller version of this kit in each vehicle: blanket, water, snacks, phone charger cable, jumper cables, ice scraper |

**Every household should also have:** a written family communication plan (an out-of-area contact everyone calls/texts, since local circuits often jam; a meeting place if separated), and copies of this plan in every go-bag.

## TORNADO

**BEFORE**
1. Identify your safe room now: an interior room, closet, or hallway on the **lowest floor** of the building, away from all windows. A basement is best; if none, a small interior room on the ground floor works.
2. If you live in a mobile/manufactured home, identify a sturdy nearby building or designated community shelter — mobile homes, even tied down, are not safe in a tornado; plan to leave for shelter before the storm arrives.
3. Know your county's outdoor warning siren pattern and download/enable a NOAA weather radio or phone alert app with audible tornado warnings.
4. Practice a tornado drill with your household so everyone knows where to go in under 30 seconds.
5. Keep shoes and a flashlight near your safe room — you may need to walk over broken glass/debris in the dark afterward.

**DURING**
1. On a **Tornado Watch**: stay alert, keep the radio on, charge phones, and stage your go-bag near your safe room.
2. On a **Tornado Warning** or when you see/hear a tornado (freight-train roar, debris cloud, rotating funnel): go to your safe room immediately.
3. Get to the **lowest floor**, **most interior room**, away from windows, doors, and outside walls.
4. Get under something sturdy (heavy table, workbench) and cover your head and neck with your arms; use a mattress, blankets, or a bike/motorcycle helmet for extra head protection if available.
5. If caught in a vehicle: do **not** shelter under a highway overpass — wind speed actually increases there and debris funnels through. Options in order of preference: drive to a sturdy building; if none reachable, get into a low-lying ditch or depression far from the vehicle, lie flat, and cover your head; as an absolute last resort, stay buckled in the car with your head below window level and a blanket/coat over you.
6. If caught outdoors with no shelter: lie flat in the lowest ground available (ditch, depression), away from trees and vehicles, and cover your head.
7. Do not open windows — this old myth does not equalize pressure and just wastes time you need to take cover.

**AFTER**
1. Stay in shelter briefly after the initial wind passes — tornadoes can produce multiple funnels or return; wait for the "all clear" or clear silence/calm.
2. Check yourself and others for injuries before doing anything else; apply first aid (see [First Aid guide](../first-aid/first-aid.md)).
3. Watch for broken glass, exposed nails, and downed power lines — treat every downed line as energized and stay at least 10 meters (30+ ft) away; report it, don't touch it.
4. Check for gas leaks (smell/hissing sound) before using any open flame or electrical switch; shut off the main gas valve if you suspect a leak and leave the building.
5. Photograph damage for insurance before cleanup if safe to do so, then begin clearing debris with gloves and sturdy shoes.
6. Avoid damaged buildings until a professional confirms they are structurally sound.

## SEVERE THUNDERSTORM, HAIL & LIGHTNING

**BEFORE**
1. Know the signs: darkening sky, increasing wind, distant thunder — severe storms in North Texas can produce hail exceeding golf-ball or even softball size and can spawn tornadoes with little notice.
2. Move vehicles into a garage or under solid cover if hail is forecast; hail can total a car and shatter windshields/skylights.
3. Secure or bring in outdoor furniture, trampolines, and anything that can become wind-blown debris.
4. Charge devices and have your NOAA radio ready.

**DURING**
1. Apply the **30-30 rule**: if you count 30 seconds or less between a lightning flash and the thunder, go indoors immediately.
2. Once indoors, stay away from windows (hail and wind-driven debris), avoid corded phones and plumbing (lightning can travel through wiring and pipes), and avoid contact with anything metal connected to the outside.
3. If caught outdoors with no shelter available: avoid open fields, hilltops, isolated tall trees, and metal objects (fences, poles, golf clubs); crouch low on the balls of your feet, feet together, minimizing ground contact — this is a last resort, not a safe position.
4. If in a vehicle, stay inside with windows up — the metal frame (not the rubber tires) helps route lightning current around occupants.
5. For hail: get away from windows and skylights; if driving, pull under an overpass or gas station canopy briefly ONLY if it does not block traffic or create a hazard, and wait it out rather than continuing to drive through large hail.
6. Wait a full **30 minutes after the last thunder** before resuming outdoor activity.

**AFTER**
1. Check for roof, window, and vehicle damage from hail and wind.
2. Treat any downed tree limbs or power lines as hazards; do not approach.
3. If someone is struck by lightning, they carry no residual charge — it is safe to touch them immediately; check breathing and pulse and begin CPR if needed while calling for help (see [First Aid guide](../first-aid/first-aid.md)).

## FLASH FLOOD

**BEFORE**
1. Know if you live, work, or regularly drive through a flood-prone area (low water crossings, creek/drainage areas are common across North Texas's clay-soil terrain, which sheds rain fast instead of absorbing it).
2. Identify high ground near your home and along your regular routes before you ever need it.
3. Keep important documents and valuables elevated off ground-floor storage if you are in a known flood zone.
4. Never assume a "flood watch" won't turn serious quickly — flash floods can develop within minutes to hours of heavy rain, including rain that fell miles upstream.

**DURING**
1. **Turn Around, Don't Drown** — never drive or walk into water covering a road, even water that looks shallow or calm. As little as 6 inches of fast-moving water can knock over an adult; 12 inches can float and sweep away most cars; 24 inches can carry away large SUVs and trucks. Depth and road condition underneath are impossible to judge by eye.
2. If your vehicle stalls or is being swept in rising water, abandon it immediately and move to higher ground on foot if you can safely do so; do not stay with the vehicle.
3. If water is rising around your home, move to the highest level of the building — but avoid sealed attics with no escape route/vent to the roof; if you must go to an attic, bring a way to break through the roof.
4. If outdoors and water is rising, move away from creek beds, storm drains, and low areas immediately toward higher ground — do not wait to "see how bad it gets."
5. Avoid walking through any moving water; if you must, use a stick to check depth and footing, and never cross alone if it can be avoided.
6. Stay off bridges over fast-moving water.

**AFTER**
1. Do not return to a flooded area until authorities declare it safe — floodwater often carries sewage, chemicals, and hidden debris, and roads may be undercut or washed out even after water recedes.
2. Avoid floodwater contact with open wounds; wash exposed skin thoroughly with soap and clean water afterward.
3. Do not use any electrical appliance or outlet that has been submerged until inspected.
4. Treat any standing water source in your home area as contaminated — do not use it for drinking, cooking, or wound cleaning; see [Water guide](../water/water.md) for emergency purification.
5. Document damage for insurance, then begin drying out the structure quickly (within 24–48 hours) to reduce mold growth.

## WINTER STORM / ICE STORM & EXTENDED GRID FAILURE

North Texas's most notorious modern disaster example is the **February 2021 winter storm/blackout**, in which a severe arctic outbreak overwhelmed the state's power grid, causing rolling and, for many, multi-day blackouts during record cold, alongside water main breaks and boil-water notices. Hundreds died, disproportionately from hypothermia and carbon monoxide poisoning (people running generators, grills, or cars in garages, or bringing charcoal/propane heaters indoors, to stay warm). This section assumes both an ice storm AND the real possibility the power does not come back for days.

**BEFORE**
1. Insulate/protect pipes: know where your home's main water shutoff is, let faucets drip during extreme cold, open cabinet doors under sinks on exterior walls, and disconnect/drain outdoor hoses.
2. Stock extra blankets, warm layered clothing, and a safe non-electric heat source with a documented safe-use plan (see CO warning below).
3. Fill bathtubs and containers with water before a storm if a boil-water notice or main break is possible — this is stored water for flushing/washing, not drinking untreated.
4. Keep vehicle fuel tanks at least half full; a full tank can serve as an emergency heat/charging source if you must shelter in the car briefly (never for extended periods, and only with a cracked window, never in an enclosed garage).
5. Have a battery or hand-crank radio and flashlights; know that cell towers and Wi-Fi routers fail within hours of a power outage without battery backup.

**DURING**
1. Layer clothing (several thin layers trap more warmth than one thick layer); wear a hat — a large share of body heat is lost through the head; keep hands and feet dry.
2. Close off unused rooms and gather the household into ONE small, well-insulated room to conserve body heat; stuff towels/blankets under doors to block drafts.
3. **Carbon monoxide danger is the single deadliest indoor risk of winter grid-down survival.** NEVER run a generator, gas grill, camp stove, or charcoal burner indoors, in a garage (even with the door open), or near an intake vent. Never use an oven for heating. Install/check battery CO detectors if available; symptoms of CO poisoning (headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion) can appear before you notice anything is wrong, and it can be fatal in sleep.
4. If using a generator, run it outdoors, more than 20 feet from any door, window, or vent, with the exhaust pointed away from the structure.
5. Eat and drink regularly — digestion generates body heat; avoid alcohol, which increases heat loss despite feeling warming.
6. Keep moving periodically to generate body heat, but avoid sweating heavily, which then cools you further.
7. If pipes have not yet frozen, keep a trickle of water running from faucets on exterior walls; if a pipe does freeze, shut off the main water valve before it can burst and flood the home, and thaw it slowly (never with an open flame).
8. Watch each other for signs of hypothermia: shivering, slurred speech, confusion, drowsiness — these need immediate warming and, if severe, emergency medical care (see [First Aid guide](../first-aid/first-aid.md)).
9. If driving is unavoidable on ice: slow down significantly, increase following distance, avoid bridges/overpasses (they freeze first), and keep an emergency kit and full tank in the vehicle.

**AFTER**
1. Inspect pipes for bursts before fully restoring water pressure; shut off the main valve if a break is found.
2. Discard refrigerated/frozen food that has been above 40°F (4°C) for more than 2 hours, or use the "when in doubt, throw it out" rule.
3. Ventilate the home thoroughly before assuming CO risk has passed if any combustion heater was used.
4. Check on elderly or vulnerable neighbors — hypothermia and CO risk often hit isolated individuals hardest.
5. Boil or treat tap water if a boil-water notice is in effect (see [Water guide](../water/water.md)) — winter storms frequently cause water-main breaks and treatment plant failures.

## EXTREME HEAT

Extreme heat is one of the most under-recognized killers, and it is a defining, near-annual North Texas summer risk — afternoon heat index routinely exceeds 105–110°F (40–43°C) for weeks at a time.

**BEFORE**
1. Identify air-conditioned locations (home, a public cooling center, library, mall) you can reach if home cooling fails.
2. Check on elderly, very young, chronically ill, and outdoor-working members of your household or neighborhood — they are at highest risk.
3. Never plan to leave a person, child, or pet in a parked vehicle, even briefly — vehicle interior temperature can exceed 140°F (60°C) within an hour even with a cracked window.
4. Stock electrolyte drinks/oral rehydration salts and lightweight, light-colored clothing.

**DURING**
1. Stay hydrated with water throughout the day; do not wait until thirsty — thirst is already a sign of mild dehydration.
2. Avoid strenuous outdoor activity during peak heat (typically 11am–6pm); if outdoor work is unavoidable, take frequent shaded breaks and hydrate every 15–20 minutes.
3. Move to the coolest available space: lowest floor of a building (basement if available), shaded room, or a public cooling center if home AC is out.
4. Wet a cloth or your clothing and drape it on skin, especially neck, wrists, and forehead, to cool via evaporation, especially if AC is unavailable.
5. Recognize heat exhaustion (heavy sweating, weakness, nausea, cool clammy skin, still alert) vs. heat stroke (sweating may STOP, hot/dry or clammy skin, confusion, rapid pulse, collapse — a medical emergency). For heat stroke: call for emergency help, move the person to shade/cool area, remove excess clothing, and cool aggressively with water, ice packs at neck/armpits/groin, or immersion if possible, while awaiting help.
6. Never leave anyone — person or pet — in a parked vehicle.
7. Limit alcohol and caffeine, both of which increase fluid loss.

**AFTER**
1. Continue monitoring anyone who showed signs of heat illness for several hours — symptoms can recur or worsen.
2. Replenish electrolytes, not just plain water, after heavy sweating.
3. Check that AC/fans and any backup cooling plan are ready before the next heat event — extreme heat in Texas is rarely a single-day event.

## WILDFIRE / GRASSFIRE

North Texas's dry grasslands and periodic drought conditions make fast-moving grassfires a real risk, especially in exurban and rural fringe areas.

**BEFORE**
1. Create **defensible space**: clear dry grass, leaves, and dead brush within at least 30 feet of structures; keep gutters clear of debris; store firewood/propane away from the house.
2. Know your evacuation routes in at least two directions in case one is blocked by fire or smoke.
3. Keep a go-bag ready and vehicle fueled during high-fire-risk conditions (dry, windy weather, burn bans in effect).
4. Sign up for local emergency alerts; monitor for red-flag warnings (weather conditions favorable to fast fire spread).

**DURING**
1. If evacuation is ordered or fire is visibly approaching, **leave early** — do not wait to "see how close it gets." Roads clog and visibility drops fast in smoke.
2. Close all windows and doors, remove flammable curtains, and shut off gas at the meter before leaving if time allows.
3. Wear long sleeves/pants and an N95 mask if smoke is present; keep car windows up and recirculate air on if driving through smoke.
4. If trapped with no escape route, look for a cleared area, plowed field, or large body of water; avoid dense vegetation and canyons/draws that funnel fire and heat.
5. Never try to outrun a fire uphill or through dense brush — fire moves faster uphill and through fuel-loaded vegetation than most people can run.

**AFTER**
1. Do not return until officials declare the area safe — hot spots, weakened trees, and unstable structures remain dangerous for days.
2. Check the roof and gutters for embers/smoldering material before assuming the structure is safe.
3. Avoid breathing ash and smoke residue; wear a mask while cleaning up, and treat ash as an irritant, not something to sweep dry (dampen first).
4. Check on neighbors and livestock/pets that may have been affected.

## EXTENDED POWER GRID-DOWN (multi-day to multi-week)

This section covers what changes when an outage stretches from days into weeks (major ice storm, cascading grid failure, or severe cyberattack/infrastructure damage), beyond the specific hazards already covered above.

**BEFORE**
1. Build supplies for water, food, and medication well past the standard 72 hours if feasible — two weeks is a much safer planning target for a region prone to cascading winter grid failures.
2. Establish a priority order in your head now: **water, then shelter/warmth or cooling, then food, then sanitation, then security**, in that rough order — this keeps decisions clear when stressed.
3. Learn manual alternatives for daily tasks: hand-crank or battery radio, non-electric can opener, backup cooking method used only outdoors (see [Fire guide](../fire/fire.md)).

**DURING**
1. **Water:** ration and treat/purify all water from questionable sources; do not assume tap water is safe if treatment plants have also lost power (see [Water guide](../water/water.md)).
2. **Food:** eat perishable/refrigerated food first, then freezer food, then shelf-stable/canned last, to avoid spoilage waste; discard anything of uncertain safety.
3. **Sanitation:** if water service is also down, plan an alternative toilet method (bag-and-bucket system) and handle waste safely to prevent disease spread (see [Sanitation guide](../sanitation-hygiene/sanitation-hygiene.md)).
4. **Security:** be aware that extended outages can increase local crime/opportunism; keep a low profile about supplies, secure the home, and know your neighbors — mutual aid is one of the most effective security strategies (see [Security guide](../security-defense/security-defense.md)).
5. **Communication:** conserve phone battery, use text over calls (texts often get through when voice networks are jammed), and check in with your out-of-area contact per your family plan.
6. **Mental health:** extended stress and isolation take a toll — maintain routines, check on neighbors, and watch for signs of serious distress in yourself and others (see [Psychological First Aid guide]).

**AFTER**
1. Reintroduce systems gradually — flush water lines, discard spoiled food, and have gas/electrical systems inspected before full reliance if there was any risk of damage.
2. Restock the 72-hour kit immediately so you are ready for the next event; extended outages often recur in the same region within a few years.
3. Debrief as a household: what ran out first, what worked, what to change — update your kit and plan accordingly.

## PANDEMIC / DISEASE OUTBREAK

**BEFORE**
1. Keep a slightly larger stock of shelf-stable food, medication, and hygiene supplies so you can minimize trips out during a local outbreak.
2. Know basic hygiene practices as routine, not just crisis response: regular handwashing, covering coughs/sneezes, staying home when sick.
3. Keep a thermometer, basic OTC medications, and a way to isolate a sick household member (a separate room and, ideally, bathroom) identified in advance.

**DURING**
1. Follow guidance from local health authorities on distancing, masking, and isolation — these measures work best when adopted early, not after local spread is already high.
2. Isolate anyone who is sick in a separate room; if possible, have one designated caregiver interact with them to limit exposure to the rest of the household.
3. Wash hands frequently with soap for at least 20 seconds, especially before eating and after coughing/sneezing/using the bathroom; use hand sanitizer (60%+ alcohol) when soap is unavailable.
4. Disinfect frequently touched surfaces (doorknobs, phones, counters) regularly.
5. Avoid unnecessary crowds and indoor gatherings during active local outbreaks; increase ventilation indoors when possible.
6. Monitor symptoms and know local guidance on when to seek medical care versus manage at home.

**AFTER**
1. Continue basic hygiene practices as outbreaks often come in waves.
2. Restock any depleted medical and hygiene supplies.
3. Watch for lingering symptoms in recovered household members and know signs that warrant medical follow-up.

## EARTHQUAKE & EMP — brief note

These are lower-probability in North Texas specifically but worth a short mention for completeness.

- **Earthquake:** if indoors, **Drop, Cover, and Hold On** — drop to hands and knees, take cover under sturdy furniture, and hold on until shaking stops; stay away from windows and heavy furniture that could topple. If outdoors, move away from buildings, trees, and power lines. Minor, induced seismic activity (often linked to industrial activity) does occur in parts of Texas, though damaging earthquakes are rare.
- **EMP (electromagnetic pulse, natural or man-made):** could disable unprotected electronics and the power grid over a wide area with little warning. There is no reliable individual "shelter" response during the event itself; the practical preparation is the same as extended grid-down above (water, food, manual tools, non-electronic backups for critical information and communication) rather than any special action at the moment it occurs.

## ⚠️ Safety
- Never drive or walk into flood water of any depth you cannot clearly verify — this is the single most common preventable disaster death.
- Never run a generator, grill, or any fuel-burning heater indoors or in a garage — carbon monoxide is odorless, invisible, and can be fatal in sleep within hours.
- Never shelter under a highway overpass during a tornado — it increases wind speed and debris risk.
- Treat every downed power line as live and stay at least 10 meters away; do not touch it or anything it is touching.
- Do not return to a disaster-damaged area, flooded zone, or fire-affected zone until officials declare it safe — hidden structural, electrical, and contamination hazards persist after the visible danger passes.
- In extreme heat, never leave a person or pet in a parked vehicle, even briefly, even with windows cracked.
- If someone shows signs of heat stroke, hypothermia, or CO poisoning, treat it as a medical emergency — cool/warm/ventilate immediately and get emergency help; do not "wait and see."

## Troubleshooting
- **"The power/water/cell service failed before I finished preparing."** Prioritize in this order: personal safety and shelter first, then water, then warmth/cooling, then food, then everything else — you do not need a perfect kit to survive the first 24–48 hours if you get the priority order right.
- **"I don't know if I should shelter or evacuate."** If the hazard is fast-moving and localized to your structure's safety (tornado, severe storm) — shelter-in-place in the safest part of the building. If the hazard is approaching your whole area and will worsen (wildfire, mandatory evacuation order, rising floodwater with time to leave) — evacuate early, before roads are blocked.
- **"My phone has no signal and I can't get alerts."** Fall back to a battery or hand-crank NOAA weather radio; these work without cell towers and are one of the highest-value low-cost preps for this exact scenario.
- **"I smell gas or see sparking wires after a storm."** Leave the building immediately, do not operate any switch or open flame, and report it from a safe distance; do not go back in until authorities/utility clear it.
- **"We used up our water/food faster than planned in a grid-down."** Ration immediately to a lower daily amount, prioritize water over food (humans survive far longer without food than water), and treat any additional water source before use (see [Water guide](../water/water.md)).
- **"Someone is showing confusion, slurred speech, or collapse during heat/cold exposure."** Treat as a medical emergency immediately — begin cooling (heat) or warming (cold) while calling for help; do not wait for symptoms to "pass on their own."

- [ ] Household knows the safe room for tornadoes (lowest floor, interior, no windows)
- [ ] Family communication plan written down (out-of-area contact, meeting point)
- [ ] Know your area's flood-prone spots and never drive through covered roads
- [ ] Main water and gas shutoff locations known by all adults in the household
- [ ] Non-electric heat/cooking source identified, with a firm rule: outdoors only, never in garage/indoors
- [ ] Battery or hand-crank NOAA weather radio available and working
- [ ] Defensible space cleared if in a wildfire/grassfire-prone area
- [ ] Plan reviewed and kit restocked every 6 months, and immediately after any event that used supplies
