# North Texas Regional Survival Guide — Anna, TX & Collin County Blackland Prairie

## Read this first
This guide is written for **Anna, Texas** and the surrounding **Collin County blackland prairie** (Melissa, McKinney, Prosper, Van Alstyne, Weston, Farmersville, and the rural county roads between them). It exists because generic survival advice — "find a stream," "look for a hill to navigate by," "watch for frost in October" — is often **wrong or useless here**. This land is flat, hot, clay-soiled, storm-prone, and shaped by cattle pasture and grid-pattern farm roads, not mountains or forests. Read this guide alongside the general topic guides (water, foraging, weather, first aid, disaster-specific) — this one tells you **how those general rules apply to this specific ground**.

Three facts to internalize immediately:
1. **Heat kills faster here than cold, but the Feb 2021 ice storm proved winter can kill too** — Texas's power grid (ERCOT) is not required to be winterized to the same standard as northern states, and a hard freeze can mean days without power or water pressure.
2. **The nearest "clean" natural water is usually a muddy cattle pond, not a mountain stream.** Every rural property near Anna has at least one stock pond. Treat every one of them as contaminated until you clear and disinfect it.
3. **There are no ridgelines or peaks to navigate by.** You navigate this country by the **section-line road grid**, fence lines, and the direction water drains (toward the East Fork Trinity River, which flows generally south-southeast into Lavon Lake).

## Why this matters
Generic knowledge fails locally in specific, predictable ways. A guide that says "boil water from a mountain stream" doesn't tell you that the pond behind the neighbor's barn is thick with cattle manure runoff and blue-green algae by August. A guide that says "find high ground in a flood" doesn't help when the highest point for a mile is a highway overpass. A guide that says "watch for snakes in rocky outcrops" misses that the water moccasins here live in the pond you're about to fill your bucket from. Local knowledge is what turns a survival manual from theory into a plan you can actually execute from your own back door. Every recommendation below is chosen because it is common, present, and relevant within a few miles of Anna, TX.

## Key terms
- **Blackland Prairie** — the dark, clay-rich soil ecoregion that covers Collin County; it is fertile but turns to deep, sticky mud when wet and cracks hard when dry.
- **Stock pond / tank** — a small, man-made pond dug on rural/ranch land to water cattle; extremely common (nearly every rural parcel has one); also called a "tank" locally.
- **East Fork Trinity River** — the river that drains most of Collin County; flows south/southeast, feeding Lavon Lake and eventually Lake Ray Hubbard downstream.
- **Section road** — a farm-to-market or county road following the old mile-square survey grid; in flat country these roads, not terrain, are your main navigation reference.
- **ERCOT** — the Texas electric grid operator; because Texas's grid is largely isolated from other states, widespread outages (like Feb 2021) can last longer with less outside help.
- **Blue-green algae bloom** — a toxic bacterial bloom common in warm, nutrient-rich (fertilizer/manure runoff) still water in summer; can poison people and pets even after boiling for some toxin types.
- **Flash flood** — rapid rise of normally small or dry creeks after heavy rain; extremely dangerous on flat prairie because water spreads fast and low-water crossings flood before drivers realize it.
- **Cross Timbers** — the belt of oak/juniper woodland just west of the blackland prairie; Collin County sits mostly in blackland prairie with some Cross Timbers influence to the west.

## What you need
- A paper county road map or downloaded offline map of Collin County (do not rely on a phone with no signal/power).
- A compass (flat terrain makes this more useful, not less — you cannot eyeball direction from hills that don't exist).
- Multiple water containers (5-gallon buckets, water jugs) and the water-treatment supplies covered in `../water/water.md` (unscented bleach, coffee filters/cloth, a pot to boil in).
- Sturdy boots or snake gaiters if you will walk pasture, pond edges, or tall grass.
- A NOAA weather radio (battery/hand-crank) — cell towers and internet fail first here, and this region depends on timely tornado/flood warnings.
- Sun protection: wide-brim hat, long sleeves, sunscreen, and more water than you think you need — heat is the area's most consistent killer.
- A list of nearby known water sources, neighbors, and shelter points, written down on paper (see the "Local Resources" section).

## Climate & Seasons
Anna sits in **USDA Hardiness Zone 8b** per the 2023 USDA Plant Hardiness Zone Map — meaning average winter lows in the 15–20°F range, with occasional colder outliers. Typical **average last spring frost is mid-to-late March** and **average first fall frost is mid-to-late November**, giving a growing season of roughly 230–250 days. These are averages, not guarantees — hard freezes have occurred outside this window.

- **Summer (June–September): the primary threat.** Highs regularly reach 95–105°F with high humidity, turning the heat index dangerous. July–August often bring **drought** conditions — ponds and creeks drop or stop flowing, grass cures to tinder, and grassfire risk spikes. Heat is the deadliest hazard in this region historically, more so than any storm.
- **Spring (March–May): severe storm and tornado season.** North Texas sits inside "Tornado Alley's" southern extension. Supercell thunderstorms, large hail, damaging straight-line winds, and tornadoes are common March through June, with an additional secondary peak possible in the fall. Flash flooding from training thunderstorms is a major spring/early-summer risk on the flat prairie.
- **Fall (October–November): second severe-weather window, then cooldown.** Fall can also produce severe storms, though less reliably than spring. First frost typically arrives mid-to-late November.
- **Winter (December–February): usually mild, occasionally brutal.** Most winter days are cool and dry, but this region is vulnerable to sudden Arctic air intrusions ("blue northers") that drop temperatures 40+ degrees in hours. The **February 2021 winter storm (Winter Storm Uri)** froze natural gas infrastructure and wind turbines statewide, causing days-long rolling blackouts, burst pipes, and dozens of deaths from cold and carbon monoxide poisoning (improper generator/grill use indoors). Because Texas's grid (ERCOT) is largely self-contained, it cannot easily import power from neighboring states during a statewide event — assume outages could last **several days**, not hours.

**Practical takeaway:** plan for heat first (May–September), severe storms second (March–June, and again some falls), and occasional multi-day winter grid failure third (any year, worst risk January–February).

## Local Water Sources — Ranked

Always assume any water you did not pour from a sealed, labeled container is contaminated. This ranking reflects what is realistically nearby around Anna and Collin County, from safest/easiest to hardest/riskiest.

| Rank | Source | Where to find it locally | Local caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Home reserves** | Water heater tank (30–50 gal), toilet tank, canned goods liquid, any stored water/ice | Safest; still worth disinfecting if uncertain |
| 2 | **Rainwater** | Roof runoff via gutters/downspouts, tarps, buckets during spring/fall storms | Clean-ish but collect after first "roof wash" rain; still filter/disinfect if roof is dusty |
| 3 | **Stock ponds ("tanks")** | Extremely common on any rural/ranch parcel around Anna, Weston, Van Alstyne, Farmersville | Cattle manure and urine runoff, blue-green algae blooms in summer heat, muddy blackland silt — always clear AND disinfect; avoid ponds with visible scum, dead animals, or foul smell |
| 4 | **Creeks feeding the East Fork Trinity** | Numerous small tributary creeks cut through pastures and cross under county roads throughout the area | Often dry or reduced to stagnant pools in summer drought; can flash-flood violently in spring storms; agricultural and road runoff |
| 5 | **Lavon Lake / Lake Ray Hubbard** | Lavon Lake (East Fork Trinity reservoir, roughly 15–25 miles south/southeast of Anna depending on location); Lake Ray Hubbard farther downstream/south | Large public reservoirs — more diluted than a farm pond, but still require full treatment; boat traffic, shoreline development, and lake currents differ from still ponds; farther to travel to on foot |
| 6 | **Private wells** | Common on older rural properties throughout Collin County | Without power, most electric well pumps won't run; a well with a hand pump or bucket-drop is usable but still disinfect; abandoned/unused wells may have contamination or structural hazards |

**Rule for all surface water in this region:** blackland prairie runoff is naturally muddy (high clay turbidity) even when uncontaminated — this is normal, not a sign it's "extra dirty," but it means you must **settle and filter before you disinfect**, or pathogens hiding in the silt will survive chemical treatment. Full step-by-step purification is covered in `../water/water.md` — this guide tells you *which* local sources to prioritize and what extra caution each needs.

## Local Edible Plants
These grow wild and are commonly found on the blackland prairie, in fence lines, pastures, and creek bottoms around Anna:

- **Mesquite pods** — the thorny mesquite tree is everywhere in pastures; the sweet pods (not the beans inside raw, but the dried pod pulp) can be ground into a flour-like meal. Ripe pods are yellowish-brown and fall in late summer.
- **Prickly pear cactus** — common on drier, sunnier ground; both the paddles (nopales, de-spined and cooked) and the red/purple fruit (tuna, careful of glochid spines) are edible.
- **Pecans** — native pecan trees line many creek bottoms and old farmsteads; nuts drop in October–November.
- **Dewberries and blackberries** — bramble thickets along fence lines and creek edges fruit in May–June.
- **Wild onion** — thin grass-like leaves with a distinct onion smell, found in fields and lawns especially in cooler months (late winter/spring); crush a leaf to confirm the onion odor before eating anything that looks like it.
- **Persimmon (Texas/American persimmon)** — small trees along creek bottoms and fence lines; fruit is astringent and inedible until fully soft/ripe, typically after first frost.

Cross-reference `../food-foraging-wild-edibles/food-foraging-wild-edibles.md` for identification detail and the universal edibility test — never eat anything from this list based on this summary alone.

## Local Poisonous Plants to Avoid
- **Water hemlock** — grows near creeks, ditches, and wet pasture edges; one of the most acutely toxic plants in North America. Avoid any umbrella-flowered ("carrot family"-looking) plant growing directly in or beside water.
- **Poison ivy** — "leaves of three" vines common on fence lines, tree trunks, and creek-bottom woods; causes severe contact rash, not internal poisoning, but very common here.
- **Nightshade (white horsenettle / silverleaf nightshade, *Solanum elaeagnifolium*, and relatives)** — common weedy plant in pastures and roadsides with purple star-shaped flowers and yellow berries; berries and foliage are toxic.

## Local Hazards

### Venomous snakes
All three of Texas's major venomous snake families are present in this area:
- **Copperhead** — found in wooded creek bottoms, brush piles, and leaf litter; well-camouflaged tan/copper banded pattern; bites are painful but rarely fatal to healthy adults.
- **Cottonmouth (water moccasin)** — lives in and around ponds, creeks, and slow water — exactly the same stock ponds you may be drawing water from. Will stand its ground rather than flee. Collin County sits near the northwestern edge of the cottonmouth's Texas range, so it's less consistently common here than in East/Southeast Texas — don't assume every water snake you see locally is one, but don't rule it out either.
- **Rattlesnakes (mainly Western diamondback locally)** — found in open pasture, brush, rock/debris piles, and around barns; give audible warning but not always.

**Avoidance:** wear boots and watch foot placement in tall grass and around pond edges; do not put hands into brush piles, hollow logs, or under structures without looking; check around barns/woodpiles before reaching in; be extra cautious approaching stock ponds at dawn/dusk when snakes are active near water.

**Bite first aid — DO:** stay calm, move away from the snake, keep the bitten limb still and below heart level, remove rings/tight clothing before swelling starts, get to medical care as fast as possible, note the snake's color/pattern if safely possible (do not chase or handle it, dead or alive).
**Bite first aid — DO NOT:** do not cut the wound, do not attempt to suck out venom, do not apply a tight tourniquet, do not apply ice, do not give the person alcohol or caffeine. See `../first-aid/first-aid.md` for full envenomation protocol.

### Fire ants
Reddish mounds in lawns, pastures, and disturbed soil throughout the region; swarm and sting simultaneously when a mound is disturbed. Watch where you sit, kneel, or place gear on the ground, especially after rain when mounds relocate.

### Spiders
Both of Texas's medically significant spiders are present locally: **black widows** (shiny black with a red hourglass marking, found in woodpiles, sheds, garages, and undisturbed outdoor clutter) and **brown recluses** (light brown with a faint violin marking, also common in sheds, garages, closets, and stored boxes). Both prefer dark, undisturbed spaces — shake out gloves, boots, and stored gear before use, and wear gloves when moving woodpiles or reaching into infrequently-used sheds/garages. See `../animal-insect-pest-hazards/animal-insect-pest-hazards.md` for identification detail and bite first aid.

### Chiggers and ticks
Common in tall grass, brush, and along trails spring through fall. Tuck pants into boots, use repellent, and do a full-body check after being in brush or grass. Ticks here can carry disease; remove promptly with fine-tipped tweezers, grasping close to the skin.

### Heat
The single most consistent killer in this region. Heat exhaustion and heatstroke risk climbs sharply June–September, worsened by high humidity. Hydrate proactively, rest in shade midday, and know the signs (see `../first-aid/first-aid.md`).

### Tornadoes and severe thunderstorms
Primary risk window March–June. Have a NOAA weather radio, know your safest interior room (small interior room/closet/bathroom on the lowest floor, away from windows), and do not wait to see the tornado — act on warnings.

### Flash floods
Flat terrain means water spreads fast rather than channeling downhill; low-water road crossings over local creeks can go from dry to impassable in minutes during heavy spring rain. **Turn around, don't drown** — never drive or wade into moving water over a road, even if it looks shallow.

### Grassfires
Summer drought cures pasture grass into fast-burning fuel; grassfires can move quickly across open range, driven by the region's near-constant wind. Keep defensible space (mowed/cleared ground) around structures, especially in late summer.

## Terrain & Navigation
This is flat blackland prairie — do not expect hills, ridgelines, or forests to orient by. Practical local navigation:
- **The section-road grid is your map.** Farm-to-market roads and county roads largely run true north-south and east-west in a mile-ish grid pattern; if you know which county road you're near, you can usually walk to a known road within a mile or two in any direction.
- **Water drains generally south/southeast toward the East Fork Trinity River**, which itself flows toward Lavon Lake and eventually Lake Ray Hubbard. If you are lost and find a creek, following it downstream (in the direction of flow) tends to lead toward these larger, more populated water bodies and eventually toward roads and towns.
- **Fence lines and tree lines** (mesquite mottes, creek-bottom pecan/oak stands) are often the only visible landmarks for miles; use them as waypoints and note their compass bearing.
- **The sun and a watch/compass** are more reliable here than "look for the mountain" advice from other regions — there isn't one. See `../navigation/navigation.md` for shadow-stick and analog-watch methods, which work especially well on this open, flat ground.

## Local Resources
Anna and its neighboring towns are small enough that community networks matter as much as any kit:
- **Neighbors and ranch owners** — in a rural/exurban area like this, most neighboring properties have livestock, wells, generators, or stored feed/water; a knock on the door is a legitimate survival resource, especially for people who've lived here for years.
- **Feed stores** (e.g., local co-ops and farm supply stores common throughout Collin County) — often carry water containers, tarps, basic tools, batteries, propane, and sometimes bottled water/ice even when grocery stores are picked clean, because they serve an agricultural customer base.
- **Churches** — in small North Texas towns, churches frequently open as informal shelters, warming/cooling centers, and distribution points during declared emergencies, even without an official Red Cross designation.
- **Volunteer fire departments and county emergency management** — rural Collin County is served heavily by volunteer fire departments; local Facebook groups and county emergency management pages are often the fastest source of real-time local information when official channels are slow.

## Seasonal Survival Calendar
- **Winter (Dec–Feb):** Weatherize now — insulate/drip exposed pipes before a hard freeze, keep extra blankets and a non-electric heat source (used only with ventilation), store extra water in case pipes burst or freeze. Watch for sudden Arctic fronts; a mild week can turn into a Uri-style multi-day grid failure with little warning.
- **Spring (Mar–May):** Peak tornado/severe storm season. Keep the weather radio charged, know your safe room, watch for flash flooding at low-water crossings. Last frost typically passes by late March — safe to plan outdoor food-growing activity after this.
- **Early Summer (Jun):** Storms taper, heat ramps up fast. Dewberries/blackberries ripen. Check stock ponds — levels still usually decent before deep drought sets in.
- **Peak Summer (Jul–Aug):** Heat and drought peak — ponds and creeks may drop or stagnate, algae blooms more likely, grassfire risk high. Prioritize shade, hydration, and extra caution with any open-water source. Mesquite pods ripen.
- **Fall (Sep–Nov):** Secondary severe-storm risk, then cooldown. Pecans and persimmons ripen (persimmons after first frost, roughly mid-to-late November). Good window to restock firewood and check winterization before December.

## Scenario walkthroughs

### Scenario: No power in Anna, TX — you need to find water and make it drinkable
1. **Start inside your own house first.** Before going anywhere, check your water heater tank (30–50 gallons, often overlooked) and toilet tank (not the bowl). Full method: `../water/water.md`, Part 1A.
2. **If home reserves run low, check for a stock pond nearby.** Nearly every rural property near Anna has one. This is your most likely next source — but treat it as contaminated: it very likely has cattle runoff, and in summer may have algae blooms.
   - Walk the pond edge carefully — cottonmouths live in exactly this habitat. Wear boots, watch where you step, do not reach blindly into brush at the water's edge.
   - Skip any pond with visible green/blue-green scum, foul smell, or dead animals nearby — some algae toxins are not removed by boiling.
3. **Collect water away from the immediate shoreline mud**, in the clearest-looking area, ideally after letting a bucket sit undisturbed so silt settles.
4. **Clear it first.** Blackland-prairie pond water is naturally muddy (turbid) even without contamination. Pour it through a clean cloth, coffee filter, or a DIY sand/charcoal filter to remove visible particles — see `../water/water.md`, Part 2, for the settle-and-filter method. Do not skip this: germs can hide inside silt and survive disinfection otherwise.
5. **Disinfect it — do one of these (both covered fully in `../water/water.md`):**
   - **Boil** for a full minute (most reliable; kills germs from cattle runoff).
   - **No way to boil?** Add 8 drops of plain unscented household bleach per gallon, stir, wait 30 minutes.
6. **If the pond is unusable (algae bloom, dead animal, or otherwise suspect)**, walk or drive to the next-ranked source: a local creek feeding the East Fork Trinity (check for flow, avoid stagnant pools), and apply the same clear-then-disinfect process. In a longer emergency, Lavon Lake or Lake Ray Hubbard are viable but farther-away options.
7. **Store the finished water** in a clean, covered, food-grade container, clearly separated from any untreated ("raw") water so you never mix them up.
8. **If truly stuck with no other option**, knock on a neighbor's door — in this area many rural households have a generator-powered well pump, stored water, or another pond you haven't tried.

### Scenario: A tornado warning is issued for Collin County while you're at home in Anna
1. Get the warning as early as possible via a battery/hand-crank NOAA weather radio (do not rely solely on a phone — towers can fail).
2. Move immediately to your safe room: an interior room, closet, or bathroom on the lowest floor, away from windows; put as many walls as possible between you and the outside.
3. Cover yourself with a mattress, blankets, or a helmet if available to protect against debris.
4. Stay there until the warning is cancelled or the storm clearly passes — do not go outside to look.
5. After the storm, watch for downed power lines, gas leaks, and flash flooding from the rain that came with the storm system; check on elderly/rural neighbors, who may not have gotten the warning.

### Scenario: A February-style hard freeze knocks out power for several days
1. Assume the outage could last days, not hours (Texas's ERCOT grid has limited ability to import power during a statewide event, as happened in Feb 2021).
2. Let faucets drip (if water pressure remains) to reduce pipe-freezing/bursting risk; know where your main water shutoff is in case a pipe does burst.
3. Never run a generator, gas grill, or camp stove indoors or in a garage — carbon monoxide poisoning killed people region-wide during the 2021 storm. Vent any combustion heat source outdoors or through a proper flue.
4. Layer blankets and clothing; close off unused rooms to concentrate warmth; keep a household member's phone charged for emergencies only.
5. Check on elderly or rural neighbors who may be isolated by icy section roads (Collin County roads are not always priority-plowed/treated).

## ⚠️ Safety
- **Never drink untreated pond, creek, or lake water in this region** — assume agricultural runoff and algae risk are present even when water looks clear.
- **Do not handle or attempt to kill venomous snakes** — most bites happen when someone tries to move or kill a snake rather than simply avoiding it.
- **Never drive or wade through a flooded low-water crossing** — flat terrain hides how fast and deep floodwater can be; turn around.
- **Never run generators, gas grills, or charcoal indoors, in a garage, or near windows/vents** — carbon monoxide is a leading cause of death in Texas winter storm outages.
- **Do not shelter under highway overpasses during a tornado** — despite being a common myth, overpasses can create dangerous wind-tunnel effects; a low interior room is far safer.
- **Treat every stock pond as contaminated** — clear (settle/filter) and disinfect (boil or chemical) before drinking, every time, no exceptions.

## Troubleshooting
- **"The only pond nearby is covered in green scum."** Do not use it — this can indicate a toxic algae bloom that survives boiling. Move to the next-ranked source (creek, well, or a different pond) or a neighbor's supply.
- **"I don't have a compass and I'm lost in open pasture."** Use the shadow-stick or analog-watch method (see `../navigation/navigation.md`) and orient toward the nearest section road rather than trying to walk cross-country; roads here form a reliable grid.
- **"Power's out and I don't know how long it'll last."** In a statewide winter event, plan for multiple days, not hours, given ERCOT's limited ability to import power; prioritize warmth safety (no indoor combustion) over convenience.
- **"I was bitten by a snake and I'm not sure if it was venomous."** Treat it as venomous regardless — keep the limb still and low, remove tight items, and get to care immediately; do not waste time trying to identify or catch the snake.
- **"The creek near me is completely dry."** Normal in summer drought; check upstream pools that sometimes persist, or move to a stock pond or larger reservoir (Lavon Lake / Lake Ray Hubbard) instead.
- **"I found wild onions/mesquite pods/pecans but I'm not 100% sure of the ID."** When in doubt, don't eat it — cross-check against `../food-foraging-wild-edibles/food-foraging-wild-edibles.md` and use the universal edibility test before consuming anything based on this summary alone.
