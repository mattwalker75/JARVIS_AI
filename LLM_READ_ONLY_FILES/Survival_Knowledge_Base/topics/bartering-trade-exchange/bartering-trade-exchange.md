# Bartering, Trade & Resource Exchange

## Read this first (the 30-second version)
1. **This is ordinary neighbor-to-neighbor trading during a disruption** — a power outage, storm, flood, or supply shortage where stores are closed, ATMs are down, or a specific item is simply unavailable. It is not about currency collapse or "doomsday economics."
2. **Trade surplus, not need.** Before offering anything, ask: *do I have more of this than I'll need before things return to normal?* If you're unsure you have enough, keep it and offer something else instead (see Part 1B).
3. **Value is about need, scarcity, and condition — not a fixed price.** The same item is worth more to a diabetic than to someone without medical needs, and worth more the day before a store reopens than the day after (Part 2).
4. **Skills and services (first aid, repair, childcare, hauling water) are often more valuable than any physical item**, because a skill doesn't run out (Part 3).
5. **Trade fairly and safely:** verify what you're getting before you hand anything over, don't gouge a neighbor in genuine need, meet in a safe visible place, and write down what happened (Parts 4–6).
6. **Keep some cash in small bills** ($1s, $5s, $10s) — card readers and ATMs commonly fail in outages, and stores may have no way to make change for a $100 bill (Part 7).

---

## Why this matters
When a storm, flood, ice event, or extended power outage disrupts normal supply chains for days or weeks, stores may be closed, delivery trucks stop, and the item you desperately need — a specific medication, a phone charger, propane, a working flashlight — may simply not be purchasable at any price, from anyone but a neighbor who happens to have one extra. At the same time, you likely have things others need: a full gas can, spare batteries, first-aid skills, or just two working hands to help someone haul water. **Trading directly with people around you — goods for goods, goods for skills, skills for skills — fills these gaps quickly, strengthens the neighborhood, and reduces desperate, unsafe behavior** (looting, price gouging, dangerous scavenging) because people have a legitimate way to get what they need. Handled badly — through scams, exploitation, or unsafe meetups — trading can also create real harm. This guide is about doing it well: fairly, safely, and without unnecessary risk.

## Key terms (plain language)
- **Barter** — trading a good or service directly for another good or service, without cash changing hands (though a trade can also be part-cash, part-goods).
- **In-kind trade** — the general term for trading "in goods" rather than in money.
- **Relative value** — what something is worth *right now, to these two people*, based on need and scarcity — not its normal retail price.
- **Scarcity** — how hard something is to get. Scarcity rises fast when supply chains break, even for cheap everyday items (batteries, ice, gas).
- **Price gouging** — charging an unfair, exploitative price for a necessity during a declared emergency, taking advantage of someone's desperation rather than trading fairly. Most U.S. states have price-gouging statutes that switch on automatically once a disaster is officially declared (by a governor or the president) and apply to businesses selling necessities — Texas's version, for example, activates the moment the Governor or the President declares a disaster and defines an illegal "exorbitant" price as one **15% or more above** what the seller was charging just before the disaster (full detail in Part 4B and the North Texas notes). These statutes are aimed at retailers, not neighbor bartering — but the *ethical* principle behind them (don't exploit someone's desperation just because you can) applies just as much to trading with a neighbor.
- **Ledger** — a written record of who traded what with whom and when.
- **Swap / exchange event** — an organized gathering where several households bring items to trade, rather than one-on-one trading.
- **Benchmark good** — a common, stable, easy-to-compare item (e.g., a AA battery, a bottle of water, a roll of toilet paper) people informally use as a mental "unit" to compare the value of very different items during a trade.

# PART 1 — WHAT TO TRADE: GOODS THAT ACTUALLY MOVE IN A SHORT/MEDIUM EMERGENCY

## 1A. Categories people actually want and trade
These are the categories that come up again and again in real disaster aftermaths (hurricanes, ice storms, extended outages) because they solve immediate, physical problems — not because they're exotic or rare in normal times.

| Category | Specific examples | Why it's wanted |
|---|---|---|
| **Water treatment** | Water filters/straws, purification tablets, unscented household bleach, coffee filters | Tap water may be off, boil-water notices are common; see the [Water guide](../water/water.md) |
| **Power & batteries** | AA/AAA/9V/D batteries, battery packs, hand-crank or solar chargers, working flashlights, headlamps | Devices die fast; batteries are small, light, and easy to trade in exact quantities |
| **Fuel** | Propane canisters, gasoline (in an approved container), lighter fluid, charcoal | Needed for cooking, heat, generators — see the [Power & Energy guide](../power-energy/power-energy.md) |
| **First aid & OTC medicine** | Bandages, gauze, antiseptic, pain relievers (ibuprofen/acetaminophen), antidiarrheal, antihistamines, electrolyte packets | Small injuries and stomach illness are common in a disruption; see the [First Aid guide](../first-aid/first-aid.md) |
| **Hygiene & sanitation** | Toilet paper, soap, hand sanitizer, feminine hygiene products, diapers, wet wipes, garbage bags | Sanitation failures cause the *next* wave of illness; see [Sanitation & Hygiene](../sanitation-hygiene/sanitation-hygiene.md) |
| **Food staples** | Canned goods, rice/beans, powdered milk, baby formula, pet food, salt | Shelf-stable and universally needed; see [Supplies, Inventory & Rotation](../supplies-inventory-rotation/supplies-inventory-rotation.md) |
| **Tools & repair items** | Tarps, rope/paracord, duct tape, a working hand saw, nails/screws, a multitool, plywood | Storm damage repair is nearly universal after wind/flood events; see [Tools, Knots & Repair](../tools-knots-repair/tools-knots-repair.md) |
| **Light & warmth** | Candles, matches/lighters, hand warmers, blankets | Cheap, small, and high-value when the power's out |
| **Information/communication items** | A spare battery-powered or hand-crank radio, printed maps | Low supply, high value when cell networks are down |

> A useful rule of thumb: **the best trade goods are small, durable, divisible into useful quantities (you can trade "4 batteries," not just "the whole pack"), and solve an immediate physical problem** — not luxury items.

## 1B. Deciding what to KEEP vs. what to TRADE
Before you offer anything, run it through this simple check:

1. **Do the math on your own need first.** How many days might this disruption last (check local forecasts/official updates), and how much of this item would you use in that time? If you have exactly enough or less, **don't trade it away** — offer something else.
2. **Trade genuine surplus and duplicates.** Two flashlights when you need one, a case of water when you need three bottles, skills you have that cost you nothing extra to share (see Part 3) — these are your best trade goods.
3. **Never trade away your last of anything critical** (last dose of a needed medication, only water filter, only working light) even for something tempting in return — a bad trade you can't undo is worse than not trading.
4. **Reassess if the situation changes.** If the outage is extending longer than expected, get more conservative about what you're willing to trade away; if it's clearly ending soon (utility crews are visibly working your street, an "all clear" is announced), you can be more generous.

## 1C. What generally should NOT be traded
- **Prescription medications belonging to someone else, or medication you can't be certain is safe/authentic** — never distribute, sell, or trade prescription drugs; if a neighbor has a genuine medical emergency, the trade should be "help getting them care," not passing pills hand to hand. See the [First Aid guide](../first-aid/first-aid.md) and get professional help the moment it's reachable.
- **Firearms and ammunition** — regulated items with legal transfer requirements; not appropriate for informal barter.
- **Anything you suspect is stolen** — accepting stolen goods, even unknowingly, can create real legal trouble; if a deal seems too good given the circumstances, or someone can't explain plausibly how they have an item, decline.
- **Alcohol to minors, or anything illegal to sell/trade under normal law** — an emergency doesn't suspend these laws.

# PART 2 — JUDGING RELATIVE VALUE (there is no fixed price list — here's how to think about it)

## 2A. The three factors that actually set value in the moment
Forget the item's normal retail price. In an emergency, three things determine what something is really worth in a specific trade, right now:

```
        NEED                 SCARCITY              CONDITION
   "How badly do I      "How hard would it     "Does it actually
    need this right       be to get this          work / is it
    now?"                 another way?"            fresh/undamaged?"
        │                      │                       │
        └──────────────┬───────┴───────────┬───────────┘
                        ▼                   ▼
                  Higher on all three  =  Higher relative value
                  Lower on any one     =  Lower relative value
```

- **Need** — a AAA battery is nearly worthless to someone whose devices all take AA, and priceless to someone whose child's hearing aid just died. Need is *personal and situational*, not universal.
- **Scarcity** — before the disruption, ice was free at any gas station; in a multi-day summer power outage, a single bag of ice can become one of the most-wanted items in the neighborhood because it's now genuinely hard to get.
- **Condition** — a "working" flashlight with dead batteries is not the same trade value as one with fresh batteries; a first-aid kit with expired antiseptic is worth less than a full, in-date one. **Always check condition before agreeing to a trade** (Part 4A covers verifying this).

## 2B. Why "prices" shift during an emergency — and why that's not automatically unfair
Value shifting with scarcity is normal economics, not inherently exploitative — water is nearly free when the tap works and becomes precious during a boil-water notice because the *supply* changed, not because anyone is being predatory. The line between "normal value shift" and "exploitation" is about **who is setting the terms and how**:
- **Normal:** two neighbors each have something the other needs and agree on a trade that feels reasonable to both, given today's circumstances.
- **Exploitative:** someone demands far more than a fair trade specifically *because* they know the other person has no other option and is desperate (see Part 4B on price gouging ethics).

## 2C. A practical way to compare very different items
Since there's no price tag, most informal trading uses a **benchmark good** — something common enough that people intuitively agree on its relative worth — as a mental yardstick. In many disaster contexts this has been a bottle of water, a roll of toilet paper, or a AA battery. You don't need a formal system: just ask both sides the same two questions:
1. **"How many of X would it take me to get Y another way (however inconvenient)?"** — if getting a working flashlight any other way would take a risky 10-mile walk, that flashlight is worth more than "one battery," even if a battery is what's being offered.
2. **"How much am I actually inconvenienced without this, versus how much am I inconvenienced by giving it up?"** — the fairest trades happen when both sides feel their inconvenience is roughly balanced by what they receive.

## 2C-2. A quick numeric gut-check (optional, for when it's genuinely hard to agree)
Most experienced traders never write a number down — they just feel out fairness in conversation. But if two people are stuck, this rough tool can break the deadlock:
1. Each side privately rates their own **need** for what they'd receive, 1 (low) to 3 (high), and the **scarcity** of what they're giving up, 1 (low) to 3 (high) — condition is a pass/fail filter, not a score (a broken item doesn't belong in the trade at all; fix or disclose it first).
2. Add need + scarcity for each side (max 6 each).
3. **Roughly equal totals = a fair trade as it stands.** A gap of 2 or more points means the lower-scoring side should add a little to balance it (a few more batteries, a short chore, a couple of dollars) — or the trade should be reconsidered.
This is a gut-check, not a formula — it exists to make an intuition explicit when two people can't agree, not to replace the conversation in Part 2C.

## 2D. Worked examples
- **Batteries for bandages:** You have spare AA batteries; your neighbor has surplus first-aid supplies but no working flashlight. Four AA batteries (enough for one flashlight) for a roll of gauze, some antiseptic wipes, and 4 bandages is a common, roughly-fair shape of trade — adjust based on how much surplus each side actually has.
- **Propane for labor:** You have a spare 1 lb propane canister; a neighbor with a bad back needs help clearing a fallen branch off their driveway. An hour of labor for one canister is a reasonable service-for-goods trade (see Part 3 for valuing labor).
- **Ice for cash + goods:** In a multi-day summer outage, a full cooler of ice (genuinely scarce) might reasonably trade for a mix of a few dollars in cash *and* a few canned goods, rather than for one single small item — scarcity legitimately raises relative value, it just shouldn't be pushed to an exploitative extreme (Part 4B).
- **Skill for skill:** A neighbor with generator/small-engine repair experience gets it running again for a household whose member happens to be an off-duty nurse; in exchange, she checks on an elderly relative's medication schedule and vitals twice a day for the rest of the outage. Neither side "pays" the other in goods at all — matching skill for skill, roughly matched in time and value, is a completely normal and often the *most* efficient trade available (Part 3).
- **A gouging example, for contrast:** a neighbor with the only working generator on the block demands a family's entire remaining first-aid kit and their only battery radio just to run one extension cord to their freezer for a night — refusing to negotiate and pointing out they have "the only one that works." This is exploiting desperation, not pricing in scarcity (Part 4B); a fair version might be a modest amount of cash or a couple of shareable items, or simply helping for free between neighbors who look out for each other.

# PART 3 — TRADING SKILLS AND SERVICES (often the best trade of all)

## 3A. Why a skill can beat any physical good
A physical item, once traded away, is gone. **A skill or service is renewable** — treating one more scrape doesn't use up your first-aid knowledge, and helping watch one more household's kids for an afternoon doesn't cost you a "unit" of childcare ability the way giving away your last flashlight costs you light. This makes skills some of the most sustainable, most valuable things you can offer in a prolonged disruption, and some of the most valuable things to seek out from neighbors instead of trying to solve everything yourself with stockpiled goods.

## 3B. Categories of service/skill trades that come up constantly
| Skill/service | Typical trade partners for it | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Medical/first aid** | Anyone with an injury or illness, especially if you have real training (EMT, nurse, veteran medic) | Be honest about the limits of your training; serious injuries still need professional care the moment it's reachable — see [First Aid](../first-aid/first-aid.md) |
| **Mechanical/electrical/generator repair** | Households with a broken generator, pump, or vehicle | See [Power & Energy](../power-energy/power-energy.md) and [Tools, Knots & Repair](../tools-knots-repair/tools-knots-repair.md) |
| **Carpentry/tarping/storm repair** | Anyone with wind or water damage | See [Home Structural Assessment](../home-structural-assessment/home-structural-assessment.md) |
| **Childcare** | Parents who need to do physically demanding recovery work (clearing debris, hauling water, standing in line for supplies) | Builds trust fast; extremely high-value to a stretched-thin household |
| **Cooking (especially for a group, or for someone without power)** | Households without a working stove, elderly neighbors | Pairs well with shared propane/fuel. **Food-safety caveat: always ask about allergies and disclose ingredients before anyone eats**, and follow basic safe-handling practice without refrigeration (cook meat/eggs/leftovers thoroughly, don't let cooked food sit out more than about 2 hours — 1 hour in North Texas summer heat, discard anything that smells off) — see [Food Preservation & Cooking](../food-preservation-cooking/food-preservation-cooking.md) |
| **Physical labor** (hauling water, clearing debris, standing in a supply line) | Elderly, injured, or overextended neighbors | Time-based; see valuing below |
| **Radio operation / information relay** | The whole block, if cell service is down | See [Communication guide](../communication/communication.md) |
| **Security/watch shifts** | Any household wanting overnight coverage | Coordinate through the neighborhood's watch rotation — see [Security & Defense](../security-defense/security-defense.md) |
| **Sewing/mending** (tarps, torn clothing, closing a gap in a shelter tarp) | Anyone with storm-damaged gear or clothing | Low-tech, high-value once stores are closed |
| **Pet/livestock care** (feeding, walking, temporary boarding) | Households evacuating, hospitalized, or overwhelmed with human needs | Especially valuable in rural North Texas properties with livestock |
| **Translation / interpreting** | Non-English-speaking neighbors dealing with responders, insurance, or aid workers | Can be the single most valuable service in a mixed-language neighborhood |
| **Elder care / disability support** (medication reminders, mobility help, company/wellness checks) | Elderly or disabled neighbors, especially those living alone | Pair with the vulnerable-neighbor guidance in Part 4B and the [Community & Mutual Aid guide](../community-mutual-aid/community-mutual-aid.md) |
| **Basic tech/device troubleshooting** (charging logistics, radio setup, backing up phone data before it dies) | Anyone relying on a phone/radio for information or contact | Small effort, often outsized value when information is scarce |

**"Time banking":** some communities formalize skill trading using the concept of **time banking** — one hour of any service equals one "credit," regardless of the skill, so an hour of childcare can trade evenly for an hour of tech help. It's a useful mental model for multi-household or ongoing arrangements even if you never use a formal system: track hours given and received the same way you'd track goods (Part 5).

## 3C. Valuing a service trade
Services are usually valued by **time and difficulty**, not by a fixed "price":
1. Agree up front on roughly how long the task will take and how physically demanding or skilled it is.
2. Compare it, in plain terms, to a goods trade of similar time/difficulty ("an hour of debris-clearing feels about like the flashlight-for-bandages trade above — what would feel fair for that?").
3. For ongoing or recurring help (daily childcare, daily wound care), agree on a simple daily or per-visit arrangement rather than re-negotiating every single time — write it in your ledger (Part 5) so both sides remember the arrangement the same way.
4. **It's completely fine, and common, for a skill trade to simply be reciprocal goodwill** ("I'll look at your generator, no need to trade anything, we're neighbors") — barter doesn't have to be transactional to be worth writing down; recording it still prevents misunderstandings later.

## 3D. Consent, honesty about limits, and safety in a skill trade
Skills and services carry a different kind of risk than goods — you're trusting a person with your body, your children, or your property, not just checking an item's condition. A few rules keep this safe for everyone:
1. **Only offer skills you genuinely have, and say plainly where your training ends.** "I'm a former combat medic, not a doctor" or "I've fixed small engines for years but I'm not a licensed electrician" protects both sides from a false sense of expertise.
2. **Get a clear, spoken yes before doing anything hands-on** — a first-aid check, watching someone's kids, or working inside someone's home. A quick "is it okay if I look at that cut?" or "are you comfortable with me watching the kids in your yard?" takes seconds and prevents real misunderstandings.
3. **Treat medical help beyond basic first aid as a bridge to professional care, never a replacement for it.** Say so out loud to the person you're helping, and keep working to get them real medical care the moment it's reachable — see the [First Aid guide](../first-aid/first-aid.md).
4. **For recurring skill trades** (daily childcare, daily wound checks, ongoing generator maintenance), explicitly revisit the arrangement every few days rather than assuming it continues indefinitely — put the next check-in date in the ledger (Part 5) so it doesn't quietly drift into an unspoken obligation on either side.

## 3E. Building a skills roster ahead of time
Knowing *before* an emergency who on your street has medical training, mechanical skill, or childcare capacity makes trading services fast and natural when it's actually needed, instead of a scramble. The [Community & Mutual Aid guide](../community-mutual-aid/community-mutual-aid.md) has a ready-to-use **Skills & Resources Roster Template** for exactly this — build that list before you need it, then use this guide's ledger (Part 5) to track the actual give-and-take once trading starts.

# PART 4 — ETHICS AND SAFETY: AVOIDING BOTH BEING CHEATED AND BEING THE PROBLEM

## 4A. Protecting yourself from being cheated
Common ways people get cheated in informal trading, and how to avoid each:

| Risk | How to protect yourself |
|---|---|
| **Weak/dead batteries passed off as "new"** | Test them in a device on the spot before completing the trade, or use a cheap battery tester if you have one |
| **Diluted or bad fuel** | Smell it (fuel smell should be consistent, not sour/off), check the container is a proper approved fuel container, ask where/when it was purchased; if in doubt, don't put it in an engine untested |
| **Expired food, medicine, or purification tablets passed off as good** | Check the date yourself; for anything without a visible date, ask directly and be willing to walk away |
| **A "working" tool/flashlight/radio that doesn't actually work** | Test it in front of the other person before the trade is final — this is normal and expected, not rude |
| **Being shorted on quantity** (fewer batteries/pills/items in a bag than agreed) | Count together, in view of both people, before parting ways |
| **Bait-and-switch** (agreeing to trade for item A, then being offered a lesser substitute at the meet) | Confirm exactly what's being traded before you leave home; if it changes at the meet, you're free to decline |
| **Pressure to decide instantly** ("someone else wants it, decide now") | A fair trader gives you a reasonable minute to check the item; heavy pressure is a red flag — it's fine to say "I'll pass" |

> ⚠️ **Test it before you finalize it.** Testing an item together, in the open, before the trade is complete is completely normal etiquette in barter — it protects both sides and is not an insult.

## 4A-2. Beyond a bad trade: recognizing organized disaster fraud
Not everyone who shows up during a disaster is a neighbor trying to trade fairly. The FTC, the U.S. Department of Justice, and the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau — separately, through their own consumer guidance — all warn that real disasters reliably attract real, organized fraud aimed at people who are stressed, displaced, or desperate — a different and more serious problem than an unfair barter, and worth recognizing on sight:
- **Fake or predatory contractors/repair crews** going door-to-door after storm damage, demanding cash up front, using high-pressure "sign today or lose the deal" tactics, and then doing shoddy work or vanishing. Ask for a license and references, get a written estimate that matches what was said out loud, and never pay the full amount before the work is done.
- **People falsely claiming to represent FEMA or a relief agency**, offering to "help you get aid" for a fee, or asking for your Social Security number or bank account details over the phone or door-to-door. Genuine FEMA staff carry visible photo ID and never charge a fee for disaster assistance or registration.
- **Fake charities** collecting donations — cash, gift cards, or cryptocurrency — that imitate real relief organizations. Give to organizations you already know, or independently verify a new one before donating.
- **Payment red flags, in general:** anyone asking to be paid only by wire transfer, gift card, or cryptocurrency — for a "trade," a "repair," or a "donation" — is very likely running a scam; these payment types are difficult or impossible to reverse, unlike cash handed over in person for a good you've verified.
- **Report it:** ReportFraud.ftc.gov, the National Center for Disaster Fraud Hotline (**1-866-720-5721**), and your state Attorney General's consumer-protection line (in Texas: **1-800-621-0508**) all take these reports — reporting protects the next household, even if you weren't the one who lost anything.

## 4B. Not being the problem: avoiding price gouging your neighbors
The same scarcity that raises an item's relative value (Part 2) can tempt a trader to demand an exploitative amount from someone with no other option. This is corrosive to a neighborhood exactly when cooperation matters most.
1. **Ask yourself: would this trade feel fair to me if our situations were reversed?** If the honest answer is no, adjust the offer.
2. **Extra scarcity value is fine; exploiting desperation is not.** It's reasonable that a bag of ice is "worth more" in a heat-wave outage than normally — it is not reasonable to demand someone's last first-aid supplies for it because you know they're desperate.
3. **Give more generously to genuinely vulnerable neighbors** (elderly, disabled, families with infants, anyone in a medical emergency) — in many real disasters, the households that come out okay are the ones that treated trading as *mutual survival*, not profit-seeking. See the [Community & Mutual Aid guide](../community-mutual-aid/community-mutual-aid.md) for supporting vulnerable neighbors even outside of any trade.
4. **Remember the reputational cost.** A neighborhood remembers who gouged them during a crisis, long after the crisis ends — this has real, lasting social and safety consequences for you and your household.
5. **Know that gouging retail sales during a declared emergency is often illegal — and, in Texas, the law is specific and has real teeth.** Once the Governor declares a disaster under Texas Government Code Chapter 418 (or the President declares a federal disaster), **Texas Business & Commerce Code §17.46(b)(27) and §17.4625** make it illegal for a business to sell or lease fuel, food, medicine, lodging, building materials, construction tools, or other necessities at an "exorbitant or excessive" price — legally defined as **15% or more above** what that seller was charging immediately before the disaster. The rule automatically extends to counties adjacent to a state-declared disaster area, and to the whole state for a federal declaration. Penalties reach **up to $10,000 per violation, plus up to $250,000 more if the victims are 65 or older**; anyone can report suspected gouging to the Texas AG's Consumer Protection Hotline, **1-800-621-0508**, or online. This statute is aimed at retailers, not casual one-to-one neighbor trades — but if a "swap" starts operating like a business (regularly reselling to the public for profit, rather than occasional trades with people you know), the actual law can start to apply, not just the ethics. Barter doesn't use a percentage markup at all (Part 2 explains why there's no fixed price to begin with) — the number above is a legal yardstick for retail pricing, not a target to aim for in a neighbor trade. The ethical takeaway is simpler than the statute: informally, hold the line at "fair given today's circumstances" (point 1 above), not at whatever the law technically permits a business to charge.

## 4C. Safety when meeting to trade
Treat an in-person trade with a stranger (not an established neighbor) with the same care as meeting anyone else you don't know well:
1. **Meet in a visible, public, or well-lit shared space** — a driveway, a porch, a designated neighborhood swap spot — never an isolated location.
2. **Bring another person if you can**, and tell someone else your plan (who you're meeting, where, roughly when you'll be back) before you go.
3. **Trade in daylight when possible.**
4. **Trust your gut** — if something about the person or the deal feels off, it's completely fine to decline or walk away, even mid-conversation.
5. **Don't advertise your full stockpile** while negotiating — bring only what you intend to actually trade, not a display of everything you own.
6. **For the broader hardening/awareness/de-escalation practices** (situational awareness, what to do if a meetup turns confrontational), see the [Security & Defense guide](../security-defense/security-defense.md) — this guide covers the trade-specific precautions above; that one covers general personal and home security.

## 4D. Legal and ethical notes
- **Barter itself is legal, and the IRS's own guidance carves out casual neighbor trading.** The IRS treats the fair market value of goods or services received through bartering as taxable income, in general (IRS Topic no. 420, Bartering Income) — but its guidance draws a clear line between a formal "barter exchange" (an organization that facilitates trades among many members and must issue Form 1099-B) and small, informal, occasional exchanges between people who simply know each other. **The IRS's own example of the latter is a neighborhood babysitting cooperative run by parents** — exactly the kind of arrangement this guide describes. A one-off trade of batteries for bandages, or watching a neighbor's kids for a few days during an outage, sits squarely in that everyday, informal category; it is not the kind of thing that generates a tax form, and it should never be a reason to hesitate over a trade that gets your household through an emergency. If bartering became a repeated, business-like pattern later (regularly trading goods or services as part of running a side business), that income is more clearly reportable — on Schedule C if business-related, or Schedule 1 otherwise, at fair market value — but that's a normal-times bookkeeping question, not a mid-crisis one.
- Never trade prescription medications, firearms/ammunition, or anything you can't verify wasn't stolen (Part 1C).
- Keep trades between consenting adults, be honest about the condition and limits of anything you offer (including the limits of your own skills — don't claim medical training you don't have), and don't make promises you can't keep on a repeated/ongoing service trade.

# PART 5 — RECORD KEEPING: A SIMPLE TRADE LEDGER

## 5A. Why bother writing it down
Memory is unreliable under stress, and "I thought we agreed..." disagreements are the single most common source of neighborhood friction after a trade goes on for more than a day or two. A simple written record — even a few lines in a notebook — prevents this, protects both people, and makes recurring/ongoing arrangements (like a daily childcare-for-cooking trade) easy to track fairly over time.

## 5B. Trade ledger template
Keep this in a notebook, on an index card box, or a shared page at your neighborhood's message board (see the [Community & Mutual Aid guide](../community-mutual-aid/community-mutual-aid.md) Part 3C on physical message boards). One line per trade:

| Date | Traded with (name) | I gave | I received | Notes / condition checked | Settled? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 6/12 | J. Alvarez (next door) | 4x AA batteries | Roll of gauze + antiseptic wipes + 4 bandages | Batteries tested in his radio before leaving | Yes |
| 6/13 | T. Nguyen (across st.) | 1 hr debris clearing | 1x propane canister (1 lb) | Canister sealed, unopened | Yes |
| 6/14 | M. Reyes (2 doors down) | Childcare, 3 hrs | Ongoing — will cook dinner for my family Thu | Recurring arrangement, revisit Fri | Pending |

## 5C. Time-banked / skill-trade variant
For a recurring skill trade (Part 3D), a simple running-hours format is easier to keep fair than re-negotiating every visit:

| Date | Traded with | Hours given (what) | Hours received (what) | Running balance | Next check-in |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 6/12 | M. Reyes | 3 hrs childcare | — | +3 hrs owed to me | 6/15 |
| 6/13 | M. Reyes | — | Cooked dinner (~1.5 hrs equivalent) | +1.5 hrs owed to me | 6/15 |
| 6/15 | M. Reyes | — | — | Reviewed together: called it even, no debt carried | Renew if outage continues |

The "running balance" doesn't need to be exact to the minute — its purpose is to make sure neither side quietly feels shorted over several days, and to give both people a natural point (the check-in) to say "we're even" or "let's adjust."

## 5D. How to use it well
1. **Write it down at the time of the trade**, not from memory later that night.
2. **Both people don't need identical copies** — one shared record at a trusted spot (or each person keeping their own line about the same trade) is enough; the point is that *something* exists in writing.
3. **Mark ongoing/recurring trades clearly as "Pending"** and revisit them at agreed check-ins so an informal arrangement doesn't quietly drift into a dispute.
4. **A ledger also settles most disagreements before they start.** If a "who gave what" argument does come up, the written line — however brief — is usually enough to resolve it without hard feelings; if memories genuinely differ and nothing was written down, a neutral third neighbor can help mediate (see the [Community & Mutual Aid guide](../community-mutual-aid/community-mutual-aid.md) Part 7B).
5. **This ledger is for your own one-to-one trades.** If your neighborhood is pooling and sharing *communal* resources (a shared generator, a community water source), that's tracked differently — see the [Community & Mutual Aid guide](../community-mutual-aid/community-mutual-aid.md) Part 6 for a communal-resource tracking method; use *that* one for shared/group resources and *this* one for direct trades between your household and another.

# PART 6 — ORGANIZING A SMALL COMMUNITY SWAP / EXCHANGE EVENT

Once a disruption runs more than a couple of days, one-on-one trading often isn't efficient enough — organizing a small, regular swap lets many households match needs and surpluses at once.

## 6A. When it's worth organizing one
- The disruption is lasting multiple days and several households have surplus/need mismatches.
- Your street or block already has some organizing structure (a phone tree, message board, or regular meeting — see [Community & Mutual Aid](../community-mutual-aid/community-mutual-aid.md) Parts 3–3D).
- People are already informally trading and it would help to consolidate it into one safe, visible, predictable time and place.

## 6B. Steps to set one up
1. **Pick a safe, visible, central location** — a driveway, a cul-de-sac, a shared common area, someone's front yard. Daylight hours only.
2. **Set a fixed, recurring time** (e.g., "every day at 5pm by the mailboxes" or "Saturday/Wednesday mornings") so people can plan around it rather than guessing.
3. **Announce it through your existing communication method** (phone tree, message board, word of mouth) — see the [Community & Mutual Aid guide](../community-mutual-aid/community-mutual-aid.md) Part 3.
4. **Set a few simple ground rules up front, out loud, before the first swap:**
   - No prescription medications, firearms, or ammunition change hands here (Part 1C, 4D).
   - Test/verify items before finalizing (Part 4A) — this is expected, not rude.
   - Be respectful of scarcity — no gouging (Part 4B).
   - Bring a buddy if you're uneasy going alone; the event itself, being public and pre-announced, is inherently safer than a solo meetup.
5. **Use a simple want/have board** — a piece of poster board or the neighborhood message board where people jot "HAVE: spare AA batteries, propane" / "NEED: infant formula, ibuprofen" ahead of time, so people don't need to show up and hope.
6. **Keep it first-come, first-served and conversational** rather than a formal auction — competitive bidding for scarce necessities tends to produce exactly the exploitative dynamic Part 4B warns against.
7. **Designate one or two people to keep a shared ledger** for the event (Part 5B format works fine for group swaps too) so recurring arrangements made there don't get lost.
8. **After the event, do a quick informal check**: did anyone leave without what they urgently needed? If so, see if someone can follow up with them directly before the next swap.

## 6C. Keeping it safe as it grows
- If the swap becomes large or draws people from outside your known/trusted circle, add a check-in/sign-in sheet and consider having it coincide with an existing organized gathering (a wellness-check meeting, a water distribution point) rather than standing alone.
- Watch for the same red flags as any one-on-one trade (Part 4A–4C) — a group setting reduces individual risk but does not eliminate it.

# PART 7 — EMERGENCY CASH: SMALL BILLS AS A TRADE TOOL

Barter is not the only medium of exchange during a disruption — cash still works between people, it just needs to be the right *kind* of cash.

## Why small bills specifically
- **Card readers and ATMs commonly fail** when the power or internet is down — this happens routinely in outages, floods, and storms — so cash becomes the only payment method guaranteed to work.
- **Stores and neighbors alike may have no way to make change.** A single $100 bill can be effectively useless in this situation; **$1s, $5s, $10s, and a few $20s** (plus a little rolled coin) are what actually gets used and accepted.
- Small bills also work naturally alongside barter: a trade can be **part-goods, part-cash** ("I'll trade you these 3 cans of beans plus $5 for that propane canister") when neither side has an exact goods-for-goods match.

## How much, and where to keep it
FEMA's own financial-preparedness guidance (Ready.gov, and the FEMA/Operation HOPE **Emergency Financial First Aid Kit — EFFAK**) recommends setting aside enough cash to cover **several days to a couple of weeks** of essentials, weighted toward small denominations, precisely because ATMs, card readers, and even bank branches can be knocked out for days after a disaster — and storing it split across more than one place (some at home in a fire-resistant container, some in a portable go-bag) rather than all in one spot. Building and storing the actual emergency cash kit — exactly how much to set aside, where to physically keep it split up, and combining it with your document go-kit — is covered in full in the [Documents, Money & Emergency Administration guide](../documents-money-admin/documents-money-admin.md), Part 2. Use that guide to build the kit; use this section's principle (favor small, useful denominations) when you're actually at a trade or swap deciding what to bring or offer.

## Counterfeit-cash awareness
Cash trades with people you don't know well (a swap event, a stranger passing through) carry the same counterfeit-bill risk as any normal cash transaction — a disruption doesn't make fake bills more common, but it does mean you're accepting cash from more unfamiliar people than usual, and banks/registers that would normally catch a fake may be closed or without power. A few quick, no-equipment checks before accepting any bill $20 or larger:
- **Feel it.** Genuine U.S. currency is printed on a cotton-linen blend with a distinctive texture and small raised ridges (from intaglio printing) you can feel with a fingernail, especially on the portrait and seals — a smooth, slick, or flimsy feel is a red flag.
- **Look for the watermark.** Hold the bill up to light — a faint image matching the portrait should be visible in a blank space to the side of it (not printed on the surface, but embedded in the paper itself).
- **Check the color-shifting ink.** On $10 bills and up (2003 series and later), tilt the bill — the numeral in the lower right corner on the front should shift color (e.g., copper to green) as the angle changes; a numeral that stays one flat color is suspicious.
- **Look for the embedded security thread.** A thin vertical strip embedded in the paper (visible when held to light) printed with the denomination and "USA," which also glows a specific color under UV light if you have a UV flashlight.
- **When in doubt, decline or ask for a different bill/denomination** — a genuine trader won't be offended by a quick check, the same way testing a battery or smelling fuel isn't (Part 4A); if a bill fails more than one check, don't accept it, and don't pass it on to someone else either.

# North Texas / regional notes (e.g., Anna, TX / Collin County)

- **Seasonal high-demand trade goods:** in summer heat-wave power outages, **ice, bottled water, and anything battery/solar-powered for fans or cooling** become the most-wanted items fast; in winter ice storms, **propane, firewood, hand warmers, and blankets** move quickly. See the [Extreme Heat & Cold guide](../extreme-heat-cold/extreme-heat-cold.md).
- **Rural/suburban mix means uneven surplus:** rural properties in this area often have surplus fuel, generators, livestock feed, well water, or hay; suburban households more often have surplus stored food, batteries, and first-aid supplies. This asymmetry makes rural-to-suburban and suburban-to-rural trading especially productive here — a rural household with a generator and a suburban household with a case of bottled water and a first-aid kit both benefit from finding each other.
- **Tornado season (roughly March–June) and severe storm damage:** tarps, plywood, nails/screws, rope, and hand tools become high-demand trade goods immediately after wind damage — stock and consider trading these; see [Home Structural Assessment](../home-structural-assessment/home-structural-assessment.md).
- **Wide-open, low-density streets** mean neighbors are often further apart than in dense urban areas — coordinate swap meetups by vehicle or with enough daylight to travel safely, and lean on an existing phone tree or CB/ham radio net (see [Communication](../communication/communication.md)) to announce trades or swaps when cell service is down.
- **Texas's price-gouging law is real and specific, not just a general guideline.** Once the Governor or the President declares a disaster covering this area, Texas Business & Commerce Code §17.46(b)(27) and §17.4625 make it illegal for a business to sell fuel, food, medicine, or another necessity at 15%+ above its pre-disaster price, with penalties up to $10,000 per violation ($250,000 more if victims are 65+) — full detail and the reporting hotline are in Part 4B. This law targets retailers, but it's worth holding yourself to the same standard informally with neighbors, and worth knowing it exists if you ever see a business — not a neighbor — clearly gouging after a North Texas ice storm, tornado outbreak, or flood.

# ⚠️ Safety — the most important warnings
- **Never trade prescription medications, firearms, ammunition, or anything you suspect is stolen.** These are not appropriate for informal barter and can create serious legal and safety problems (Parts 1C, 4D).
- **Always verify condition before finalizing a trade** — test batteries, smell fuel, check expiration dates, confirm quantities together, in view of both people (Part 4A).
- **Never let desperation on either side turn into exploitation.** Don't accept a trade that feels coerced, and don't extract one from someone who has no other option (Part 4B).
- **Meet in visible, safe, daylight locations; bring a buddy; tell someone your plan.** Trust your instincts and walk away from anything that feels wrong (Part 4C). For broader personal/home security, see [Security & Defense](../security-defense/security-defense.md).
- **Never trade away your last of anything life-critical** (final dose of medication, only water filter, only working light source) for anything else, no matter how good the offer looks (Part 1B).
- **A genuine medical emergency needs professional help, not a trade.** If someone is seriously ill or injured, the priority is getting them care the moment it's reachable — see the [First Aid guide](../first-aid/first-aid.md).
- **If a trade meetup turns confrontational, de-escalate and retreat — do not stay to defend an item.** See [Security & Defense](../security-defense/security-defense.md).

# Troubleshooting
| Problem | Fix |
|---|---|
| You don't know if an offered item is "worth" what's being asked | Use the need/scarcity/condition check (Part 2A) and compare to a benchmark good (Part 2C); it's fine to counter-offer or decline. |
| The other person wants to trade for something you can't spare | Offer a skill/service instead (Part 3), or a smaller partial trade, or simply decline — no trade is ever mandatory. |
| You suspect an item is fake/expired/watered-down | Test/verify it on the spot (Part 4A); if you can't verify it, don't complete the trade. |
| A neighbor is demanding an unfair amount for something you desperately need | Try a calm conversation about fairness (Part 4B); if unavailable, look for another source or a service-trade alternative; this is also a good topic to raise gently at a neighborhood meeting. |
| Two people disagree later about what was actually traded | This is exactly what the ledger (Part 5) prevents — start keeping one now, and for the current dispute, get a neutral third neighbor to help mediate (see [Community & Mutual Aid](../community-mutual-aid/community-mutual-aid.md) Part 7B). |
| You only have large bills ($50s/$100s) and no one can make change | Try to combine with a goods trade to cover the difference, or hold the large bill for when normal commerce resumes; build a small-bill kit ahead of time next time (Part 7). |
| A swap meetup feels unsafe or is drawing unfamiliar people | Add a sign-in/check-in step, move it to coincide with another organized gathering, or scale it back to trusted neighbors only (Part 6C). |
| You have skills to offer but nothing physical to trade | Lead with the skill (Part 3) — it's frequently more valued than goods, and costs you nothing to keep offering. |
| Someone claims to be from FEMA/a charity and wants cash, a wire transfer, or your personal/bank info | This is very likely a scam, not a trade — real FEMA staff carry photo ID and never charge for aid; verify independently and report to ReportFraud.ftc.gov or 1-866-720-5721 (Part 4A-2). |

- [ ] Identified genuine surplus in my household (not items I might still need) before offering to trade anything.
- [ ] Know the categories most likely to be wanted: water treatment, batteries/power, fuel, first aid, hygiene, food staples, tools (Part 1A).
- [ ] Compare any offered trade using need + scarcity + condition, not a fixed price (Part 2A).
- [ ] Considered offering a skill or service, not just goods (Part 3).
- [ ] Tested/verified any item's condition before finalizing a trade (Part 4A).
- [ ] Checked myself against price-gouging a neighbor in genuine need (Part 4B); know that Texas law itself bans a 15%+ markup on necessities once a disaster is declared (Part 4B, North Texas notes).
