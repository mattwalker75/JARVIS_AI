## Read this first

If the power just went out, do these five things before anything else:

1. **Stop and think before you plug anything into a generator or run one at all.** More people are hurt by misusing backup power than by losing power in the first place. Carbon monoxide (CO) poisoning and backfeeding a generator into house wiring are the two biggest killers in this whole topic — read the Safety section before you touch fuel-powered equipment.
2. **Turn off or unplug things you don't need right now.** Every watt you don't use is a watt you don't have to generate or store.
3. **Keep the refrigerator and freezer closed.** An unopened fridge stays cold for about 4 hours; a full freezer, about 48 hours (24 if half full).
4. **Put your phone in low-power mode and stop using it for anything but essential communication.** It is your lifeline for information and calling for help — protect its battery like it's medicine.
5. **If you have a corded (non-battery) phone, plug it in now.** Landline corded phones often still work in a power outage because they draw power from the phone line itself, not your house wiring.

## Why this matters

Modern life runs on continuous electricity: refrigeration for food and medicine (including insulin), phones for calling 911 and checking emergency alerts, medical devices like CPAP machines and oxygen concentrators, and lighting so people don't fall or start fires with candles. When the grid goes down — from a storm, a heat-driven demand spike, a winter freeze-out, or a downed line — you are suddenly your own utility company. You don't need to power your whole house; you need to power a short list of things that keep people safe, informed, fed, and warm (or cool), for as long as the outage lasts.

The single biggest mistake beginners make is skipping straight to "I need a generator" without understanding **conservation first, sizing second, safety always**. A $20 battery-powered lantern and a fully charged phone will get most families through a one-night outage better than a generator they don't know how to run safely.

## Key terms

- **Watt (W):** A unit of power — how fast energy is being used or produced right now. A phone charger might use 5–20 W; a refrigerator, 100–800 W (with a startup surge of 3–5x that).
- **Watt-hour (Wh):** Energy over time — watts multiplied by hours. A 10,000 mAh power bank at 3.7V stores about 37 Wh. This is the number that tells you how long something can run.
- **Amp-hour (Ah):** How battery capacity is usually labeled, especially for car and deep-cycle batteries (e.g., "100 Ah"). To get watt-hours, multiply Ah by the battery's voltage (100 Ah x 12V = 1,200 Wh).
- **Volt (V):** Electrical "pressure." USB devices use 5V. Car and most portable batteries use 12V. House outlets in the US use 120V AC.
- **AC (Alternating Current):** What comes out of wall outlets and generators — used by most household appliances.
- **DC (Direct Current):** What batteries, solar panels, car electrical systems, and USB ports produce/use.
- **Inverter:** A device that converts DC battery power into AC power so you can run normal household plugs off a battery or car.
- **Charge controller:** A device between a solar panel and a battery that prevents overcharging and damage — required for any solar setup with a panel bigger than a small trickle charger.
- **Surge/starting watts vs. running watts:** Motors (fridges, well pumps, power tools) draw a big surge (2–5x normal) for about one second when starting, then settle to a lower "running" wattage. Undersized generators fail at the surge moment, not while running.
- **Backfeeding:** Plugging a generator into a wall outlet or breaker box without a proper transfer switch, which can send power backward down the line to the grid — a leading cause of utility worker deaths. Covered in depth in Safety.
- **Deep-cycle battery:** A battery designed to be drained and recharged repeatedly (unlike a car starter battery, which is designed for a brief high-current burst).

## What you need

Priority order — get the top of this list even if you get nothing else:

- Fully charged phone(s) + a low-tech backup light source (headlamp or LED lantern) — cheap, and covers most emergencies
- One or two 10,000–20,000 mAh USB power banks, kept charged year-round
- A battery-powered or hand-crank AM/FM/NOAA weather radio
- Headlamps and/or LED lanterns with spare batteries (see Lighting section)
- A 12V car charger/inverter adapter (works with any car you own)
- Optional, in rising order of cost and complexity: a small solar panel + charge controller + 12V battery kit, a portable power station ("solar generator"), or a fuel-powered generator with a properly installed transfer switch
- A CO (carbon monoxide) detector with battery backup — non-negotiable if you own or might use any fuel-burning device
- Basic tools: cable with the correct connector types for your gear, a multimeter (optional but very useful for troubleshooting), fuel stabilizer if storing gasoline

## Method 1: Conserve first — prioritize your loads

Before adding any power source, cut demand. This is free and works immediately.

1. Make a list of what actually needs power: phone(s), medical devices, one or two lights per occupied room, the refrigerator/freezer if the outage may be long, and maybe a fan or small heater blanket for temperature-sensitive people (infants, elderly, medically fragile).
2. Rank that list. Life-safety and medical devices are always #1 (CPAP, oxygen concentrator, insulin refrigeration). Communication is #2 (phone, radio). Food safety is #3. Comfort (lights beyond one per room, fans) is #4.
3. Unplug or switch off everything not on your list — TVs, game consoles, unused chargers, second refrigerators. Many devices draw "phantom" power even when off.
4. Switch every phone and laptop to its lowest power mode, turn off background app refresh, and dim screens.
5. Batch tasks. Turn a light on to find something, then turn it off. Charge all phones together during one generator run rather than running equipment continuously.
6. Reassess every few hours as you learn how long the outage might last — ration harder if it's looking like days, not hours.

## Method 2: Power banks — rationing your phone and small electronics

1. Calculate roughly how many charges you have: a 10,000 mAh bank gives most phones 2–3 full charges; a 20,000 mAh bank gives 4–6.
2. Charge your phone in shorter bursts (e.g., 20–30%) rather than to 100% each time — you get more total charge cycles out of the bank and reduce heat buildup in both devices.
3. Turn on airplane mode while charging if you don't need connectivity that moment — searching for signal drains the phone battery fast and slows charging.
4. Use one bank at a time; keep others sealed/unused as reserve.
5. If you have solar or a car available, recharge power banks during the day and use them at night — this makes a small solar panel far more useful than trying to charge phones directly from it after dark.
6. Keep power banks somewhere between 20–80% charged in normal times (not fully drained or always at 100%) to extend their long-term lifespan for whenever an emergency actually happens.

## Method 3: Solar charging chain (panel → charge controller → battery → inverter)

This is the core off-grid setup. Understand the chain before buying anything.

```
[Solar Panel] --DC--> [Charge Controller] --DC--> [Battery] --DC--> [Inverter] --AC--> [Household plug loads]
                                                       |
                                                       +--DC--> [USB port / 12V accessories]

Caption: Energy flows left to right. The charge controller protects the
battery from overcharging; the inverter is only needed for standard AC
plugs. Skip the inverter entirely if you only need USB/12V output.
```

1. **Pick your battery size first**, based on what you need to run. Example: keeping 2 phones and a lantern going overnight needs well under 100 Wh/day. Keeping a small fridge running intermittently might need 1,000+ Wh/day.
2. **Match the panel to the battery and your sunlight.** A rough rule of thumb: a 100W panel in good sun produces roughly 300–400 Wh per day (less in winter, less in North Texas' hazy summer humidity, far less if partly shaded or cloudy). To fully charge a 1,200 Wh battery from empty, you'd realistically want several days of a 100W panel, or a single day with 300W+ of panels.
3. **Always use a charge controller** if the panel is anything more than a tiny 5–10W trickle panel. Match the controller's amperage rating to your panel and battery voltage (most small kits are pre-matched — check the manual).
4. **Connect in order:** panel to charge controller, charge controller to battery, then (optionally) inverter to battery for AC devices, or a USB port directly off the battery/controller for phones.
5. **Position the panel** for maximum sun: facing south in the Northern Hemisphere, tilted roughly toward the sun's angle, no shade from trees or roof lines, and clean of dust/pollen — even light shading can cut output far more than the shaded area alone would suggest.
6. **Charge phones directly from a USB port on the controller/battery during the day** when possible — this is more efficient than battery → inverter → wall charger → phone, since every conversion step loses some energy as heat.
7. **Monitor the battery.** Most kits include a simple voltage readout or percentage. Don't run a lead-acid battery below about 50% regularly, or a lithium battery below about 10–20%, if you want it to last for future emergencies.

## Method 4: Hand-crank and pedal generators

1. These produce very small amounts of power (a few watts) — realistic uses are a hand-crank radio, a small LED light, or a slow trickle charge to a phone/power bank, not running a fridge.
2. Crank at a slow, steady pace rather than fast bursts — most hand-crank units regulate output and a steady pace is easier to sustain and less likely to damage the internal generator.
3. Many hand-crank radios/lights have a built-in small battery — crank to charge that internal battery, then use the device off the battery, rather than trying to power something directly while cranking.
4. Treat hand-crank power as a backup for your backup — useful when batteries are dead and there's no sun, but plan to rely on it as little as possible; it's slow and tiring.
5. Pedal-powered generator kits (bicycle-based) can produce more sustained wattage (tens of watts) and are worth considering only if you already have significant power needs and no other source.

## Method 5: Using your car as a power source

1. **Never run the car in a closed garage, even with the door "cracked."** Move it outside or into an open carport before running it for any length of time. This is a top cause of CO deaths during outages.
2. For basic phone charging, plug a USB adapter into the car's 12V outlet ("cigarette lighter") and run the engine at idle only long enough to top off — you don't need to run it continuously.
3. To run small AC devices (like a CPAP or a laptop charger), use a 12V-to-AC inverter plugged into the 12V outlet; keep total load well under the inverter's rating (check the label — common small inverters are 150–400W).
4. Don't run the car engine continuously for hours just to charge a phone — it wastes fuel you may need for evacuation later, and it risks CO exposure and overheating if parked in one spot for a long time. Run it in short sessions (15–30 minutes), engine off between sessions.
5. If your medical device (CPAP, etc.) has a 12V-compatible battery or DC cord, use that directly rather than going through an inverter — it's more efficient and reduces the equipment you're relying on.
6. Watch your car battery and fuel gauge — you may need that vehicle to evacuate. Don't drain the starting battery running accessories with the engine off for extended periods.

## Method 6: Portable power stations ("solar generators")

1. These are large battery packs (typically 200–2,000+ Wh) with a built-in inverter, multiple AC outlets, USB ports, and often a DC/12V port — no separate inverter or charge controller needed for basic use.
2. Size by your priority list from Method 1: check the Wh rating against your daily needs (a 500 Wh unit might run a CPAP overnight, charge several phones, and run a light for several nights).
3. Charge it in advance and keep it topped off — these are batteries first, and batteries lose some charge sitting idle. Check the manual for recommended storage charge level (commonly 40–80% for long-term storage).
4. Most accept solar panel input directly (check panel wattage/voltage compatibility with your specific unit) — this makes them a simpler alternative to building your own solar chain in Method 3.
5. They are quiet and produce no exhaust, so they are the only generator-type device safe to use indoors — a major safety advantage over fuel generators.
6. Know the unit's maximum continuous watts and surge watts before plugging anything in — a small fridge's starting surge can exceed what a modest power station can deliver even if its "running" wattage looks fine on paper.

## Method 7: Fuel-powered generators

1. **Size the generator to your priority list, not your whole house.** Add up the running watts of what you actually need (fridge ~100–200W running / ~800–1200W surge, a few lights ~60W, phone chargers ~20W, a window AC unit ~1000W running / ~2200W surge). A 2,000–3,000W generator covers most single-family "essentials only" setups; running a central AC or well pump usually requires a much larger unit sized by an electrician.
2. **Install and use a transfer switch, or use the generator only for direct cord-plugged devices — never plug a generator into a wall outlet.** A transfer switch is installed by a licensed electrician and physically disconnects your house from the grid before connecting the generator, making backfeeding impossible. See Safety section — this is the single most important rule in this entire guide.
3. **Place the generator outdoors, at least 20 feet from any window, door, or vent, pointed so exhaust blows away from the house** — never in a garage, breezeway, or under a covered porch attached to the house, even with the garage door open.
4. **Start it, let it stabilize, then plug in your highest-surge device first** (like the fridge), let it start and settle, then add other loads one at a time — don't plug everything in simultaneously.
5. **Refuel only after shutting the generator down and letting it cool** (typically 10+ minutes) — spilling fuel on a hot engine or muffler can cause a fire. Store gasoline in approved containers away from living space, and add fuel stabilizer if it will sit for more than a month.
6. **Run a CO detector inside the house whenever the generator is running**, regardless of how far away you placed it — wind can shift, and CO can still migrate indoors.
7. Keep the generator dry — never operate in rain or standing water; use a generator-rated canopy or tent if needed, not a tarp draped directly over a hot engine.

## Method 8: Lighting without power

1. **LED headlamps and lanterns are the safest first choice** — no flame, low battery draw, hands-free, and widely rated for many hours per battery set. Keep spare batteries with each light, stored separately so they don't drain in storage.
2. Stage lights by room: one lantern for a common area, one headlamp per person for moving around, rather than one light per person sitting idle.
3. **Glow sticks** are a safe backup for children's rooms or as wayfinding markers — zero fire or CO risk.
4. **Candles**: only use in a stable holder, on a non-flammable surface, away from curtains/bedding, never left unattended or burning while sleeping, and kept away from kids and pets. Extinguish fully before leaving the room.
5. **Oil lamps/kerosene lamps**: use only in a well-ventilated space (they consume oxygen and produce some CO and soot), keep away from flammable material, never refuel while lit or hot, and never use as the sole heat source in a closed room.
6. Test all battery-powered lights and rotate/replace batteries on a schedule (e.g., every 6 months, matched to smoke-detector battery changes) so they work when you need them.

## Method 9: Keeping food cold and a phone alive together

1. **Fridge/freezer first rule: keep doors closed.** Group items so you open the door once to grab several things, not repeatedly.
2. If the outage may run long, consolidate: move freezer items into a cooler with ice/ice packs, and let the freezer run near-empty (or fully off) rather than half-cold — a full freezer holds cold far better than a half-empty one, so consolidating into fewer, fuller containers helps.
3. A thermometer in the fridge/freezer removes guesswork — if the freezer stays at or below 40°F (4°C), food is generally still safe; when in doubt, throw it out, especially meat, dairy, and leftovers.
4. Use any available power source's spare capacity efficiently: run the fridge on a generator or power station in short scheduled sessions (e.g., 1–2 hours every few hours) rather than continuously, if capacity is tight — a well-insulated fridge holds temperature between sessions.
5. Keep a phone charging plan separate from the food-cold plan — don't let a big appliance load starve your ability to charge communication devices. Charge phones/power banks first thing when any generator or solar surplus is available, then run the fridge.
6. A cooler with ice, kept in a shaded/cool spot, is a valid backup for a day or two of food safety with zero power draw at all — don't overlook it just because it feels "low-tech."

## Regional & scenario variants

**North Texas — summer AC demand outages:** Texas summers regularly push electricity demand to the grid's limits, and rolling blackouts or brownouts can hit on the hottest days — exactly when you need power least available and heat risk is highest. Prioritize cooling people over cooling food: a battery fan on a vulnerable person matters more than the freezer for a few hours. Keep vehicles fueled and phones charged before predicted high-demand days (grid operators often issue advance conservation appeals); pre-charge all power banks and power stations when a heat-driven grid alert is announced, since the outage risk is forecastable, unlike most other causes.

**North Texas — February 2021 winter grid failure (Winter Storm Uri):** A multi-day, statewide grid failure occurred during extreme cold, with outages lasting days for millions, in a region where home heating is often electric and homes are not built for sustained sub-freezing temperatures. Lessons from that event: (1) generators sold out fast — buy and test equipment before winter, not during a storm watch; (2) CO deaths spiked because people ran generators and grills indoors or in garages trying to stay warm — never do this, use layered clothing/blankets and gather in one small room instead; (3) cell networks and cell towers also lost power over multi-day outages — a charged battery bank and a car became critical just to check emergency alerts; (4) burst pipes from prolonged cold compounded the power problem — if you lose heat for an extended period, also shut off and drain exposed plumbing (see the water topic guide); (5) rolling outages were unpredictable and sometimes lasted far longer than utilities announced — plan for multi-day, not multi-hour, when a winter storm warning covers your area.

**Urban apartment/condo:** Fuel generators are often unsafe or against building rules due to poor ventilation and shared air systems — prioritize portable power stations, solar (balcony panel where allowed), and power banks. Confirm you cannot use a generator on a balcony or breezeway; CO can travel between units through shared ventilation.

**Suburban single-family home:** Best candidate for a properly installed transfer switch and a mid-size fuel generator, plus rooftop or ground-mounted solar if already installed. Confirm generator placement respects the 20-foot/downwind rule relative to your own windows and your neighbors' windows.

**Rural/off-grid:** Solar with a larger battery bank is often the primary system rather than a backup; fuel generators serve as backup-to-the-backup. Keep spare fuel stored safely and rotated, since resupply may be far away or delayed after a regional disaster.

## ⚠️ Safety

- **Carbon monoxide (CO) is the single greatest risk in this entire topic.** CO is colorless, odorless, and can kill in minutes at high concentrations. NEVER run a generator, grill, camp stove, or any fuel engine indoors, in a garage (even with the door open), in a tent, or in a breezeway/covered porch attached to the house. Place fuel generators outdoors, at least 20 feet from any window, door, or vent, and pointed so exhaust blows away from the structure. Install battery-backed CO detectors on every level of the home and never disable them. Early CO poisoning symptoms — headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion — are easy to mistake for the flu or exhaustion; if anyone feels this way while fuel equipment is running anywhere nearby, get everyone into fresh air immediately and do not go back in until the source is off and the space is ventilated.
- **Never backfeed a generator into house wiring** (plugging it into a wall outlet or the breaker box without a listed transfer switch). This can send lethal voltage back down the line the utility believes is dead, killing line workers restoring power, and it can also overload/burn your home's wiring. Only connect a generator through (a) a licensed-electrician-installed transfer switch, or (b) direct cords plugged straight into the generator's own outlets.
- **Electrical shock and fire risk:** Keep generators and cords dry; use only outdoor-rated, properly gauged extension cords; don't overload a single cord or outlet strip; inspect cords for damage before each use; keep all electrical equipment away from standing water and never touch it with wet hands.
- **Battery gas and acid hazards:** Lead-acid batteries (including car and many deep-cycle batteries) can vent explosive hydrogen gas, especially while charging — charge in a ventilated area away from open flame or sparks. Never short the terminals. If a battery is cracked, swollen, leaking, or smells strongly of sulfur/rotten eggs, ventilate the area, avoid sparks and flame, and handle it as damaged (acid can burn skin/eyes — flush with water and seek care if exposed). Lithium batteries (power banks, power stations, EV-style packs) can enter thermal runaway if punctured, crushed, overcharged with an incompatible charger, or exposed to high heat — stop using and isolate outdoors/away from flammables any battery that becomes hot, swells, or smokes.
- **Fuel storage and refueling:** Store gasoline only in approved, labeled containers, away from living spaces and ignition sources. Never refuel a running or hot generator — shut it down and let it cool first. Add fuel stabilizer for fuel stored more than a few weeks.
- **Generators are loud and produce hot surfaces** — keep children and pets away, and be mindful of noise curfews and neighbors, especially at night.
- **Surge current can damage equipment or trip breakers** — always plug in your highest-draw device first and add loads one at a time; never assume "running watts" on a label covers the startup surge.

## Troubleshooting

- **Phone won't charge from a power bank:** Check the cable (a worn or "charge-only" cable won't transfer enough current); confirm the bank itself has charge (many have an LED/percentage indicator); try a different port on the bank; some banks won't output at all below a minimum charge threshold — recharge the bank first if possible.
- **Solar panel producing little or no charge:** Check for shading (even a small shadow can cut output drastically on some panel types), confirm connections at the charge controller are tight and correct polarity, clean dust/debris off the panel face, and re-angle toward the sun — output changes significantly with the sun's position through the day.
- **Charge controller shows an error or won't charge the battery:** Confirm the battery voltage matches the controller's setting/type (e.g., lead-acid vs. lithium profiles differ), check that the battery isn't fully dead beyond the controller's wake-up threshold, and check fuses inline if the kit has them.
- **Inverter won't turn on or trips repeatedly:** Confirm the battery has charge and connections are tight; check that connected load doesn't exceed the inverter's rating, especially surge devices; let the inverter cool if it has shut down from overheating.
- **Generator won't start:** Check fuel level and that the fuel valve/petcock is open; check the choke setting for a cold start; check the oil level (many small generators have a low-oil safety cutoff that prevents starting); check that the spark plug isn't fouled if the unit has sat unused for a long time; confirm the on/off switch and any circuit breaker on the unit itself are set to "on."
- **Generator running but no power at outlets:** Check the generator's own breaker/reset switch; confirm cords are fully seated; verify you haven't exceeded the unit's rated wattage, which can trip an internal breaker without stopping the engine.
- **Car won't provide power through the 12V outlet:** Check the outlet's fuse (often a small automotive fuse under the dash or in the fuse box, cheap and easy to replace); confirm the accessory/ignition is in the correct position for that outlet to be powered.
- **Food safety uncertain after an outage:** Use a thermometer, not touch or smell, as the first check; when a fridge/freezer has been above 40°F (4°C) for more than about 2 hours, discard perishable items — when in doubt, throw it out.

## Quick-reference checklist

- [ ] Conserve first: unplug non-essentials, dim/lower-power-mode all screens, keep fridge/freezer closed
- [ ] Phone(s) charged and in low-power mode; corded landline phone plugged in if available
- [ ] Power banks charged and rationed (short charge bursts, airplane mode while charging)
- [ ] Know your battery math: watts x hours = watt-hours; Ah x volts = watt-hours
- [ ] Solar chain understood and connected in order: panel → charge controller → battery → inverter/USB
- [ ] Car used only outdoors/well-ventilated for charging, in short sessions, engine off between
- [ ] Portable power station charged, size-matched to your priority load list
- [ ] Generator sized to actual essential loads (running + surge watts), never plugged into a wall outlet, transfer switch installed by an electrician if used regularly
- [ ] Generator placed ≥20 ft from windows/doors/vents, outdoors, exhaust pointed away
- [ ] CO detector installed, battery-backed, tested, and never disabled
- [ ] Backup lighting staged (headlamp per person, one lantern per common area), spare batteries on hand
- [ ] Candles/oil lamps used only in stable holders, ventilated, never unattended or left burning overnight
- [ ] Fridge/freezer thermometer in place; consolidate food into fewer full containers if outage is prolonged
- [ ] Fuel stored safely, stabilized, and never added to a running or hot generator
- [ ] North Texas households: pre-charge everything ahead of forecast heat-demand alerts or winter storm warnings; know your one-small-room cold-weather gathering plan; never run a generator or grill indoors to stay warm
