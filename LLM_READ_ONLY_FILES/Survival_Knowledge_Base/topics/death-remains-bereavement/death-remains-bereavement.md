# Death, Human Remains & Bereavement in Prolonged Emergencies

## Read this first (the 30-second version)
1. **Always try to reach professional help first — every time.** In normal circumstances, and even in most emergencies, you should call 911, the sheriff/police, a Justice of the Peace, a medical examiner/coroner, or a funeral home the moment you can reach one. Confirming that someone has died, and investigating how, is normally a professional's job, not yours. Everything below is only for the narrow situation where communications and services are genuinely down for an extended period and no one is reachable — not a substitute for calling for help.
2. **Any death that is sudden, violent, unexplained, or involves more than one person at once still needs professional investigation as soon as it is possible to reach anyone** — keep trying to get word out even while you do the temporary steps below.
3. **Contrary to a common and persistent myth, dead bodies from a disaster do not generally cause epidemics.** The World Health Organization and the Pan American Health Organization (WHO/PAHO) are explicit on this point: victims of trauma, drowning, fire, or structural collapse do not carry epidemic diseases like cholera or typhoid simply by having died. You do **not** need to fear the general public catching a disease from a body in your home or yard. Real, smaller-scale precautions still matter for the people directly **handling** remains (see below).
4. **Do not rush to bury or dispose of remains "to be safe."** Hasty burial is not medically necessary and can make it impossible to identify the person later, which causes lasting harm to the family and can have legal consequences. Instead: keep the person cool and covered, protect them from animals, choose and clearly mark a temporary location, and **record who, when, and where** before you do anything permanent.
5. **Protect yourself while caring for the deceased**: gloves, hand hygiene, and avoiding direct contact with body fluids are the real precautions that matter — not masks, bleach baths, or fear of the body itself.
6. **Grief is not a medical problem to fix — it is something to be present for.** Acknowledge the loss honestly with children as well as adults, allow simple rituals, and get connected to mental-health support the moment it's reachable. See the [Psychological First Aid guide](../psychological-first-aid/psychological-first-aid.md) for the deeper grief-support content this guide only summarizes.

This guide exists for the worst version of a long emergency — the kind where roads are out, phones are dead, and the people who normally handle this (paramedics, sheriff's deputies, a Justice of the Peace, a funeral home, clergy) cannot get to you for days. It is written so a person with no training can act with care, safety, and respect until real help arrives — never as a reason to skip calling for that help.

---

## Why this matters
Most people will never have to think about this until the moment they must. In a prolonged disaster — a multi-day flood, an ice storm that blocks every road, a widescale grid failure — someone may die in your household or your neighborhood before any official can reach you to confirm it, investigate it, or take the person into professional care. Doing nothing is not an option (out of basic respect for the person and the practical needs of the household), but doing the wrong thing out of fear or misinformation causes real, lasting harm: hasty burial destroys the chance to ever identify a person correctly, mishandling can spread easy-to-avoid infections to the people caring for a body, and treating death as something to hide from children or handle in cold silence deepens trauma that could have been softened. This guide's job is narrow and specific: keep you safe, keep the person's identity and dignity intact for their family and for the record, and keep you and your household emotionally functioning — until the professionals whose job this normally is can take over.

## Key terms (plain language)
- **Remains / the deceased** — the body of a person who has died. This guide avoids clinical or graphic language on purpose.
- **Decedent** — the formal/legal term for a person who has died; you'll see it on paperwork and hear it from officials.
- **Confirming/pronouncing death** — the formal medical act of determining that death has occurred and, often, its likely cause. **This is normally done by a paramedic, physician, medical examiner, or coroner** — not a bystander. In many rural Texas counties **without a medical examiner's office**, a **Justice of the Peace (JP)** performs this role instead, and can order an inquest if the death looks unnatural. **Collin County is not one of these** — it has had its own Medical Examiner's Office (headquartered in McKinney) since 1986, covering the entire county including Anna, so a JP inquest does not apply there.
- **Medical examiner / coroner** — the official (often a physician, sometimes a JP in Texas) responsible for investigating a death, especially any death that is sudden, violent, unexplained, or unattended by a doctor.
- **Suspicious or unexplained death** — any death that isn't a clearly expected, natural passing under a doctor's ongoing care (examples: injury, violence, unknown cause, found deceased with no known illness, a child, or any death you cannot confidently explain). These **always** require police/medical-examiner involvement the moment it is possible — never write these off as something to just handle yourself.
- **Decomposition** — the natural breakdown of the body after death; it speeds up sharply in heat and humidity (relevant in North Texas summers) and slows sharply in cold.
- **Personal effects** — the person's belongings found with them (ID, jewelry, clothing, phone) — keep these with a record of the person, not scattered or discarded.
- **Chain of custody / provenance** — a simple, honest record of who found the person, when, where, what was done, and by whom — this is what lets authorities and family trust what happened later.
- **PPE (personal protective equipment)** — here, simply disposable or washable gloves, and ideally eye protection if fluids are present; this guide does not require anything more specialized.
- **Bereavement / mourning** — the process of grieving and adjusting after a loss; see the [Psychological First Aid guide](../psychological-first-aid/psychological-first-aid.md) for depth on supporting yourself and others through it.

# PART 1 — When someone has died: what still requires a professional, and when

**The default answer is always: try to reach professional help.** This section exists to be clear-eyed about the narrow set of circumstances where that is genuinely impossible for a period of time, and what still absolutely cannot be skipped even then.

## 1A. Keep trying to reach someone — this never stops being step one
- Call **911** if any phone service works at all, even a text-only signal or a single bar that comes and goes.
- If phones are fully down, use any working radio, a neighbor with a working vehicle, or a physically-delivered message to reach a sheriff's deputy, fire station, hospital, or Justice of the Peace. See the [Communication guide](../communication/communication.md) for options when networks are down.
- **Keep trying at intervals**, not just once — conditions (a repaired cell tower, a cleared road, a passing patrol) can change without warning.

## 1B. Confirming death is normally a professional's role — proceed with humility
It is easy to be certain in the moment and wrong. Only trained responders reliably distinguish death from a person who is unconscious, in deep shock, severely hypothermic, or in another state that can look like death but is not. **If there is any doubt at all and you have any way to get emergency medical guidance (even a brief radio or phone contact), get it before assuming death and stopping any care.** The [First Aid guide](../first-aid/first-aid.md) covers checking responsiveness, breathing, and pulse, and when to begin CPR rather than assume the worst.

Signs that are consistent with death having occurred some time ago (not a checklist to "confirm" it yourself, but useful for judgment) include: no response to voice or pain, no breathing or pulse found after a careful check, and the body cooling to match the surrounding temperature. When these are clearly and unambiguously present and the person was already expected to be near death (for example, under hospice-type comfort care for a known terminal illness), it is reasonable to move to the steps in this guide while continuing to try to reach help. **When there is any doubt, treat it as a medical emergency and attempt resuscitation per the First Aid guide.**

## 1C. Deaths that ALWAYS require police/medical-examiner involvement — never quietly handle these alone
Some deaths must be investigated by authorities as soon as it is humanly possible to reach them, no matter how long that takes. **Do not disturb the scene more than necessary for safety and basic dignity, and preserve everything you can for that investigation** (see Part 6). This includes any death that is:
- Sudden and unexplained, with no known illness or ongoing medical condition.
- The result of injury, violence, a weapon, a fall, drowning, fire, or an accident.
- A child's death, at any time, for any reason.
- Any death where you are not fully confident of the cause.
- More than one person dying at or near the same time (a mass-casualty situation — see the [Emergency Planning & Triage guide](../emergency-planning-triage/emergency-planning-triage.md)).

> ⚠️ These are exactly the cases where authorities have both a legal duty and the expertise to determine what happened — for the family's peace of mind, for public safety, and, in the case of anything criminal, for justice. Nothing in this guide substitutes for that. The temporary steps in Parts 3–5 are about dignity and safety while you wait, not about making a determination yourself.

## 1D. What "authorities are truly unreachable" means
This guide's later sections apply only when **all** of the following are true: you have made a genuine, repeated effort to reach 911, a sheriff's office, a hospital, a Justice of the Peace, or any other emergency responder; days (not hours) have passed with no way to reach anyone; and the situation (storm, flood, grid failure, blocked roads) is one where you can reasonably expect this to be temporary. It does not mean "inconvenient to call" or "I'd rather handle it myself."

# PART 2 — The epidemic myth, and the real precautions that matter

## 2A. What the evidence actually shows
A widespread and understandable fear after a disaster is that dead bodies will cause disease outbreaks among survivors. **WHO and PAHO's joint field guidance for first responders is direct on this point: dead bodies from natural disasters do not generally cause epidemics.** People killed by trauma, drowning, fire, or structural collapse are not, by virtue of dying that way, carrying epidemic diseases like cholera, typhoid, or plague — and the organisms that do cause those diseases do not survive long in a body after death. The **real** post-disaster disease risk to the wider public comes overwhelmingly from **contaminated water and crowded, displaced **living** populations** — see the [Water guide](../water/water.md) and [Sanitation & Hygiene guide](../sanitation-hygiene/sanitation-hygiene.md) for the precautions that actually matter at that scale.

This matters practically: **it means you do not need to panic, evacuate an area, or treat a body in your home as a public-health emergency for the neighborhood.** It does mean the temptation to bury or dispose of remains hastily "for safety" is based on a myth — and doing so trades an imagined public-health benefit for a real, permanent loss (the ability to identify the person and give the family answers).

## 2B. The real, smaller-scale precautions — for people directly handling remains
The myth does not mean "no precautions." A body can carry the same ordinary bloodborne and fecal-oral organisms a living sick person could (tuberculosis, hepatitis B and C, HIV, and diarrheal-disease organisms like shigella and salmonella), and per PAHO guidance most of these do not persist more than about **two days** in a body kept at ordinary (ambient) temperature. **Tuberculosis (TB) is an exception and does not belong in that "~2 days" bucket** — TB bacteria can persist in cadaver lung tissue for **weeks**, and can become airborne (aerosolized) if the chest is moved, compressed, or manipulated (for example, during moving, wrapping, or CPR attempted before death was clearly established). Handle the chest/torso gently, avoid unnecessary pressure on it, and prefer a well-ventilated space with a simple mask if you know or suspect the person had active TB. **HIV can persist longer** — PAHO notes it can remain active for roughly up to **six days** at ambient conditions, and considerably longer (roughly **6–15 days**) in a body kept refrigerated near 2°C (36°F), which is one more reason cool storage (Part 3A) helps rather than hurts. Ordinary flu viruses, by contrast, survive only about **one day** in the environment. The risk is centered on **direct contact with blood or body fluids**, not on being in the same room or the same house — and specifically on a **break in your own skin** (a cut, a needle-stick, a scratch) that lets fluid in. To put that in perspective, the estimated risk of infection from a single accidental skin-breaking exposure to infected blood is roughly **6–30% for hepatitis B** and roughly **0.3–0.5% for HIV** — hepatitis B is the more transmissible of the two this way, which is exactly why "cover your own cuts, glove up, and don't let sharp objects near your skin" matters far more than avoiding the room the person is in.

**You need:**
- Disposable gloves (nitrile/latex) — improvised substitute: clean plastic bags secured over the hands, or the cleanest cloth gloves you have, washed thoroughly afterward.
- Soap and water for handwashing, or alcohol-based hand sanitizer if water is scarce (see the [Sanitation & Hygiene guide](../sanitation-hygiene/sanitation-hygiene.md) for handwashing without running water).
- Eye protection if there are any leaking fluids or open wounds — improvised substitute: sunglasses, safety glasses, or a clear plastic sheet held as a shield.
- A well-ventilated space; masks are **not** required for ordinary handling on health grounds (per PAHO), though a simple cloth over the nose and mouth can help with odor and is fine to use.

**Steps:**
1. **Wash your hands before putting gloves on and immediately after removing them** — this single habit does more than any other precaution.
2. **Avoid direct skin contact with blood or other body fluids.** If the person has visible leaking fluids, numerous open wounds, or died of a known transmissible illness (cholera or a viral hemorrhagic fever such as Ebola or Marburg are the specific exceptions PAHO and CDC flag by name — these are the diseases where extra caution around fluids is genuinely warranted, not the ordinary trauma/drowning/collapse deaths this guide mostly addresses), step up to **double gloves, eye protection (goggles or a face shield), and a simple mask**, and handle the body as little as possible — wrap it fully (see Part 3) before moving it. This heightened-precaution combination (double gloves + eye protection + mask) is PAHO's specific recommendation for exactly this narrower set of cases; it is not needed for an ordinary disaster death. **For cholera or a viral hemorrhagic fever specifically, CDC directives add two firm rules: do not wash or clean the body, and do not reopen the wrapping once it has been sealed** — both actions risk exposing handlers to infectious fluids for no benefit.
3. **Cover any cut or broken skin on your own hands with a bandage before gloving up**, and never handle remains barefoot or with bare hands if you can avoid it.
4. **Do not eat, drink, smoke, or touch your face while handling remains or before washing your hands.**
5. **After finishing, remove gloves inside-out (so contamination stays on the inside), dispose of them or wash reusable ones with soap and water, and wash your hands thoroughly with soap and water for at least 20 seconds** (about two rounds of humming "Happy Birthday"), **or use an alcohol-based hand sanitizer with at least 60% alcohol if soap and water aren't available** — see handwashing technique in the [Sanitation & Hygiene guide](../sanitation-hygiene/sanitation-hygiene.md).
6. Any tools, stretchers, tarps, or surfaces used should be **washed with soap and water afterward**; a diluted household bleach solution (see the dilution table in the Water guide's disinfection section) can be used on hard surfaces if available, though it is not required to prevent an epidemic — it is simply good practice.

> ⚠️ If you have any open wound, cut, or broken skin on your hands or arms, or if you are pregnant, immunocompromised, or otherwise more vulnerable, ask someone else in the household to handle direct contact instead, or use extra layers of protection.

# PART 3 — Temporary, respectful management of remains while authorities are delayed

The goal here is threefold, in this order: **protect the dignity of the person, protect the people around them (safety and reasonable sanitation), and preserve the ability to identify them and investigate the death later.** Nothing here is a substitute for the professional handling that should happen as soon as it can.

## Method A — Keep the person cool, if at all possible
Heat sharply speeds decomposition, which is both distressing for the family and can make later identification harder.
1. Move the person (if it is safe and appropriate to do so, and not a scene that must be preserved for investigation — see Part 1C) to the coolest available indoor space out of direct sun: a basement, an interior room away from windows, or the coolest room in the house.
2. If ice or a working refrigeration source (a chest freezer, ice from a cooler) is available, ice packs or bagged ice placed near — not directly on exposed skin of — the torso can measurably slow the process. This is the same principle as commercial cooling but at a much smaller scale; do not expect it to fully stop decomposition, especially in North Texas summer heat.
3. If no cooling is possible, prioritize the covering and ventilation steps below and accept that decomposition will proceed faster — this is a natural process and not a failure on your part.

## Method B — Cover, and protect from insects and animals
1. Cover the person fully and respectfully with a clean sheet, blanket, or tarp.
2. If flies or insects are a concern (very likely in North Texas heat), a fine mesh, mosquito netting, or a second layer of light cloth over the covering helps, along with keeping doors/windows in that room closed if feasible.
3. If the person must remain outdoors or in an outbuilding, take real precautions against animals: a fully-enclosed structure (shed, vehicle with windows up), weighted-down tarping, or physical barriers. This is both a safety matter (a body should never attract wildlife into an inhabited area) and a matter of basic dignity.

## Method C — Choose and clearly mark a temporary location
1. **Choose a temporary location that is not a hasty grave.** A sheltered outbuilding, garage, unused room, or a shaded outdoor spot with reliable weighted covering are all reasonable temporary choices. The point of "temporary" is that it should be **easy for authorities to find and recover the person later**, without excavation.
2. If genuinely no other option exists after multiple days (extreme heat, no cooling at all, and authorities confirmed to be many more days away), and a **temporary, shallow, marked** placement in soil is the only remaining option to manage odor and decomposition, treat it explicitly as **temporary and reversible, not final burial**:
   - Choose ground **away from any water source** (wells, ponds, creeks — at least 100 feet/30 meters, more if the ground slopes toward water) to protect that water supply, matching the same siting logic used for latrines in the [Sanitation & Hygiene guide](../sanitation-hygiene/sanitation-hygiene.md).
   - Keep it as **shallow as practical while still covering the person**, wrapped in a sheet or tarp, in a single, clearly recorded spot — not scattered, not commingled with another person, and never in a location that will be disturbed by planned digging, farming, or construction.
   - **Mark the location durably and visibly**: a sturdy stake, a cairn of stones, GPS coordinates written down if you have any device that captured them, and a note of the exact location relative to fixed landmarks (e.g., "40 paces due north of the old barn's northeast corner").
   - This step should be treated as a last resort taken only after real necessity, not a default — and it is explicitly **not** the same as a funeral or final burial, which should follow once authorities and the family are able to be involved.
3. **Never** cremate, dispose of remains in a way that destroys the possibility of identification, or bury more than one person in the same undocumented spot.

> ⚠️ The single most damaging mistake in this entire topic is treating a temporary, forced decision as if it were final and undocumented. A rushed, unmarked, unrecorded burial can permanently take away a family's ability to ever recover, identify, or properly bury their loved one — this has real legal consequences and lasting grief consequences. Slow down, document (Part 4), and mark the location clearly, even under pressure.

# PART 4 — Identification and documentation

Good documentation is a gift to the family and to the authorities who will eventually take over — it is often the only way a person can be identified with confidence later, especially if conditions (heat, time) change how they look.

**Record, in writing, as much of the following as you can:**

| What to record | Notes |
|---|---|
| Full name, if known | Include nicknames or how others knew them if the legal name is uncertain. |
| Date and approximate time found/died | Best estimate is fine — write "approx." rather than guessing precisely. |
| Exact location found | Address, room, or landmark-relative description; GPS coordinates if available. |
| Physical description | Approximate age, height, build, hair color, any distinguishing marks (scars, tattoos) — described plainly and respectfully, not graphically. |
| Clothing and personal effects | List everything found with the person: clothing, jewelry, wallet/ID, phone, keys. Keep these items **together with the person or with this record**, in a labeled bag — do not distribute or discard them. |
| Photographs, if you have any camera/phone | A respectful photograph of the person's face (if intact and appropriate) and of any identifying items or marks is one of the most valuable things you can provide to authorities later. Photograph the temporary location and its markers too. |
| Who found them, and who is recording this | Your name, and anyone else present. |
| Apparent cause, if known | Only what you actually know (e.g., "was under hospice care for cancer," "found after the flood receded") — do not speculate or guess at a cause you cannot support. |
| Names and contact info of next of kin | So authorities can notify or coordinate with family as soon as they are reachable. |

Keep this written record in a safe, dry place (a sealed bag or folder), and keep a mental or written note of where it is. If more than one person has died, use a **separate record for each person** and keep their personal effects separately labeled — never combine them.

**If more than one person has died, use a simple numbered-tag system** — this is the same basic principle disaster-response teams use when no forensic experts are on scene yet, per the WHO/PAHO/ICRC/IFRC field manual: assign each person a number (1, 2, 3…) the moment you begin documenting them, and write that same number on **three matching places**: a tag or piece of tape attached to the person (e.g., tied to a wrist or toe, or pinned to the covering), the bag or container holding their personal effects, and the top of their written record sheet. This one habit is what prevents a name, a record, and a set of belongings from ever being separated or mixed up between people — especially important if conditions change (someone else finds them, you're exhausted, or days pass) before authorities arrive.

# PART 5 — Contacting authorities once communications return

1. **Report as soon as any contact is possible** — 911, sheriff's non-emergency line, a Justice of the Peace, or the nearest open hospital or fire station. Do not wait for a "convenient" moment.
2. **What to report:** the person's name (if known), when and where they were found, what temporary steps you took (cooling, covering, location, any movement of the body), and that you have a written record and any personal effects/photographs ready to hand over.
3. **Preserve the site as much as possible** until authorities can respond, especially for any death in the "always requires investigation" category from Part 1C — avoid unnecessary additional handling, cleaning, or moving beyond what dignity and basic safety required.
4. **Hand over your documentation and personal effects intact.** This is often what allows a medical examiner or Justice of the Peace to complete a death certificate and what allows a family to eventually receive their loved one's belongings.
5. Expect that in a large-scale, multi-fatality disaster, authorities may be overwhelmed and slower than you'd like even once reachable — your careful documentation is what lets the system catch up correctly rather than losing track of who is who.

# PART 6 — Grief and family support

This guide covers only the practical minimum here; for real depth on supporting yourself, other adults, and children through grief and trauma, use the [Psychological First Aid guide](../psychological-first-aid/psychological-first-aid.md), which this section deliberately does not duplicate.

## The essentials
1. **Acknowledge the loss plainly and honestly**, with adults and children alike. Avoid euphemisms with children ("gone to sleep," "went away") that can confuse or frighten them more than clear, gentle honesty ("Grandpa's body stopped working and he died; he's not in pain, and it's okay to be sad").
2. **Let children see calm, and let them grieve in their own way** — through questions, play, drawing, or silence. They do not need to be shielded from the fact of the death, only from unnecessary graphic detail. See the Psychological First Aid guide's children-in-crisis section for more.
   - **Simple, concrete phrases that help**, per SAMHSA's guidance for supporting a grieving child: *"People will still take care of you." "Everything you're feeling is okay." "It's okay to be sad, mad, or scared." "We can talk about anything you want." "You can ask me any questions."* Saying these plainly, and meaning them, does more than any elaborate explanation.
3. **There is no fixed timeline or "correct" way to grieve.** Sadness, anger, numbness, guilt, and even moments of relief or laughter can all show up in the same person within the same hour — all of this is normal.
4. **A simple, sincere ritual helps**, even improvised: a moment of silence, sharing a memory or a favorite story about the person, saying a few words at the temporary location, lighting a candle if you have one, or observing a familiar religious or cultural practice if you can. Humans across every culture use ritual to mark and process loss — a small one, done sincerely, is far better than none.
5. **Watch for grief that stops a person from functioning at all** — not eating or drinking, not sleeping at all, or talking about wanting to die or join the deceased. This has crossed from ordinary grief into a safety concern; see the Safety section of the [Psychological First Aid guide](../psychological-first-aid/psychological-first-aid.md) for the warning signs and what to do.
6. **Caregivers need care too.** Whoever handled the practical steps in this guide is carrying real weight — make space for them to rest, talk, and grieve as well; do not expect the person who "handled things" to also be unaffected by them.
7. **When mental-health or spiritual-care professionals become reachable, use them** — grief counseling, clergy, and community support are not luxuries; reconnecting with them should happen as soon as it is possible. Once phone service returns, **SAMHSA's National Helpline, 1-800-662-4357 (free, confidential, 24/7)**, can connect anyone — adult or child, in crisis or just needing to talk — to local mental-health and grief support; it does not require an emergency to call.

# North Texas / regional notes (Anna, TX & Collin County)
- **Heat accelerates everything.** North Texas summers regularly exceed 95–100°F (35–38°C); decomposition proceeds much faster than in cooler climates, and cooling/covering (Parts 3A–3B) become more urgent, not optional. Plan for this reality rather than being caught off guard by it.
- **Collin County has its own Medical Examiner's Office**, headquartered in McKinney, which has served the entire county — including Anna — since 1986; a Justice of the Peace (JP) does **not** perform death investigation here. (JP inquest is still the norm in many other, more rural Texas counties that don't have a medical examiner — a normal, legitimate part of the Texas legal system elsewhere, just not the one that applies locally.) Keep trying to reach the Collin County Medical Examiner's Office, a sheriff's deputy, or 911 dispatcher just as you would anywhere else.
- **Flooding and tornadoes** are this region's most likely mass-casualty triggers (flash floods along the East Fork Trinity and its creeks, tornado outbreaks in spring); in a multi-fatality event, prioritize the documentation habits in Part 4 for each person separately, and see the [Emergency Planning & Triage guide](../emergency-planning-triage/emergency-planning-triage.md) for triage-of-attention principles that also apply here.
- **Rural properties often have stock ponds, wells, and septic fields** — if a temporary ground placement (Part 3C, last resort only) is ever truly necessary, the distance-from-water siting rule matters even more here, since many neighbors rely on the same shallow groundwater.
- **Grid-down summer heat also endangers the living** — do not let the emotional weight of a death cause you to neglect hydration and heat safety for the rest of the household; see the [Extreme Heat & Cold guide](../extreme-heat-cold/extreme-heat-cold.md).

# ⚠️ Safety — the most important warnings
- **Always attempt to reach professional help first, and keep attempting it.** Nothing in this guide is a reason to stop trying, and it does not apply until authorities are genuinely, repeatedly unreachable for an extended period.
- **Any sudden, violent, unexplained, or child death must be investigated by authorities as soon as humanly possible** — do not treat these as something to quietly resolve yourself, even under this guide's temporary-care steps.
- **If there is any doubt about whether a person has actually died, treat it as a medical emergency** and follow the [First Aid guide](../first-aid/first-aid.md) rather than assuming the worst.
- **Do not fear the general public catching a disease from a body** — WHO/PAHO guidance is clear that this is a myth for ordinary disaster deaths. Do not let this fear drive you to hasty, undocumented disposal.
- **Do not skip hand hygiene and glove use when directly handling remains** — this is the real, evidence-based precaution, especially with any leaking fluid, open wound, or known transmissible illness.
- **Never perform a hasty, unmarked, or undocumented burial.** It is not required for public health, and it can permanently prevent identification and closure for the family — treat any such placement as strictly temporary, shallow, marked, and recorded, and only as an absolute last resort.
- **Never bury or place more than one person in the same unmarked location**, and never mix personal effects between different people.
- **Keep any water source (well, pond, creek) at least 100 feet/30 meters from any temporary ground placement.**
- **Do not force anyone, adult or child, to view remains or "process" grief on a schedule** — presence and honesty matter more than any particular ritual or timeline.

# Troubleshooting
| Problem | Fix |
|---|---|
| No way to reach any authority at all | Keep trying at intervals (conditions change); follow Parts 2–4 in the meantime; document everything for when contact is possible. |
| Unsure whether the person is actually deceased | Treat it as a medical emergency — check responsiveness, breathing, pulse; begin CPR per the First Aid guide if trained and in doubt; do not proceed with this guide's later steps until death is clearly established. |
| Extreme heat and no way to cool the remains | Prioritize covering and insect/animal protection (Method B); accept faster decomposition; document (Part 4) before any change in condition makes identification harder. |
| No gloves or PPE available | Use clean plastic bags over hands, minimize direct contact, wash hands thoroughly with soap and water immediately after, and cover any cuts on your own skin beforehand. |
| Fear that a body will make others sick | This is a documented myth for ordinary disaster deaths (WHO/PAHO) — the real risk is only to people in direct contact with fluids; wash hands and use gloves, but there is no need to evacuate or panic. |
| Pressure to bury quickly "to be safe" | Explain (or apply) that hasty burial is not medically necessary and can prevent identification — use temporary cooling/covering/marking instead, and keep trying to reach authorities. |
| Multiple deaths at once | Document each person completely separately (Part 4); do not commingle remains or effects; prioritize per the Emergency Planning & Triage guide. |
| A grieving person or child stops eating, sleeping, or talks about wanting to die | This is a safety concern beyond ordinary grief — see the Psychological First Aid guide's safety section immediately and get professional help the moment it's reachable. |
| Unsure what to say to a grieving child | Use plain, honest, gentle language; don't force conversation; let them ask questions and grieve in their own way — see the Psychological First Aid guide. |

# Quick-reference checklist
- [ ] Tried, and kept trying, to reach 911, sheriff, hospital, Justice of the Peace, or any responder.
- [ ] Confirmed there is no meaningful doubt that death has occurred (otherwise treated as a medical emergency).
- [ ] Recognized whether this is a death that always requires investigation (sudden, violent, unexplained, or a child) and preserved the scene accordingly.
- [ ] Did NOT assume the body poses a public epidemic risk to others in the household or neighborhood.
- [ ] Used gloves and hand hygiene when directly handling remains; avoided direct contact with body fluids.
- [ ] Kept the person as cool as possible and fully covered; protected from insects and animals.
- [ ] Chose (did not rush) a temporary location; if ground placement was truly unavoidable, kept it shallow, away from water sources, and clearly marked.
- [ ] Recorded identity, date/time, location, description, personal effects, and photographs in writing.
- [ ] Kept personal effects together and labeled with the record; did not discard or mix them between people.
- [ ] Reported to authorities the moment contact was possible, and handed over documentation and effects.
- [ ] Acknowledged the loss honestly with children and adults; allowed a simple ritual; watched for grief that stops someone from functioning.
- [ ] Made space for whoever handled the practical steps to also grieve and rest.
