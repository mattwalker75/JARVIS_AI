# Food Preservation & Cooking Without Power

## Read this first
1. **Two separate jobs:** (A) keep food from going bad without a refrigerator, and (B) cook it without electricity or gas mains. This guide covers both.
2. **The single biggest killer mistake:** canning low-acid food (meat, most vegetables, beans) in a **boiling-water bath** instead of a **pressure canner**. This can grow *Clostridium botulinum*, an invisible, odorless, tasteless toxin that can be fatal in tiny amounts. **If you do not have a pressure canner, do not can low-acid food — dry, salt, smoke, ferment, or freeze/refrigerate it instead.**
3. **Fridge/freezer outage rule:** keep doors shut. A full freezer stays safe about **48 hours** (24 hours half-full); a fridge keeps food safe about **4 hours**. After that, apply the **40°F / 4-hour rule** below to decide what to keep or toss.
4. **Cooking without power is easy and safe once you respect two hazards:** open flame (burns, wildfire) and **carbon monoxide** (any combustion device — charcoal, propane, wood — used indoors or in a garage can kill you silently). Cook fueled fires **outdoors or in a properly vented space only**.
5. **Doneness is a temperature, not a guess.** Use a food thermometer: poultry 165°F, ground meat 160°F, whole cuts of beef/pork/fish 145°F (with a 3-minute rest). These numbers matter more than any recipe.

---

## Why this matters
When the power goes out, your refrigerator and freezer stop working, grocery stores may close, and your stove or oven may not run. Two failures kill people in disasters almost as often as the disaster itself: **foodborne illness** from eating spoiled or improperly preserved food, and **carbon monoxide poisoning** from cooking or heating indoors with combustion devices. Neither requires the disaster to be dramatic — a bad batch of jerky or a charcoal grill run in a closed garage can be just as dangerous as the storm that caused the outage. Humans preserved and cooked food without electricity for thousands of years; the methods below are proven, but they only work if the **temperatures, times, and ratios are followed exactly** — "close enough" is how people get sick.

## Key terms
- **Water activity (Aw)** — how much "free" water is in food for bacteria to use. Drying, salting, and sugaring all work by lowering water activity below the point where most bacteria can grow (below about 0.85).
- **Low-acid food** — pH above 4.6 (most vegetables, meats, poultry, seafood, dairy). Can support *Clostridium botulinum* growth in a sealed, oxygen-free jar — **requires pressure canning**.
- **High-acid food** — pH 4.6 or below (most fruit, pickles with enough added vinegar, jams). Safe for boiling-water-bath canning.
- **Botulism** — a rare but often fatal paralytic illness from a toxin made by *C. botulinum* spores growing in sealed, low-oxygen, low-acid, low-salt conditions (improperly canned food, home-fermented oils, some smoked/vacuum-packed fish). Spores survive boiling; only pressure-canning temperatures (240–250°F) reliably kill them.
- **Curing** — preserving meat/fish with salt (and often nitrite/nitrate) to lower water activity and inhibit bacteria.
- **Cold smoking vs. hot smoking** — cold smoking (below ~90°F) flavors and helps preserve but does **not** cook the food — it must be fully cooked separately or cured first. Hot smoking (225–275°F) both cooks and flavors.
- **Danger zone** — 40°F to 140°F, the temperature range where bacteria multiply fastest. Food should spend as little time in this range as possible.
- **Retained-heat cooking (hay-box)** — bringing food to a boil, then finishing the cooking in a heavily insulated container using only the heat already in the pot — no added fuel.
- **Carbon monoxide (CO)** — an odorless, colorless, poisonous gas produced by any incomplete combustion (charcoal, propane, gas, wood, gasoline generators). Builds up indoors and kills without warning.

## What you need
**For preservation (have some, not all):**
- Sharp knife, cutting board; food-grade containers, canning jars with new lids and bands, a pressure canner (for low-acid canning) or large pot (for water-bath canning of high-acid food only)
- Non-iodized salt (canning/pickling salt), sugar, white vinegar (5% acidity), curing salt if doing a nitrite cure (**Prague Powder #1/Instacure #1** for short, cooked/smoked cures; **Prague Powder #2/Instacure #2** for long, uncooked, dry-aged cures — see Method 4 for ratios; optional for short cures eaten/cooked promptly, but safer)
- Drying racks or screens, cheesecloth to keep off insects, a food thermometer (essential), a hygrometer if available
- Cool, dark storage space (root cellar, basement, buried container, north-facing shaded area)

**For cooking without power:**
- Fire-starting kit (see `../fire/fire.md`), seasoned dry firewood, charcoal, or a propane/camp stove with extra fuel canisters
- Cast-iron dutch oven with a flanged lid (for coal cooking), a metal grate or grill, tongs, welding/leather gloves, a metal poker
- Materials for a rocket stove (bricks, a metal can/pipe) and/or a solar oven (cardboard box, foil, glass/plastic glazing, black pot, insulation)
- A carbon monoxide detector (battery-powered) if cooking anywhere near an enclosed space
- A cooler with ice or frozen gel packs to slow refrigerator losses

# PART A — PRESERVING FOOD WITHOUT REFRIGERATION

## 1. The outage food-safety rules (do this first, before anything else)
1. **Keep the fridge and freezer doors closed as much as possible.** Every opening lets cold air escape.
2. **Freezer:** stays safe about **48 hours** if full, **24 hours** if half-full. Group items together to hold cold longer. Add bags of ice or dry ice if you have them.
3. **Refrigerator:** stays safe about **4 hours** without power.
4. **The 40°F / 4-hour rule:** perishable food (meat, poultry, fish, eggs, milk, leftovers, cut produce) that has been above **40°F for more than 4 hours** must be thrown out — even if it looks and smells fine. Bacteria that cause illness often do not change taste, smell, or appearance.
5. **When power returns (or you regain a thermometer reading):** check each item's temperature.
   - Below 40°F: safe, refrigerate/refreeze/cook.
   - Above 40°F for less than 2 hours: safe to cook and eat right away, but do not refreeze raw.
   - Above 40°F for more than 4 hours: **discard**. When in doubt, throw it out.
6. **Frozen food that still has ice crystals and feels cold (below 40°F) may be safely refrozen**, though quality may suffer.
7. **Discard immediately, no exceptions:** anything with an off odor, color, or texture; any bulging, leaking, or damaged can/jar; any raw meat/poultry/fish/eggs/dairy left unrefrigerated over 4 hours; any food that touched flood water.

## 2. Method 1: Sun and air drying (dehydrating)
Drying removes enough moisture that bacteria, yeast, and mold cannot grow. Works on fruit, vegetables, and herbs; meat drying (jerky) has extra rules below because raw meat is higher-risk.
1. Slice food thin and uniform: fruit/vegetables about 1/4 inch (6 mm) thick.
2. Pretreat fruit if desired (dip in lemon juice/water 1:4 to slow browning) — optional, not a safety step.
3. Lay pieces in a single layer, not touching, on a clean screen or rack raised off the ground for airflow. Cover loosely with cheesecloth or fine mesh to keep off insects and birds — do not let plastic touch the food (traps moisture).
4. **Sun-dry** in hot, dry, breezy weather, turning pieces once or twice a day. Bring inside at night if humidity/dew is high (moisture reabsorbs and can spoil the batch).
5. Typical times: fruit 1–4 days; vegetables 1–2 days; herbs a few hours to 1 day, depending on heat, humidity, and thickness.
6. **Doneness check:** fruit should be leathery and pliable with no visible moisture when cut; vegetables should be brittle/crisp; herbs should crumble. Let cool, then check — warm food feels drier than it is.
7. **Condition** dried fruit before long storage: pack loosely in a jar for 4–7 days, shaking daily; if condensation appears, return to drying — this evens out any remaining moisture so mold doesn't grow in storage.
8. Store in airtight containers, cool and dark. Properly dried and stored fruit/vegetables keep many months to a year.

> Air drying works but is slower and less reliable in humid climates — see the North Texas note below.

## 3. Method 2: Making jerky (dried meat)
Jerky is the classic no-power meat preservation method, but raw meat carries bacteria (*Salmonella*, *E. coli*), so extra steps matter.
1. **Choose lean meat** — trim off all visible fat (fat goes rancid and shortens shelf life). Beef eye of round, venison, or other lean cuts work well.
2. **Slice** 1/8–1/4 inch (3–6 mm) thick, with the grain for chewier jerky or against the grain for a jerky that breaks more easily.
3. **Marinate or dry-cure for flavor and safety.** A basic marinade: 1 cup soy sauce or 1/4 cup salt dissolved in 2 cups water, plus black pepper, garlic, and optional curing salt (follow package directions) — marinate refrigerated 6–24 hours. Curing salt (sodium nitrite) is optional for flavor/color but adds some protection against bacterial growth during the low-temperature drying process.
4. **Pre-heat the meat before drying** (USDA/food-safety recommendation): heat strips to **160°F (71°C)** internal temperature — either simmer briefly in the marinade or steam — before placing them in the dehydrator/oven. This step kills bacteria that low drying temperatures alone may not reliably kill.
5. **Dry at 130–140°F (55–60°C)** in a dehydrator, oven with the door propped open, or in strong sun (see below) until the meat bends and cracks but does not snap, with no moist or shiny spots. (This drying temperature is separate from — and lower than — the 160°F bacteria-kill step in step 4: that step happens once, before drying begins, not throughout the whole dry.) This typically takes **4–8 hours** in a dehydrator/oven, or **1–2+ days** in strong, dry sun with good airflow.
6. **Sun-drying meat** is possible in hot (90°F+), low-humidity, breezy conditions, but is riskier — meat sits in the danger zone longer. Slice thinner, use more salt, cover with mesh, and bring in at night. If in doubt, prefer a fire/oven/dehydrator method for meat.
7. **Doneness check:** bend a cooled piece — it should crack and show no pink or moist fibers, only dry, fibrous meat throughout.
8. Cool completely, then store airtight; refrigerate or freeze for the longest shelf life, or keep in a cool, dark, dry place for a few weeks.

## 4. Method 3: Smoking (cold vs. hot)
Smoking adds flavor and some preservative effect (smoke compounds + drying), but **smoke alone does not make meat safe** — temperature does.
1. **Hot smoking (225–275°F / 107–135°C):** cooks and flavors at the same time. Use for meat, poultry, fish you intend to eat soon or refrigerate/freeze. Cook to the safe internal temperature for that food (poultry 165°F; whole cuts of beef/pork 145°F; ground meat 160°F) as measured with a thermometer, not by time or color. **Fish is a special case:** reach **145°F internal AND hold it there for at least 30 minutes**, in a finished product with **at least 3–3.5% water-phase salt** (salt content of the fish's moisture, from an adequate brine before smoking) — *Clostridium botulinum* type E can grow in smoked fish even under refrigeration, and 145°F alone, without both the 30-minute hold and the salt level, is not considered sufficient.
2. **Cold smoking (below 90°F / 32°C, ideally 70–90°F):** adds flavor and helps dry the surface but does **not** cook the food. Only cold-smoke meat that has **already been fully cured** (salt-cured, see Method 4) or that will be cooked to a safe temperature afterward. Never eat cold-smoked raw meat or fish without curing/cooking — this is a known route to botulism and other illness, especially with fish and low-oxygen storage (vacuum bags).
3. **Basic hot-smoke setup:** a covered grill or barrel with charcoal/wood on one side (indirect heat) and soaked wood chips (hickory, oak, mesquite, apple) added for smoke; food on the cooler side, lid on, vents cracked to maintain 225–275°F. Use a grate thermometer and a meat thermometer — do not guess.
4. Typical hot-smoke times: whole chicken 3–4 hours to 165°F; pork shoulder 8–12+ hours to 195–205°F (for pulling) or 145°F minimum for slicing; fish fillets 1–2 hours to reach 145°F, then hold at 145°F+ for a further 30 minutes (see the fish salt/time note above). Times vary with size and fire temperature — **always finish by internal temperature, not the clock.**
5. Smoked meat intended for longer storage should still be refrigerated, frozen, or further dried/salted — smoking is a flavor and partial-preservation step, not a substitute for proper storage.

## 5. Method 4: Salting and curing
Salt draws out water (lowering water activity) and directly inhibits many bacteria, making it one of the oldest reliable no-power preservation methods.
1. **Dry curing (for meat, e.g., a small ham or bacon slab):** rub/pack the meat with a **plain-salt dry cure of roughly 3–4% of the meat's weight** (for example, about 1.5–2 oz of salt for 3 lb of meat) in a non-reactive container, refrigerated — **not** "a pound of salt per pound of meat," a dangerously wrong ratio that some folk recipes repeat. Follow a tested cure recipe/ratio rather than guessing, since under-curing is a real food-safety risk for anything not eaten soon.
   - **If adding curing salt (nitrite/nitrate) rather than, or alongside, plain salt:** these are potent and measured completely differently from plain salt. **Prague Powder #1 / Instacure #1** (sodium nitrite) is for short-cure, cooked, or refrigerated/smoked items — use roughly **1 level teaspoon per 5 lb of meat**. **Prague Powder #2 / Instacure #2** (sodium nitrite + sodium nitrate) is only for long, uncooked, dry-aged cures lasting weeks to months, where the nitrate slowly converts to nitrite over time. **Never substitute one for the other, and never measure either anywhere close to a 1:1 ratio with meat weight** — follow the package/tested-recipe measurement exactly; too much is toxic, too little under-protects against botulism.
   - Cure time is roughly **1 day per 1/2 inch of thickness plus 2 extra days**, refrigerated the whole time.
2. **Brining/wet curing:** dissolve salt in water until an egg floats — a strong brine, roughly **1 cup of salt per GALLON of water** (not per quart — a common and dangerous mis-scaling). Submerge meat or fish, refrigerate, and brine hours to a few days depending on size/thickness.
3. **After curing,** rinse off excess surface salt, then air-dry, smoke, or cook/refrigerate as appropriate — do not eat cured raw meat straight from the cure unless following a specific tested traditional recipe (e.g., some dry-cured hams) with known safe timelines.
4. **Salting vegetables** (e.g., traditional salt-preserved cabbage, green beans) works similarly and overlaps with fermentation (Method 8).
5. **Verify:** properly salt-cured meat feels firm (not squishy), has no off odor, and has visibly lost volume/moisture compared to raw. When unsure of a cure's safety, cook it fully rather than eating it raw or lightly smoked.

## 6. Method 5: Root cellar / cool storage (no drying or salt needed)
Some foods preserve for weeks to months just by keeping them cool, dark, humid (or dry, depending on the food), and away from ethylene-producing fruit.
1. **Where:** a basement corner, an underground pit lined with straw/boards, a buried trash can with a lid, or simply the coolest, darkest room in the house — target 32–40°F (0–4°C) for root vegetables if possible, up to 50–60°F (10–15°C) for others.
2. **What keeps well cool and dark:** potatoes, carrots, beets, turnips, onions, winter squash, apples, cabbage (each with different humidity preferences — root vegetables like it humid/packed in damp sand; onions and squash like it dry).
3. **What to keep separate:** apples and other ethylene-producing fruit ripen/rot nearby vegetables faster if stored together — keep them apart.
4. **How:** wrap or layer root vegetables in slightly damp sand, sawdust, or straw in boxes/bins; check periodically and remove any that are softening or sprouting so they don't spread rot.
5. **A DIY "cooler" root cellar:** dig a hole or use a shaded north-facing spot, place a covered container partly buried, add a wet towel over the top for evaporative cooling — effective even without any digging in mild climates.
6. This method needs no fuel, salt, or equipment, which makes it one of the best "first line" options in a prolonged outage.

## 7. Method 6: Pressure canning — the ONLY safe way to can low-acid foods
Low-acid foods (meat, poultry, fish, most vegetables, beans, and soups/stews containing them) must reach **240–250°F** to reliably kill *Clostridium botulinum* spores — a boiling-water bath tops out at 212°F (100°C) at sea level no matter how long you boil it, so it can never do this job. **Do not attempt this without a canner rated for the purpose (dial-gauge or weighted-gauge) and a tested recipe with a stated process time and pressure.**
1. Prepare and fill jars per a tested recipe — correct headspace, wiped rims, lids and bands fingertip-tight — the same as water-bath canning (Method 7, below). The difference is entirely in how the jars are processed.
2. Add the amount of water the canner manufacturer specifies to the canner's bottom, load the jars on the rack, and lock the lid.
3. **Vent (exhaust) for a full 10 minutes before pressurizing.** Heat the canner with the vent/petcock open and no weight on it until steam flows out in a steady, forceful stream, then keep venting for **10 full minutes by the clock** before closing the petcock or adding the weight. **Skipping this step is a real botulism risk** — trapped air pockets make the inside of the canner run cooler than the gauge shows, so the food underprocesses even though the dial or weight looks correct.
4. Bring the canner up to the pressure the recipe specifies and **start timing once that pressure is reached**; process for the full stated time, adjusting the heat to hold pressure as steady as possible (small fluctuations are fine — don't chase the needle).
5. **Altitude changes the pressure, not the time (opposite of water-bath canning):** add **½ lb of pressure per 1,000 ft above sea level**. In practice — **weighted-gauge canners:** use the 15 lb weight (instead of 10 lb) at any altitude above 1,000 ft. **Dial-gauge canners:** use the banded PSI for your altitude from the recipe's altitude chart (commonly around 11 lb at 0–2,000 ft, 12 lb at 2,001–4,000 ft, 13 lb at 4,001–6,000 ft — check your recipe's specific chart).
6. When the time is up, turn off the heat and **let the canner depressurize and cool down completely on its own** — **never** run water over it, open the vent early, or otherwise force-cool it; this can cause loss of liquid from the jars, seal failure, or be dangerous while still under pressure.
7. **Wait until the gauge reads zero pressure, then wait 10 more minutes** before unlocking the lid — this lets pressure inside the jars equalize safely. Open the petcock/vent first, then unlock and **lift the lid with the far edge tilted away from your face** so escaping steam doesn't burn you.
8. Remove jars with a jar lifter, set upright on a towel, and don't disturb for 12–24 hours; check seals the next day exactly as described for water-bath canning below.

(NCHFP / UC Master Food Preserver Program guidance.)

## 8. Method 7: Water-bath canning — high-acid foods ONLY
> ⚠️ **BOTULISM WARNING — READ BEFORE CANNING ANYTHING.** Water-bath (boiling water) canning only reaches 212°F (100°C) at sea level. That is **not hot enough** to kill *Clostridium botulinum* spores. It is only safe for **high-acid foods** (pH 4.6 or below): most fruits, jams/jellies, and properly acidified pickles (with enough added vinegar per a tested recipe). **Never** water-bath can plain vegetables, meat, poultry, fish, or soups/stews containing them — these are low-acid and require a **pressure canner** (Method 6, above). **If you do not have a pressure canner, do not can low-acid foods at all** — freeze, dry, salt, ferment, or eat them fresh instead. When in doubt, don't can it.
1. Use only tested recipes with correct acid ratios (real lemon juice or 5% vinegar in stated amounts) — do not reduce the acid or sugar in a recipe "to taste."
2. **Tomatoes need acid added, every time.** Modern tomato varieties are not reliably acidic enough on their own for safe water-bath canning. Add **2 Tbsp bottled lemon juice OR ½ tsp citric acid per quart jar** (**1 Tbsp bottled lemon juice or ¼ tsp citric acid per pint**) before filling — use bottled lemon juice, not fresh, since its acidity is standardized. This applies to whole/halved tomatoes, crushed tomatoes, and juice alike.
   - **Worked example:** canning crushed tomatoes with no added liquid — add ¼ tsp citric acid (or 1 Tbsp bottled lemon juice) to each pint jar, fill with hot crushed tomatoes to ½-inch headspace, wipe rims, apply lids, and process in a boiling-water bath for **35 minutes (pints) or 45 minutes (quarts)** at altitudes 0–1,000 ft — add more time at higher altitude per a canning altitude chart.
3. Inspect jars for chips/cracks; use new lids each time; wash jars, lids, and bands in hot soapy water.
4. Fill hot jars with hot food, leaving the recipe's stated headspace (commonly 1/4–1/2 inch), remove air bubbles with a non-metal tool, wipe the rim clean, and apply lid and band fingertip-tight.
5. Submerge jars fully in a pot of boiling water (at least 1–2 inches of water over the jar tops) and boil for the time the recipe specifies (commonly 5–20+ minutes depending on food and jar size) — start timing once the water returns to a full boil. **Add more time at higher altitude** (consult a canning altitude chart) — for water-bath canning it's the *time* that changes with altitude, not the temperature.
6. Remove jars with a jar lifter, set upright on a towel, and do not disturb for 12–24 hours.
7. **Verify the seal:** the lid center should be concave (curved down) and not flex when pressed. If a lid flexes/pops, that jar did not seal — refrigerate and eat soon, or reprocess with a new lid.
8. Label with the date; store sealed jars in a cool, dark place; use within about a year for best quality.
9. **Before eating any home-canned food:** check the seal again, look for bulging lids, leaks, spurting liquid, off odor, or cloudiness. **Any of these = discard without tasting**, and dispose so animals/people cannot access it. For low-acid home-canned food specifically, USDA guidance recommends boiling it 10 minutes before tasting even if it looks normal, because botulinum toxin (unlike the spores) is destroyed by boiling — but this is a rescue step for already-canned low-acid food, not a substitute for pressure canning in the first place.

**Never use these "folk" canning shortcuts — none of them are safe substitutes for a real water-bath or pressure canner:** do **not** oven-can, dishwasher-can, or open-kettle can (filling jars with boiling food and sealing them without any water-bath or pressure processing); do **not** invert jars to help them "seal"; do **not** seal jars with a layer of paraffin/wax instead of a proper two-piece lid. All of these fail to reliably reach or hold a safe temperature and are documented sources of real botulism cases (USDA/NCHFP).

## 9. Method 8: Pickling and fermentation
1. **Quick vinegar pickling (not for long-term shelf storage unless water-bath canned per Method 7):** submerge vegetables in a boiled brine of roughly equal parts vinegar (5% acidity) and water with salt and sugar to taste, cool, refrigerate; keeps refrigerated for weeks.
2. **Lacto-fermentation (traditional, no refrigeration needed during the ferment):** dissolve **about 2–3 tablespoons non-iodized salt per quart/liter of water** (roughly a 3–5% brine), submerge vegetables (cabbage for sauerkraut, cucumbers, etc.) completely under the brine using a weight, cover loosely to let gas escape, and keep at room temperature (65–75°F ideal) out of direct sun.
3. **Watch for:** bubbling and a pleasant sour smell after a few days to a couple of weeks (normal); a thin white film (kahm yeast) can often be skimmed off; **fuzzy mold in colors (black, pink, orange) or a putrid smell means discard the batch.**
4. Always keep vegetables **fully submerged** under the brine — anything exposed to air can mold. Once fermented to taste, move to the refrigerator or a cool root-cellar-type space to slow further fermentation.
5. Fermentation is generally lower-risk than canning because the acid produced by the process itself inhibits botulinum growth, but still discard anything that smells wrong, looks slimy, or grows colored mold.

# PART B — COOKING WITHOUT ELECTRICITY

## Method 1: Open campfire cooking
1. Build a fire per `../fire/fire.md`; let it burn down to glowing **coals** for cooking (coals give steady, controllable heat — flames mostly just blacken food).
2. **Direct methods:** skewer/roast over coals; place a grate over coals for grilling; use a pot/pan set directly on rocks or a tripod over coals for boiling/frying.
3. **Ash/ember baking:** wrap food (potatoes, corn, fish in foil or wet leaves) and bury it directly in hot coals/ash; typical potato takes **45–60 minutes**, turning occasionally.
4. **Verify doneness** with a thermometer for meat/poultry (see temperatures below); for vegetables, a knife should slide in easily.
5. Keep a bucket of water or dirt nearby to fully extinguish afterward — stir ashes and feel for heat before leaving.

## Method 2: Rocket stove (build + use)
A rocket stove burns small sticks very efficiently in an insulated L- or J-shaped chamber, concentrating heat under a pot and using far less wood than an open fire.
1. **Simple build:** stack or mortar bricks (or use two nested metal cans) into an "L" shape — a short horizontal feed tunnel at the bottom leading into a vertical chimney of the same width, roughly 4–6 inches (10–15 cm) across and 12–18 inches (30–45 cm) tall.
2. Leave a small gap at the base of the vertical section for airflow, and set your pot directly on top of the chimney opening, with a small gap around the pot rim for exhaust.
3. **Use:** feed small sticks/twigs horizontally into the feed tunnel a few inches at a time; the design pulls air in and concentrates a hot flame under the pot.
4. **Realistic performance:** boils a liter of water in roughly **8–12 minutes** once established, using a fraction of the wood an open fire needs; good for boiling, simmering, and pan-frying; less suited to slow baking.
5. Always operate outdoors or in a fully open, well-ventilated area — it still produces carbon monoxide and smoke.

## Method 3: Solar oven (build + use)
1. **Simple build:** line a cardboard box (or a box-within-a-box with insulation like crumpled newspaper between them) with aluminum foil, shiny side out, to reflect and trap light; add a foil-covered cardboard flap as a reflector angled to bounce extra sunlight in; cover the box opening with a clear plastic bag or piece of glass to trap heat (greenhouse effect); paint or place a dark pot/pan inside to absorb heat.
2. **Use:** set up in direct, strong sunlight, angle the reflector flap to bounce maximum light onto the dark pot, and re-aim every 30–60 minutes as the sun moves.
3. **Realistic temperatures:** a well-built DIY solar oven typically reaches **200–300°F (93–150°C)** on a clear, sunny day — hot enough to cook, not to sear or deep-fry.
4. **Realistic times:** rice or vegetables 1.5–3 hours; a whole chicken or dense casserole 3–5+ hours, depending on sun strength and box quality. Solar cooking is slow — start early in the day.
5. **Verify doneness by thermometer, not time** — cloud cover can silently stall cooking; check internal temperature (poultry 165°F) before assuming food is done.
6. Best performance is within about 3 hours of solar noon; performance drops sharply in haze, clouds, or wind unless the box is well sealed.

## Method 4: Charcoal / grill cooking
1. Light charcoal in a chimney starter or with a small kindling fire underneath (avoid excess lighter fluid, which taints food and is a burn hazard); coals are ready when covered in **gray ash**, roughly 15–20 minutes after lighting.
2. **Direct heat** (coals spread evenly under the grate) for quick-cooking items (burgers, vegetables, thin fillets); **indirect heat** (coals banked to one side, food on the other, lid on) for larger cuts and slower cooking, similar to an oven.
3. A basic charcoal grill with vents cracked typically holds **250–350°F (120–175°C)**; opening vents wider raises heat, closing them lowers it.
4. Cook to safe internal temperatures (below) using a thermometer inserted into the thickest part, avoiding bone.
5. **This is an outdoor-only method** — never burn charcoal in a garage, tent, or any enclosed space; see the carbon monoxide warning under Method 5.

## Method 5: Camp/propane stove — safe indoor use and the carbon monoxide warning
> ⚠️ **CARBON MONOXIDE WARNING.** Camp stoves, propane burners, charcoal, and any other combustion cooking device consume oxygen and produce carbon monoxide (CO) — an odorless, colorless gas that causes drowsiness, headache, confusion, and death **with no warning smell**. **Never** run any fueled stove, grill, or heater inside a closed house, tent, garage, camper, or vehicle. If you must cook indoors during an emergency, do so **only** with a stove explicitly rated for indoor use, in a well-ventilated space with a window cracked and airflow through the room, and with a **battery-powered CO detector running**. When possible, cook outdoors or in a fully open garage/carport with the door up and cross-ventilation — that is always safer than any "indoor-safe" claim.
1. **Setup:** place the stove on a stable, heat-proof, level surface away from anything flammable; check the fuel line/canister connection is snug before lighting.
2. **Lighting:** open the fuel valve slightly, then ignite immediately with a lighter/match held at the burner — do not let unburned gas accumulate before lighting.
3. Most single-burner camp/propane stoves output roughly **7,000–10,000 BTU**, enough to boil water in **5–8 minutes** and simmer/fry normally, similar to a kitchen stovetop.
4. Turn off fully when finished; store fuel canisters upright, away from heat, out of direct sun, and never in a fully sealed hot vehicle.
5. Keep a spare canister/fuel supply — running out mid-cook in cold weather especially can leave food half-cooked in the danger zone.

## Method 6: Wood stove cooking
1. A wood-burning stove (indoor stove or outdoor box-type) provides sustained, controllable heat for a household already using one for heating — cook on the stovetop surface (hottest directly over the firebox) or inside an oven compartment if the stove has one.
2. Regulate heat with the air-intake damper: more air = hotter/faster fire; less air = a longer, cooler burn suited to simmering.
3. Stovetop surface heat is highly variable by design and fire size — use a thermometer in any oven compartment (a stove oven often runs **300–450°F**) and check food doneness by internal temperature rather than by dial settings, since wood-stove ovens are far less precise than electric ones.
4. **Only use a wood stove properly installed with a chimney/flue to the outside** — this is a heating appliance, not a substitute for an open fire in an unvented room, and still requires a working CO detector nearby as a backup safety check.
5. Keep a fire extinguisher or bucket of water/sand nearby, and never leave a lit wood stove unattended with children or pets present.

## Method 7: Hay-box / retained-heat cooking
This method finishes cooking food using only heat already stored in the pot — it uses almost no additional fuel after the initial boil, which matters greatly when fuel is scarce.
1. Bring the food to a full boil on any heat source for the normal early cooking time (e.g., rice or beans: boil 5–10 minutes).
2. Immediately wrap the sealed, lidded pot in heavy insulation — hay, straw, blankets, towels, sleeping bags, or pillows — packed tightly on all sides including underneath, inside an insulated box or basket ("hay box").
3. Leave undisturbed, without adding heat, for the remaining cook time — often **2–4 hours** for rice, beans, or stews (roughly 3–4 times the normal stovetop time, since retained heat cooks more slowly).
4. **Verify doneness** by temperature and texture when you open it — grains/beans should be tender; if still hard, re-boil briefly and re-insulate.
5. This method is ideal for rice, beans, grains, and tough cuts of meat, and can extend a single small fire's usefulness across a whole meal.

## Method 8: Dutch oven cooking
1. A cast-iron dutch oven with a flanged (lipped) lid can bake, roast, simmer, and fry using charcoal briquettes or wood coals placed both underneath and on top of the lid.
2. **Rule of thumb for baking temperature (~350°F):** total briquette count ≈ **2× the oven's diameter in inches** (e.g., a 12-inch oven ≈ **24–25 briquettes total**) — split roughly **⅔ on top of the lid, ⅓ underneath** (for a 12-inch oven, that's about **16–17 on top, 8 underneath**); top heat browns/bakes, bottom heat can scorch if overloaded. (An older "diameter plus 2" rule of thumb is close to the TOP-only count, not the total — using it as a total will badly underheat the oven.)
3. For simmering/stewing, use fewer coals, mostly or all underneath, and monitor with a thermometer through the lid gap or by lifting briefly.
4. Rotate the oven and lid a quarter turn every 15–20 minutes to avoid hot spots from uneven coal placement.
5. **Verify doneness** by internal food temperature for meat/poultry, or by testing bread/biscuits with a toothpick/knife for baked goods.
6. Season and maintain cast iron (light oil coat, dry storage) so it doesn't rust between uses — a well-seasoned dutch oven is one of the most versatile no-power cooking tools available.

## Food-safety internal temperatures (applies to every cooking method above)
Use a food thermometer inserted into the thickest part, away from bone, for every method — sight, time, and color are not reliable substitutes.
- **Poultry (whole, pieces, ground):** 165°F (74°C)
- **Ground meat (beef, pork, lamb, veal):** 160°F (71°C)
- **Whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, veal, and fish:** 145°F (63°C), then rest 3 minutes before cutting/eating
- **Eggs:** cook until both yolk and white are firm, or to 160°F if being combined into a dish
- **Leftovers/reheated food:** 165°F (74°C)
- **Hot-held food (kept warm before serving):** keep at or above 140°F (60°C); food below 140°F for more than 2 hours (1 hour above 90°F ambient) should be discarded per the danger-zone rule.

## Rationing a food store
1. **Inventory first:** list what you have and roughly how many calories/servings it provides; prioritize perishables (use them before shelf-stable items spoil).
2. **Plan by calories, not just meals:** a sedentary adult needs roughly 1,600–2,400 calories/day; hard physical work or cold weather raises this significantly — plan food stretch accordingly rather than assuming "three meals" is enough activity-adjusted fuel.
3. **Stretch food safely, not riskily:** reduce portion size and add filling low-cost bulk (rice, beans, oats) before you cut corners on food-safety steps like proper cooking temperature or discarding spoiled food — a case of food poisoning during a shortage is far more dangerous than being hungrier.
4. **Rotate stock:** eat oldest supplies first ("first in, first out"); recheck dates and can/jar seals regularly.
5. **Protect the supply:** store in rodent/insect-proof containers, off the ground, away from heat and damp, and keep it inventoried so no one under- or over-eats blindly.
6. **Prioritize vulnerable people** (children, elderly, pregnant/nursing, those with medical conditions) for adequate calories and protein first if the store must be stretched.

## Regional & scenario variants
- **North Texas heat note (summer, 95–105°F+ days):** Sun-drying and jerky-making actually work fast in this heat, but the **danger zone is reached quickly** — raw meat and marinating meat must stay refrigerated or iced until the moment you start heating/drying it, and any perishable food left in a hot car, garage, or countertop crosses the 4-hour discard limit much faster than the label times assume; treat 2 hours as your practical outdoor limit on 95°F+ days. Solar ovens perform very well here (expect the higher end of the 200–300°F range). A wood/rocket stove or dutch oven is more comfortable used outdoors, in shade, rather than heating up a kitchen.
- **Cold climates:** Freezing is a free, effective preservation method when ambient temperatures stay below 32°F — an unheated porch, shed, or buried container can function as a natural freezer; but check food frequently, since freeze-thaw cycles from swinging temperatures can push food into unsafe ranges. Solar ovens are far less effective in low winter sun angles and short days; rely more on wood stoves, rocket stoves, and hay-box cooking to conserve fuel.
- **Desert:** Very low humidity favors fast, safe air/sun drying of both produce and meat, but wide day/night temperature swings mean cool storage (root-cellar style, buried or shaded) can be excellent overnight and poor at midday — check temperatures rather than assuming.
- **Urban/suburban:** Space for a root cellar or fire pit may be limited — a shaded, insulated cooler substitute or an interior closet on an exterior wall can serve as cool storage; balconies and small patios can still host a rocket stove, solar oven, or camp stove **only** with good airflow and never in an enclosed balcony or breezeway.
- **Rural:** More room for a true buried root cellar, an outdoor smokehouse, and larger-batch canning/curing; also generally easier to keep fires a safe distance from structures and neighbors.

## ⚠️ Safety — warnings & mistakes to avoid
- **Never** water-bath can low-acid foods (meat, most vegetables, beans, soups). Use a pressure canner or don't can them at all.
- **Never** run a charcoal grill, propane stove, camp stove, generator, or wood fire inside a closed house, tent, garage, or vehicle — carbon monoxide kills without warning. Cook outdoors or in a space with genuine cross-ventilation, and use a battery CO detector as backup, not as the primary safety measure.
- **Never** taste-test food to check if it's spoiled or safe — some toxins (including botulinum) have no smell or taste.
- **Never** eat home-canned or preserved food with a bulging lid, spurting liquid, off odor, or cloudy contents — discard without tasting, out of reach of people and animals.
- **Never** cold-smoke raw meat/fish as a finished, ready-to-eat product unless it was fully cured first or will still be cooked to a safe temperature — cold smoke flavors, it does not make food safe.
- **Never** guess doneness by color or time alone for meat/poultry — always confirm with a food thermometer at the numbers listed above.
- **Never** refreeze food that has been above 40°F for more than 2 hours, or eat perishable food left unrefrigerated more than 4 hours.
- **Never** leave a fermenting or curing container's food exposed above the brine line — mold risk rises sharply.
- Do not reduce the vinegar, sugar, or salt in a tested preservation recipe "to taste" — these ratios are food-safety limits, not flavor choices.
- Keep raw meat, its juices, and its marinade away from anything eaten raw (cutting boards, utensils, surfaces) — wash between uses.

## Troubleshooting
- **Jerky feels dry outside but is still moist/soft inside:** slice thinner next time; for the current batch, continue drying at low heat until it bends and cracks without any moist give — do not stop early.
- **Dried fruit/vegetables grow mold in storage:** they weren't dry enough or weren't conditioned before sealing — discard moldy pieces; for future batches, condition (open-jar rest, shake daily) before final sealed storage.
- **Fermentation jar shows fuzzy colored mold or smells rotten/putrid (not just sour/yeasty):** discard the whole batch — do not try to scoop around visible mold on a ferment.
- **Canning jar lid didn't seal (flexes when pressed):** refrigerate and use within a few days, or reprocess within 24 hours with a new lid and the full process time.
- **Solar oven isn't getting hot:** re-aim the reflector directly at the sun, check the plastic/glass glazing is sealed with no gaps letting heat escape, and confirm you're within about 3 hours of solar noon; clouds/haze will stall it regardless of setup.
- **Rocket stove smokes heavily or won't draw:** feed smaller/drier sticks a little at a time rather than large pieces, and check the base air gap isn't blocked.
- **Charcoal won't reach cooking temperature:** ensure coals are fully ashed-over gray before cooking, and open vents wider for more airflow/heat.
- **Suspect carbon monoxide symptoms (headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion) while cooking indoors or in a garage:** get everyone into fresh air immediately, stop the combustion source, and seek medical help — do not go back in to "just check."
