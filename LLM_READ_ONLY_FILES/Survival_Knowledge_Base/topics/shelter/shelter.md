# Building Shelter and Staying Warm or Cool Without Power

## Read this first (the 30-second version)
1. **Shelter is often more urgent than food or even water.** A human can die of hypothermia (too cold) in a few hours, or of heatstroke (too hot) in under a day. You can survive weeks without food.
2. **The ground will steal your heat faster than the air.** A layer of insulation UNDER you (leaves, pine boughs, cardboard, foam) matters as much as anything over you — never lie directly on bare ground.
3. **Pick your site before you build:** avoid low spots (cold air and flood water pool there), dead trees/branches overhead ("widowmakers"), ant/wasp nests, and dry creek beds. Get near a water source but not in it. Note wind direction.
4. **Small and low beats big and drafty.** A shelter just big enough for your body traps your own body heat; a shelter you can stand up in cannot be kept warm without a fire.
5. **If you are indoors with no power:** seal off ONE small room, layer clothing and blankets, build a "tent" inside the room, and if you burn anything for heat, ventilate and never use a charcoal/propane/generator device indoors — carbon monoxide kills silently.
6. **In extreme heat (North Texas summer):** get INTO shade before midday, move to the lowest/coolest part of a building (basement or lowest floor, or shaded ground level), soak cloth in water and drape it on your skin, and slow down — heat illness kills faster than most people expect.
7. This guide gives you **six** different shelter builds plus full urban heat/cold guidance, so you always have an option that matches your materials, climate, and time.

---

## Why this matters
Your body must hold its **core temperature** near 98.6°F (37°C). Move only a few degrees off that and your organs and brain stop working correctly.

- **Too cold — hypothermia:** Below about 95°F (35°C) core temperature, you get shivering, clumsiness, slurred speech, and confusion. Shivering is not a reliable early-warning sign that resolves quickly — it usually continues through mild hypothermia and only stops (a very bad sign, indicating moderate-to-severe hypothermia) once core temperature drops to roughly 86–90°F, at which point the heart can go into a fatal rhythm. Wet clothing, wind, and contact with cold ground all pull heat away far faster than still, dry cold air. **You can become hypothermic even at 50°F (10°C) if wet and windy** — this is not just a "snow" problem.
- **Too hot — heat exhaustion and heatstroke:** Above about 104°F (40°C) core temperature, the brain and organs begin to fail. Heatstroke is a medical emergency: confusion, hot/dry or clammy skin, rapid pulse, collapse. In North Texas summers, afternoon heat index routinely passes 105°F, and a closed vehicle or attic can exceed 140°F (60°C) within an hour.
- **Shelter solves both problems** by controlling four things at once: wind, precipitation (rain/snow), sun/radiant heat, and — critically — **contact with the ground**, which conducts heat away from (or into) your body 20–25 times faster than still air.
- Without power, you lose HVAC, so shelter skill becomes the difference between a survivable night and a medical emergency, whether you are in a forest, a suburban backyard, or your own living room.

## Key terms (plain language)
- **Core temperature** — the internal temperature of your vital organs; what actually determines survival, not how your skin feels.
- **Hypothermia** — dangerously low core body temperature from cold exposure.
- **Heat exhaustion / heatstroke** — heat exhaustion is heavy sweating, weakness, nausea (still sweating, still somewhat alert); heatstroke is life-threatening — sweating may STOP, skin can be hot and dry or clammy, confusion or collapse. Heatstroke needs emergency cooling and medical help immediately.
- **Conduction** — heat loss (or gain) through direct contact, e.g., lying on cold ground, or a hot floor radiating heat up into you.
- **Convection** — heat carried away by moving air (wind chill) or water (a wet body loses heat fast).
- **Radiation** — heat lost as infrared energy to the cold night sky, or gained from direct sun.
- **Evaporation** — heat lost as sweat or wet clothing dries; useful for cooling in heat, dangerous in cold (wet = cold).
- **Ground insulation / bedding** — a layer of dry material between your body and the ground/cold air that stops conduction. Often the single most-overlooked survival item.
- **Widowmaker** — a dead branch or tree that can fall without warning; never camp or sit directly under one.
- **Microclimate** — the localized conditions around your specific chosen spot (a hollow can be 10–15°F colder at night than open ground nearby; a shaded north-facing spot can be 20°F cooler than open sun).
- **Shelter-in-place** — staying inside an existing structure (your home) rather than building a new outdoor shelter, and adapting that structure to hold heat or stay cool.

## What you need
**For an outdoor shelter (any method below):**
- A cutting tool (knife, hatchet, saw) — or none; many builds can be done by hand/breaking branches.
- Cordage: paracord, rope, or improvised (see the [Tools & Knots guide](../tools-knots-repair/tools-knots-repair.md)) — vines, inner tree bark strips, torn cloth, or shoelaces work as substitutes.
- A tarp, poncho, painter's plastic sheeting, or large trash bags — or, with no tarp at all, natural debris (branches, leaves, bark, pine boughs) can build a complete shelter.
- Ground insulation material: dead dry leaves, pine needles/boughs, dry grass, cardboard, newspaper, foam padding, extra clothing, a car floor mat.
- Gloves if available (protect hands while gathering debris).

**Improvised substitutes:**
| Need | Substitute |
|---|---|
| Tarp | Painter's plastic drop cloth, large contractor trash bags cut open, poncho, space/emergency blanket, car cover |
| Cordage | Shoelaces, strips of torn t-shirt/bedsheet, vines, duct tape, backpack straps |
| Stakes | Sharpened sticks, tent stakes, large rocks to weigh down edges |
| Ground insulation | Cardboard boxes flattened, newspaper stuffed in clothing, dry leaf piles 12+ inches deep (compresses a lot), car seats/mats, packing foam |
| Saw/axe | Any sturdy rock for pounding, a large sturdy knife baton'd with a stick, or simply choosing dead-fall wood that's already down |

# SITE SELECTION — do this before any build
Where you put a shelter matters as much as how you build it. Spend 10–15 minutes scouting before committing.

1. **Look up.** Check for dead branches or leaning dead trees ("widowmakers") over your chosen spot. Do not camp under them — they fall without warning, including in calm weather.
2. **Avoid low ground.** Valleys, ravines, and dry creek/stream beds collect cold air at night (colder by 10–15°F than a spot just upslope) AND can flash-flood in rain, even from rain falling miles away. Choose a spot slightly elevated, on a bench or shoulder of higher ground.
3. **Check for water nearby, but not too close.** You want water for drinking within a reasonable walk, but not so close that you're in the flood zone, near mosquito breeding areas (standing water = bugs), or on an animal trail leading to a water source.
4. **Check the ground itself.** Look for ant hills, wasp/hornet nests, animal burrows, and poison ivy/oak before clearing a spot or gathering debris. Clear rocks and sticks from where your body will lie.
5. **Note the wind.** Face any shelter opening AWAY from the prevailing wind direction. A hill or dense treeline can serve as a windbreak.
6. **Think about sun** for the opposite reason in heat: in hot climates, pick a spot shaded most of the day (north side of a rise, under dense tree canopy); in cold climates, a spot that gets morning sun helps you warm up.
7. **Consider concealment and safety** if relevant to your situation (visible to rescuers if you want to be found; hidden if security is a concern — see the [Security guide]).
8. **Match shelter size to body heat.** Build only as large as your body (plus gear) requires. Excess airspace is space you have to heat with your own body warmth.

## Method 1: Tarp A-Frame (fastest all-weather build)
**Best for:** having a tarp/plastic sheet and some cordage; rain and wind protection; 15–30 minutes to build.

**Materials:** 1 tarp (approx. 8x10 ft / 2.4x3 m or larger), ~15–20 ft (5–6 m) of cordage, 2 trees roughly 8–10 ft (2.5–3 m) apart OR two sturdy poles/tripods, stakes or rocks.

**Steps:**
1. Tie a **ridgeline** (a straight length of cordage) between two trees at about waist-to-chest height (roughly 3 ft / 1 m off the ground), pulled taut, using a taut-line hitch or two half-hitches (see Knots guide).
2. Drape the tarp evenly over the ridgeline so equal amounts hang on both sides, like an upside-down V (a tent shape).
3. Pull each side out and down to the ground at an angle, and stake down the corners and edge grommets/loops. If the tarp has no grommets, wrap a small pebble in the tarp corner and tie cordage around the bunched fabric to create an anchor point ("rock-in-a-sock" method).
4. Close one end fully (stake it to the ground) — this becomes your wind-block end; face it into the prevailing wind. Leave the other end as your entry, or partially close it too and crawl in from the side.
5. **Ground insulation:** Before you consider it finished, pile at least 4–6 inches (10–15 cm) of dry leaves, pine boughs, or grass inside, under where your body will lie.

**How to tell it worked:** The tarp is taut (a finger-flick makes a drum-like sound, not a floppy flap) so wind doesn't collapse it; no low spots that will pool rainwater on top; you are fully dry inside during a rain test; you cannot feel the cold ground through your insulation layer when lying down.

## Method 2: Tarp Lean-To (best with a fire, or in mild/dry weather)
**Best for:** when you'll have a fire to sit facing (radiant heat reflects off the angled tarp back at you); less rain protection than the A-frame since one side is fully open.

**Materials:** same as Method 1, but you only need to stake one edge — the other stays open.

**Steps:**
1. Tie the ridgeline between two trees at about chest height, same as the A-frame.
2. Drape the tarp over the ridgeline but let ALL of it hang down on ONE side only (instead of splitting it into a V).
3. Stake the bottom edge of that hanging side to the ground, and stake the two bottom corners out to the sides so the tarp forms a slanted wall/roof — like a single sloped panel, open on the front and often the two ends.
4. Build your fire **several feet in front of the open side** (never touching the tarp — most tarps are flammable or melt) so the angled tarp reflects heat back down onto you. Keep the fire small, contained, and attended.
5. Add a thick ground bed of leaves/boughs as in Method 1. In wind or rain, angle the lean-to's back to the weather and the open face away from it (unless you need the fire's reflected heat, in which case balance the two).

**How to tell it worked:** You can sit or lie under the angled panel and feel the fire's heat reflecting down on you; the tarp stays several feet from any flame or spark; rain runs off the slope without pooling.

## Method 3: Debris Hut (no tarp needed — pure natural materials)
**Best for:** no tarp/tools available; cold nights; this is one of the best natural shelters for retaining body heat because it's built small and thickly insulated on all sides.

![Two-panel illustration of a debris hut: (1) the ridgepole propped against a tree stump with rib branches leaned along both sides forming the frame, and (2) the same frame fully covered in a thick shingled layer of leaf/branch debris, with a dark entrance opening at one end](images/shelter_section.jpg)
*Figure 1 — Debris hut construction (US Army FM 21-76 Survival Manual, Fig. 5-11; public domain, US federal government work). Panel 1 shows the **ridgepole and rib framework** propped against a stump/support with ribbing branches leaned along both sides. Panel 2 shows the finished shelter with a thick outer layer of piled **leaf/branch debris** shingled over the entire frame and a small entrance left open at one end. Use it to picture the frame you build first and how completely the debris layer should cover it before you crawl in.*
*Source/license: U.S. Army, FM 21-76 Survival Manual (public domain, U.S. government work), via Wikimedia Commons (File:Debris_hut.gif).*

**Materials:** a long, sturdy **ridgepole** (8–10 ft / 2.5–3 m, as thick as your wrist or thicker), shorter ribbing branches, and a LOT of dead leaves/debris (more than you think — a finished pile is often 2-3 ft / 60-90 cm thick).

**Steps:**
1. Find or prop your ridgepole with one end on the ground and the other end resting on a stump, low branch fork, or a forked support stick, about waist height, forming a long wedge shape ("A-frame skeleton").
2. Lean shorter branches (ribbing) along both sides of the ridgepole, spaced a few inches apart, like ribs, to form the frame — angled from the ridgepole down to the ground on both sides, leaving a body-sized tunnel underneath just big enough to wriggle into feet-first.
3. Layer smaller sticks and twigs crosswise over the ribbing to create a lattice that will hold debris in place (like lath under plaster).
4. Pile leaves, pine needles, ferns, or dry grass over the entire frame, starting at the bottom and shingling upward (like roof shingles) so water sheds off. Build this layer **at least 2 feet (60 cm) thick** on all sides and the top — thin debris shelters fail; thick ones are remarkably warm. Test by pushing a hand in from outside; if light shows through easily, add more.
5. Stuff the inside floor with another foot (30 cm) of dry, fluffed debris as your bedding — this compresses under your body weight, so pile it generously.
6. Build a door plug: a small bundle of debris or your pack that you pull in behind you to close the entrance once inside.
7. Enter feet-first and wriggle down into the debris bedding so it surrounds your body like a sleeping bag.

**How to tell it worked:** Inside, it should feel almost cramped — barely room to turn over — which is correct; your own body heat should noticeably warm the tight air space within 15-20 minutes of getting in; no cold drafts should be felt through the walls; a hand pushed against the outside should not easily poke through to the inside.

## Method 4: Simple Branch Lean-To (natural materials, faster than a debris hut)
**Best for:** quick, simple wind/rain break using only branches and foliage; less warm than a debris hut but much faster to build (20–40 minutes) and works well against one dominant wind direction or light rain.

**Materials:** a horizontal support (a fallen log, a rope tied between two trees, or a branch wedged in a tree fork at waist-to-chest height), and many long branches to lean against it.

**Steps:**
1. Set your horizontal support at roughly waist to chest height between two trees or on natural supports.
2. Lean long branches against this support at an angle, side by side, so they form a slanted wall — similar to Method 2's shape but built from branches instead of a tarp.
3. Weave smaller branches and twigs across the angled branches to thicken the wall and help hold on the next layer.
4. Pile on leafy branches, bark slabs, bundled grass, or ferns, shingled from the bottom up, until the wall blocks light and wind (a hand pushed from outside should not easily poke through — same test as the debris hut).
5. Add a thick ground bed of leaves/boughs underneath, as in every method above.
6. If time and material allow, build a second angled wall on the opposite side to fully enclose the space (turning it into an A-frame shape), or build a fire in front of the single open wall as in Method 2.

**How to tell it worked:** The wall blocks a hand-held light shone from outside at night (or blocks wind you can feel with your hand); it has held together and not collapsed after being nudged/tested; you have a ground bed under you.

## Method 5: Snow Trench (cold/snow regions)
**Best for:** cold climates with snow cover but not enough deep, settled/wind-packed snow for a quinzhee (Method 6); fast to dig, much warmer than sitting in open cold air, though not as warm as a quinzhee or debris hut.

**Materials:** a shovel, snowshoe, pot lid, or any flat digging tool (or your hands with gloves); a tarp or branches for a roof; insulation for the floor (boughs, pack, foam pad — never bare snow under your body).

**Steps:**
1. Choose a spot with snow at least 2-3 ft (60-90 cm) deep, away from avalanche slopes, cornices, or anything that could collapse on you.
2. Dig a trench roughly body-width (about 2 ft / 60 cm wide) and just long enough to lie down in, as deep as you can manage — deeper is warmer since you get below the wind.
3. If possible, dig the trench slightly wider at the bottom than the top, or carve a small shelf, so you can lay a roof (branches + tarp, or snow blocks) across the top rather than leaving it fully open to the sky. A covered trench traps far more heat than an open one.
4. Line the floor with a THICK layer of pine boughs, dry grass, or your pack and any insulating pad — this is non-negotiable in snow; without it the snow will conduct your body heat away rapidly.
5. Cover the trench opening with branches, then a tarp or more snow, but always leave a small ventilation gap — never seal it completely, even with no flame or stove inside. Your own exhaled CO2 builds up in a sealed snow shelter (see Safety section on carbon monoxide/suffocation — this applies to snow shelters too).

**How to tell it worked:** Wind noise from outside is noticeably reduced or gone once inside; your body heat visibly starts to soften/glaze the trench's inner snow walls after a while (a good sign of retained warmth, in moderation); you are not lying on bare snow.

## Method 6: Quinzhee (cold/snow regions — warmest snow shelter, more time needed)
**Best for:** cold climates with access to loose/powdery snow that can be piled up, even if it isn't naturally deep — you make your own snow pile. Takes 2-4 hours including a wait for the snow to harden, so plan ahead; not an emergency-minute shelter.

**Materials:** a shovel or digging tool, several sticks about 12 inches (30 cm) long, gloves, dry clothing to change into after digging (you WILL sweat and get wet building this).

**Steps:**
1. Pile loose snow into a mound roughly 6-7 ft (2 m) across and at least 4-5 ft (1.2-1.5 m) high — bigger than you think you need, since digging out the inside removes a lot of volume. Mix snow from a wide area as you pile it (this helps it bond/sinter together better than snow from one spot).
2. Push the 12-inch sticks into the mound from the outside, all over its surface, so they stick straight in about a foot deep. These are your "depth gauges" for hollowing later.
3. Let the pile sit and harden ("sinter") for at least 1-2 hours. This step cannot be rushed — digging in too early causes collapse.
4. Dig an entrance tunnel low on the downwind side, angled slightly upward into the mound (a low entrance and a raised sleeping shelf trap warm air, since cold air sinks and pools near the entrance/floor).
5. Hollow out the inside, digging until you reach the base of each stick from the inside — that tells you the wall is the right thickness (roughly 12 inches / 30 cm all around, thick enough for strength and insulation, thin enough not to collapse from too little wall-to-span ratio).
6. Poke a small ventilation hole through the roof with a stick and keep it clear — quinzhees can and do trap carbon dioxide/reduce oxygen in a sealed space, especially with a candle or stove inside.
7. Smooth the interior ceiling with a gloved hand or tool; a smooth ceiling drips less than a rough one as it warms.
8. Line the sleeping shelf with a thick bough/pack/foam bed, same rule as always: never lie directly on snow.

**How to tell it worked:** The mound held its shape through the hollowing process without collapsing; wall thickness is consistent (sticks just barely visible/removed from inside all over); a lit candle stays lit but you still have and use the vent hole (never treat a candle staying lit as proof of safe oxygen levels — always keep the vent clear regardless); the interior stays noticeably above outside air temperature (quinzhees commonly hold in the 20s-30s°F / around 0°C even when it's far colder outside).

⚠️ Quinzhees and snow trenches carry real suffocation/collapse risk — see the Safety section before relying on one.

## URBAN & SUBURBAN — Sheltering in place without power

When you're in a house or apartment and the power (and likely heat/AC) is out, you don't need to go outside to build a shelter — you shrink your living space instead.

### Staying warm indoors without power
1. **Pick ONE small, interior room** to seal off and live in — a bedroom or room without large windows or exterior walls if possible. Small = easier to warm with body heat; interior = fewer cold walls.
2. **Seal the gaps.** Use towels or blankets rolled against the bottom of the door, tape over obvious drafts, and close curtains/blinds over any windows (adds a surprising amount of insulation).
3. **Layer clothing in loose layers** rather than one thick layer — trapped air between layers insulates. Cover your head (a huge portion of heat loss) and hands/feet; wear dry socks.
4. **Build a "tent-in-a-room":** drape blankets or a tarp over a clothesline, a doorway frame, or furniture (chairs, a table) to create a small enclosed tent INSIDE the room. This shrinks the air volume you have to warm even further than the room itself — the same principle as the outdoor debris hut, applied indoors.
5. **Insulate from the floor** exactly as outdoors: couch cushions, blankets, and rugs between you and a cold floor or ground-level tile matters as much as blankets on top of you.
6. **Group together.** Sharing a small space with other people (or pets) multiplies the body heat available.
7. **Eat and hydrate.** Your body generates heat by burning calories — being fed helps you make more of your own warmth. Warm (not hot/scalding) drinks help.
8. **If you must add heat, do it safely** (see below) — never skip the carbon monoxide warnings.

### Safe (and unsafe) indoor heat sources
- **Safe-ish with precautions:** a properly maintained, vented gas fireplace or wood stove designed for indoor use, used as intended with its flue open; battery-powered heated blankets/hand warmers; your own body heat in a tented small space.
- ⚠️ **NEVER burn charcoal, run a gasoline/propane generator, use a camp stove/grill, or run a car engine INSIDE a house, garage, tent, or any enclosed space — even with a window cracked.** These produce carbon monoxide (CO), a colorless, odorless gas that kills. Every year, people die doing exactly this during power outages. Generators must run outdoors, at least 20 feet from the house, away from windows/doors/vents.
- If you have a battery or solar-powered **carbon monoxide detector**, use it. Symptoms of CO poisoning — headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion — can be mistaken for flu; if multiple household members feel the same symptoms at once, get everyone into fresh air immediately.
- A single well-ventilated candle for light is generally low-risk for CO in a normal-sized room, but multiple candles in a small sealed tent-in-a-room add up — keep an eye on it, and prioritize insulation over flame for warmth.

### Staying cool indoors without power (critical for North Texas summer)
1. **Move to the lowest level of the building** — a basement if you have one, or the ground floor. Heat rises, so upper floors and attics become dangerously hot first (attics can exceed 140°F/60°C).
2. **Close blinds/curtains on sun-facing windows** during the day; open windows at night if outdoor temps drop and it's safe/secure to do so, to flush hot air out and pull cooler night air in.
3. **Minimize activity during peak heat** (typically noon-6 PM in Texas summer). Do chores/errands early morning or evening instead.
4. **Wet cloth on skin** (neck, wrists, forehead) speeds evaporative cooling — this is one of the most effective no-power cooling tricks. Re-wet as it dries.
5. **Cross-ventilate** if outside air is cooler than inside: open windows on opposite sides of a room/house to create airflow.
6. **Reduce your own heat load:** loose, light-colored, breathable clothing; avoid cooking with the oven/stove (adds heat to the house); avoid alcohol/caffeine (worsen dehydration).
7. **Hydrate proactively** — don't wait for thirst in serious heat; see the [Water guide](../water/water.md).
8. **If a car AC or a public cooling center (library, mall, community center — often kept open/generator-powered during outages) is available, use it**, even for a few hours, to reset your body temperature.
9. Watch every household member (especially infants, elderly, and pets) for heat exhaustion signs — see Safety section.

## Bedding & insulation — what to use, how thick
Regardless of which shelter you build, ground insulation follows the same rules everywhere:
- **Dry material only** — wet leaves/grass conduct heat away almost as fast as bare ground; if your gathered debris is damp, spread it to dry near a fire first if you can.
- **Loft matters more than weight** — fluffy, uncompressed material traps air (the actual insulator); pack it loosely, don't compress it, and fluff it back up if you shift and flatten it.
- **Minimum thickness:** aim for at least 4-6 inches (10-15 cm) compressed under your body weight for mild conditions; 12+ inches (30 cm) loose (which compresses to less) for cold nights — more is always better here.
- **Improvised options indoors or in a pinch:** flattened cardboard boxes, stacks of newspaper, packing foam, seat cushions, car floor mats, extra clothing you're not wearing.
- **Layer order when lying down (outdoors, cold):** ground → insulating bed (leaves/boughs/cardboard) → any pad/tarp you have → your body → blanket/bag/clothing over you. Skipping the insulating bed is one of the most common and most dangerous shelter mistakes.

## Regional & scenario variants

**Urban vs. rural:**
- **Urban/suburban:** Prioritize sheltering IN your existing structure (Section above) over building outdoors — you likely have four walls and a roof already; the job is retaining/rejecting heat, not building shelter from scratch. If you must shelter outdoors in a city (vehicle breakdown, evacuation), a car provides real wind/rain protection — insulate the seats/floor the same as you would ground, and never run the engine in a closed garage for heat.
- **Rural/wilderness:** More raw material (branches, leaves) but less existing infrastructure — the natural builds (Methods 1, 3, 4) are your main tools. Take extra care with site selection since help may be farther away.

**Hot / desert climates:**
- Shade is more valuable than shelter walls — even a simple tarp strung horizontally overhead (no need to touch the ground) blocks direct sun and can drop the felt temperature dramatically. Raise it several feet off the ground with airflow underneath rather than building a sealed low tent, which traps heat.
- Light-colored or reflective material (space blanket, reflective side out) reduces radiant heat gain.
- Dig down slightly for a resting spot in extreme heat — a few inches below grade is cooler than the surface, similar to how basements stay cool.
- Travel/work in early morning, late evening, or night; rest in shade during peak hours.

**Cold climates:**
- Wind is often more dangerous than the raw temperature (wind chill). Prioritize a windbreak first, then insulation, then a heat source.
- Snow, properly used (Methods 5-6), is an excellent insulator — don't fear building in it, but respect the ventilation and hardening-time rules.
- Change out of sweaty clothing before it can chill you — exertion from building a shelter can leave you wet, which becomes dangerous once you stop moving.

**North Texas note:**
- Summers regularly bring 100°F+ (38°C+) days with high humidity and heat index over 105°F — treat heat illness as the primary shelter risk most of the year, more so than cold. Prioritize shade, airflow, and the lowest level of any building.
- Flash flooding is a serious regional risk — flat blackland prairie means water moves fast across the land during storms; never shelter in a low spot, dry creek bed, or drainage ditch, even if it looks dry and inviting.
- Winters bring occasional hard freezes and ice storms (e.g., the February 2021 Texas freeze) that catch residents underprepared for cold; homes here are often built and insulated for heat, not cold, so sealing a small room and layering clothing (see Urban section) matters more here than in a place built for winter.
- Severe thunderstorms/tornadoes are a regional hazard distinct from routine shelter — for tornado-specific sheltering (interior room, lowest floor, away from windows), see the [Regional North Texas guide](../regional-north-texas/regional-north-texas.md) and [Disaster-specific guide].
- Trees here (post oak, cedar/juniper) can supply lean-to and debris-hut material; open pasture land often has little natural cover, so plan on carrying a tarp if you'll be away from tree lines.

## ⚠️ Safety — warnings & mistakes to avoid
- **Never skip ground insulation.** The single most common fatal mistake is a "great" overhead shelter with the person lying on bare ground or bare snow — you can lose more heat down than you gain from a roof.
- **Never run a generator, grill, camp stove, or any fuel-burning device indoors, in a garage, or in an enclosed tent** — carbon monoxide is odorless and kills quickly. Keep such devices 20+ feet from any structure, doors, and windows.
- **Never camp under dead branches or leaning dead trees** ("widowmakers") — they fall with no warning, in calm weather as easily as in wind.
- **Never shelter in low ground, dry creek beds, ravines, or drainage ditches** — cold air pools there at night, and they can flash-flood even from rain falling far away, especially in North Texas.
- **Do not build a fire touching or under a tarp** — most tarps melt or ignite; keep flames several feet away and always attended.
- **Do not enter a quinzhee before it has hardened** (1-2+ hours minimum) — an unset mound can collapse and trap or suffocate the digger.
- **Always keep a ventilation gap in any fully enclosed snow shelter** — even without a flame, exhaled CO2 can build up in a sealed space; never fully seal every opening.
- **Recognize hypothermia and heatstroke early** — confusion is often the first sign in BOTH conditions, and a confused person cannot reliably help themselves; a companion noticing slurred speech, stumbling, or irrational behavior should act immediately (warm/cool the person, seek help) rather than wait.
- **Change wet clothing as soon as possible in cold weather** — wet fabric loses insulating value and accelerates heat loss even in mild temperatures.
- **Do not underestimate heat.** Heatstroke can kill within hours; a hot car interior can be lethal to a child or pet in minutes, not hours. Never leave anyone in an unventilated closed vehicle in the sun.
- **Test your shelter before you truly need it, if time allows** — a quick check (does the tarp hold in wind, does the wall block a flashlight beam, do you feel the ground through your bedding) catches failures while you can still fix them.

## Troubleshooting
| Problem | Fix |
|---|---|
| Still cold despite an overhead shelter | Check ground insulation first — thin or compressed bedding is the #1 cause; add more loose, dry material underneath. |
| Tarp keeps collapsing in wind | Re-tension the ridgeline (taut-line hitch); lower the profile; add more stakes/rocks at the edges; angle the closed end into the wind. |
| Debris hut roof lets light/rain through | Not thick enough — add more debris, shingled from bottom up, until a hand can't easily push through from outside. |
| Can't find two trees the right distance apart for a ridgeline | Use one tree plus a sturdy planted pole, or two crossed tripod poles lashed together, as anchor points. |
| Snow shelter interior is dripping/wet | Normal once it warms above freezing inside; smooth the ceiling so drips run to the walls instead of falling on you, or brush frost off before it can melt. |
| No natural debris available (open field, urban lot) | Rely on a tarp/plastic sheet method (1-2), or a vehicle, or shelter-in-place indoors instead of a natural build. |
| Room won't hold heat despite sealing | Check for the coldest surface (usually windows/floor) and add another insulating layer there specifically; shrink the space further with a tent-in-a-room. |
| Someone is confused, stumbling, or shivering has stopped in the cold | Suspect hypothermia — get them dry, insulated from the ground, and warmed gradually; treat as a medical emergency. See the [First Aid guide](../first-aid/first-aid.md). |
| Someone stops sweating, is hot/confused in extreme heat | Suspect heatstroke — move to shade/cool area immediately, cool the skin with wet cloth/water, fan them, and treat as a medical emergency. See the [First Aid guide](../first-aid/first-aid.md). |
| Quinzhee mound collapsed during digging | It wasn't hardened long enough or walls were dug too thin unevenly — start over, mix snow more thoroughly when piling, and wait longer before hollowing. |

## Quick-reference checklist
- [ ] Scouted the site: no widowmakers overhead, not in a low spot/creek bed, checked for ant/wasp nests, noted wind direction, water nearby but not in the flood path.
- [ ] Chose a build matching materials and climate (tarp A-frame/lean-to, debris hut, branch lean-to, snow trench/quinzhee, or shelter-in-place indoors).
- [ ] Built shelter SMALL — just big enough for body and gear, not walk-in size.
- [ ] Ground insulation is at least 4-6 inches thick (more for cold) UNDER the body — never lying on bare ground/snow.
- [ ] Closed/faced the shelter opening away from prevailing wind (or toward a fire, safely separated).
- [ ] If indoors: sealed one small room, layered clothing, considered a tent-in-a-room, insulated the floor.
- [ ] If using any heat source: confirmed it is rated/safe for indoor use and ventilated — no generators/grills/charcoal indoors, ever.
- [ ] If extreme heat: identified shade and the lowest/coolest area of the building; have wet cloth and water ready; planned activity for early morning/evening.
- [ ] Know the early signs of hypothermia and heatstroke in yourself and others, and what to do about each.
- [ ] Tested the shelter (wind, light-through-walls, felt the ground through bedding) before relying on it overnight.
