## Read this first

When disaster strikes, cell networks fail in three ways, not one: cell towers
lose commercial power and run out of backup battery (usually 4–8 hours);
towers that still work get so congested with call attempts that nothing gets
through ("fast busy" or endless spinning); and physical damage (flooding,
wind, fire, ice) takes towers offline directly. The internet and landlines
fail for the same underlying reasons — they ride on the same power grid and
much of the same fiber/cell backhaul.

The single most important idea in this guide: **separate receiving
information from sending information.** You can almost always receive
information cheaply and reliably with a battery/hand-crank radio. Sending
information to other people, or getting help to come to you, is much harder
and requires different tools depending on distance. Read this guide in order:
first learn to listen (NOAA/AM/FM), then learn to talk short distance (FRS/
GMRS/CB/MURS), then understand the long-distance backbone (amateur/HAM
radio), then build your family's plan, then learn the no-tech fallback
(whistles, boards, runners).

Do this today, before you need it: buy or dig out a hand-crank or battery
NOAA weather radio, put fresh batteries in it, and write your family's
out-of-area contact's phone number on a card in every household member's
wallet/backpack. That single step covers the two most common failures.

## Why this matters

In almost every disaster after-action report — hurricanes, earthquakes,
wildfires, ice storms, tornado outbreaks — the two recurring communication
failures are: (1) people didn't know an official warning had been issued
until it was too late, and (2) family members could not reach each other and
panicked, sometimes driving into the hazard to search for each other. Both
failures are preventable with inexpensive equipment and a plan made in
advance. Communication is not a luxury item in a disaster kit — it is what
turns "we don't know what's happening" into "we know the tornado warning
covers our county until 4:15pm and Mom already reached the out-of-area
contact, so we execute the plan."

## Key terms

- **EAS (Emergency Alert System)** — the system that lets national, state,
  and local officials interrupt TV and radio broadcasts with emergency
  messages.
- **WEA (Wireless Emergency Alert)** — the loud, distinct-tone alert your
  cell phone can receive even without a data/voice plan, as long as the
  tower itself is up and your phone is registered to a network.
- **NOAA Weather Radio (NWR)** — a nationwide network of stations broadcasting
  continuous weather info and All Hazards alerts on 7 dedicated VHF
  frequencies, unrelated to AM/FM broadcast radio.
- **FRS (Family Radio Service)** — short-range, license-free two-way "walkie
  talkie" radios, typically good for real-world ranges of a few hundred feet
  to about a mile.
- **GMRS (General Mobile Radio Service)** — similar frequency band to FRS but
  higher power, larger antennas, and repeater use allowed; requires a paid
  FCC license (no test) that covers your whole family.
- **CB (Citizens Band) radio** — license-free, 40 channels around 27 MHz,
  popular with truckers/off-roaders, several miles of range depending on
  terrain and antenna.
- **MURS (Multi-Use Radio Service)** — license-free VHF band, 5 channels,
  a middle ground between FRS and business radios.
- **Amateur ("HAM") radio** — a licensed radio service with enormous
  flexibility (many bands, high power, repeaters, long-distance modes) that
  forms the backbone of volunteer emergency communications (ARES/RACES) in
  most U.S. disasters.
- **Repeater** — a fixed station, usually on a hilltop or tower, that
  receives a weak signal and re-transmits it at high power, dramatically
  extending the range of handheld radios.
- **Simplex** — direct radio-to-radio communication with no repeater in
  between; shorter range but does not depend on any infrastructure.
- **ICE contact** — "In Case of Emergency" contact stored in a phone and
  written on paper, used by responders/strangers to reach your family.

## What you need

Minimum (every household):
- Battery and/or hand-crank/solar NOAA weather radio with an alert/alarm
  feature (look for "S.A.M.E." capability, described below)
- Spare batteries (or verified working hand crank/solar) for the radio
- A written, laminated or paper family communication plan card for every
  family member
- A basic list of local emergency numbers, hospital, and the out-of-area
  contact's number (memorize it too — phones die)
- A whistle per person (signaling, covered in the Signaling & Rescue guide)

Recommended (better preparedness):
- A pair or more of FRS/GMRS radios for household/neighborhood use
- GMRS license ($35 for 10 years, covers your immediate family, no test) if
  you buy GMRS-capable radios
- A CB radio if you have a vehicle-based bug-out plan or live somewhere with
  active CB-using neighbors (rural areas, trucking routes)
- A dedicated battery bank or solar charger reserved only for phones/radios
- A basic paper map of your area with meeting points marked

Advanced (serious preparedness):
- An amateur (HAM) radio license (Technician class — a weekend of study, one
  easy multiple-choice exam, no Morse code required) and a dual-band
  2m/70cm handheld radio
- A list of local repeater frequencies and your county's ARES/RACES net
  information
- A shortwave receiver for long-distance news/broadcast reception when local
  stations are off the air

## Step 1: Receiving information — radios that just listen

### 1a. NOAA Weather Radio — buy this first

NOAA Weather Radio is a network of transmitters, run by the National Weather
Service, that broadcasts weather forecasts and All Hazards alerts (severe
weather, tornado warnings, flash floods, but also non-weather emergencies
like chemical spills and AMBER alerts in many areas) 24/7 on 7 dedicated
VHF frequencies. These are NOT the same frequencies as FM radio — you need a
receiver built for this band, sold cheaply everywhere as a "NOAA weather
radio" or "emergency weather radio."

**Why it matters:** it runs on its own network, independent of cell towers
and internet, and most units include a loud alarm feature that will wake you
up or interrupt other activity the instant a warning is issued for your
county — you do not have to be actively listening.

The 7 NOAA Weather Radio frequencies (MHz):

```
Channel   Frequency
  WX1       162.550
  WX2       162.400
  WX3       162.475
  WX4       162.425
  WX5       162.450
  WX6       162.500
  WX7       162.525
```

Your radio will scan these automatically, or you pick whichever comes in
clearest at your location — reception depends on which transmitter is
nearest you, not on the channel number.

**S.A.M.E. alerting.** Most modern NOAA radios support Specific Area Message
Encoding (S.A.M.E.): you program in your county's 6-digit FIPS code, and the
radio stays silent until an alert is issued specifically for your county,
then sounds a loud tone and speaks the message. This avoids constant false
alarms from alerts meant for other counties. Find your county's FIPS code
printed in the radio's manual index or via the National Weather Service
website before you need it (and write it down — you may not have internet
when you need to look it up).

### 1b. AM/FM broadcast radio

Ordinary AM and FM stations are required to carry EAS alerts, and many local
AM stations serve as a county's designated emergency broadcast station.
AM signals travel farther at night and bend around terrain better than FM,
so a basic AM/FM radio with fresh batteries is a reliable second listening
tool. Local news/talk AM stations are usually the best source of
"what's actually happening in my town right now" information, since NOAA
Weather Radio only covers weather and a narrow set of other hazards.

### 1c. Shortwave

Shortwave receivers pick up longer-range broadcasts (international
broadcasters, some emergency and ham traffic) that can travel hundreds to
thousands of miles by bouncing off the upper atmosphere. This is a "nice to
have," not a priority purchase — most beginners get more value from a good
NOAA/AM/FM radio first. Shortwave is most useful when local stations are
knocked off air entirely (major regional disaster) and you need any news at
all.

## Step 2: Two-way radios — talking to people nearby

Two-way radios let you talk, not just listen. Choose based on distance and
whether you're willing to get a license.

```
Service   License?      Typical real-world range      Best for
-------   -----------   ----------------------------  -------------------------
FRS       None          0.25–1 mile (buildings/hills   Family in same building/
                         cut this a lot)                campsite/neighborhood
GMRS      Yes ($35/10yr, ~1–5 miles simplex; 20+ miles   Neighborhood/community,
          no test)      via repeater                    farm/ranch operations
CB        None          1–5 miles ground, more with     Vehicle convoys, rural
                         good antenna/terrain            roads, trucker channels
MURS      None          1–2 miles                       Business-like local use
HAM       Yes (exam     Simplex: 1–5 miles handheld;     Long-distance and
          required)     via repeater: 20–50+ miles       emergency-network backbone
```

Realistic range depends enormously on terrain, buildings, antenna height,
and weather — treat the numbers above as "flat open ground, no obstacles"
best cases. In a hilly or heavily built-up area, cut them by half or more.

### FRS (walkie-talkies)

These are the cheap radios sold in twin-packs at any big box store. No
license needed, legal for anyone to use, and good for keeping track of
family members within a building, campground, neighborhood block, or hiking
group. Buy a set now, learn the channel/privacy-code system, and give one to
each family member during any situation where you might get separated
(evacuation, crowded venue, disaster response).

### GMRS

Physically similar radios to FRS but with higher allowed power and the
ability to use repeaters, which multiplies range. One license (an
individual's name) legally covers that person's immediate family, and it is
inexpensive with no test required — just an application and fee to the FCC.
If your household wants a serious "talk across the neighborhood or farm"
radio and doesn't want to study for the HAM exam, GMRS is the easiest step
up from FRS.

### CB radio

An older, still-legal, no-license service on 40 channels around 27 MHz.
Popular with truckers and off-roaders, and useful in rural areas or along
highways where CB culture is still active — you may be able to reach other
travelers or truckers for help even if you own no other radio gear. Channel
9 is the traditional emergency-monitoring channel in the U.S.; Channel 19 is
the common trucker/highway chatter channel.

### MURS

A lesser-known, license-free VHF service with only 5 channels, popular for
short-range business and security use. Worth knowing about if you find
inexpensive MURS gear, but not a priority purchase for most households.

## Step 3: Amateur (HAM) radio — the emergency backbone

When phones, internet, and even FRS/GMRS aren't enough — because you need
real distance, or because you need to reach organized emergency responders —
amateur radio is what actually works, and it is what volunteer emergency
groups (ARES, RACES, and local Community Emergency Response Teams) use to
relay information between shelters, hospitals, and emergency operations
centers when normal infrastructure is down.

**Why it's the backbone:** amateur radio operators can use far higher power
than FRS/GMRS, a huge range of frequencies (including bands that bounce off
the atmosphere for national/international range), and a nationwide network
of volunteer-maintained repeaters — many with independent battery/solar
backup power — that stay on the air after commercial infrastructure fails.

**Licensing.** In the U.S., a license (the entry-level "Technician" class)
is normally required to transmit on amateur bands. It requires passing a
multiple-choice written exam (no Morse code needed anymore); study guides
and practice exams are widely available, and most beginners pass after a
weekend of study. Getting licensed now, before a disaster, means you already
know the etiquette, local repeaters, and how to operate your radio when it
counts.

**The emergency exception.** Under FCC rules, in a genuine emergency
involving immediate danger to life or property, any person may use any
means of radio communication necessary — including amateur equipment and
frequencies — to get or give help, licensed or not. This is a last-resort
legal safety valve, not a substitute for getting licensed; know it exists,
but don't treat it as a reason to skip training.

**Bands to know:** the two most useful for local/regional emergency use are
the 2-meter (VHF, roughly 144–148 MHz) and 70-centimeter (UHF, roughly
420–450 MHz) bands, because handheld radios are cheap, small, and reach
local repeaters well. National calling frequencies exist by convention
(146.520 MHz is the widely recognized 2-meter simplex calling frequency in
the U.S.) as a place to listen for or make first contact when you don't know
a local repeater's frequency.

**Get started cheaply:** an inexpensive dual-band (2m/70cm) handheld radio,
a list of your county's active repeaters (available from local ham clubs or
repeater directories), and the Technician license are enough to make you a
genuinely useful link in your community's emergency communication chain.

## Step 4: Build a family communication plan

Do this exercise with your whole household before you need it.

1. **Pick an out-of-area contact.** Choose a relative or friend who lives in
   a different region/state, not likely to be affected by the same
   disaster. During a local disaster, local phone circuits are often jammed
   while long-distance/out-of-area calls sometimes still connect —
   everyone in the family calls or texts this one person to check in, which
   is far more reliable than everyone trying to reach each other directly.
2. **Write it down and distribute it.** Every family member — including
   kids — carries a card (wallet, backpack, laminated tag) with the
   out-of-area contact's name and number, plus two local meeting points.
3. **Pick two meeting points.** One near home (e.g., a neighbor's driveway
   or a mailbox at the end of the street) for a sudden local hazard like a
   fire, and one farther away (e.g., a relative's house or a specific
   landmark outside the neighborhood) in case you cannot return home at
   all.
4. **List ICE (In Case of Emergency) contacts** in every phone's contacts
   app under "ICE" so first responders and strangers can find someone to
   call if you're incapacitated.
5. **Practice it.** Run a 10-minute family drill twice a year: everyone
   pretends phones don't work, walks or bikes to the near meeting point,
   and states out loud what they'd do next.
6. **Include out-of-town-relative texting** as the default channel (see
   below — text works when calls don't).

## Step 5: Understand official emergency alerts

- **EAS (Emergency Alert System)** interrupts TV and radio broadcasts with
  official warnings (weather, AMBER alerts, civil emergencies). You'll hear
  the distinctive attention tone followed by a spoken message. This is why
  keeping a battery radio on, even quietly, during a threat period is worth
  it.
- **WEA (Wireless Emergency Alerts)** are the loud, distinctively-toned
  alerts your phone can receive directly from cell towers, targeted to your
  current location, for imminent threats (tornado warnings, flash floods,
  AMBER alerts, presidential alerts). Critically, WEA messages arrive over
  the cellular network's broadcast channel, separate from voice/data — they
  can often get through even when the network is too congested for normal
  calls or texts, as long as the tower itself has power and is on the air.
  You do not need a data plan, a contract, or even a SIM card registered to
  a carrier billing account for basic emergency alert and 911 functionality
  in most cases (see Step 6).
- Both systems are designed to work with **zero action from you** other
  than having a working radio or a phone with a signal — but a phone with no
  signal receives nothing, which is exactly why a battery/hand-crank radio
  is the more dependable backstop.

## Step 6: Phone tricks when signal is weak

Even when voice calls won't connect, your phone may still be useful:

1. **Text uses far less signal than a voice call** and is more likely to get
   through congested or damaged networks — send a text before trying to
   call, and prefer text for check-ins during a disaster.
2. **911 is designed to work under degraded conditions.** In the U.S.,
   phones are generally required to attempt 911 calls on any available
   carrier's network, without a SIM, without a service plan, and without a
   phone unlock code — call 911 even from an old "spare" phone with no
   active plan, or from a phone showing "no service" (it may still find an
   emergency-only signal).
3. **Turn off nonessential apps and background data** to conserve both
   battery and the limited bandwidth available, improving your odds of a
   text or call getting through.
4. **Move to higher ground or near a window** — cell signal is
  line-of-sight sensitive; a few dozen feet can matter.
5. **Conserve phone battery** — turn on airplane mode when not actively
   trying to communicate, dim the screen, and use it as your last-resort
   backup, not your primary tool, once other radios are running.

## Step 7: Low-tech neighbor signaling

When you have no working radio at all, simple pre-agreed signals let
neighbors check on each other without power or batteries:

- **Whistle codes:** 3 short blasts is the universal distress signal
  ("I need help"); 1 long blast can mean "I'm okay, checking in"; agree on
  your household's/neighborhood's specific codes in advance. (See the
  Signaling & Rescue guide for a fuller code table.)
- **Message boards:** a whiteboard, taped paper sign, or chalk message on a
  door or mailbox — "OK, at Aunt Sue's, back Sunday" — lets returning family
  or worried neighbors know status without anyone having to wait around.
- **Colored flag or cloth system:** e.g., green cloth on the door means "all
  okay, no help needed," red means "need help/check on us" — agree on colors
  with your immediate neighbors ahead of time.
- **Runners:** in a true no-communication situation, a physically fit person
  walking or biking to a neighbor's house, a fire station, or a known
  gathering point is a legitimate, low-tech option — treat it like a
  wilderness "send for help" decision (see the Navigation guide for
  safe-travel basics).

## Regional & scenario variants

- **Urban:** dense buildings badly degrade FRS/GMRS/CB range but usually
  offer more amateur radio repeaters and more numerous AM/FM stations;
  cell congestion after a mass event is typically severe — texting and WEA
  are your best phone-based bets.
- **Suburban:** generally the best-case scenario for FRS/GMRS neighborhood
  networks; organize a block-level radio check-in plan with neighbors now.
- **Rural:** CB radio culture is often still active along rural highways;
  amateur radio repeater coverage can be sparse, so a HAM handheld may only
  reach in simplex mode — know your terrain and consider a mobile (vehicle)
  HAM rig with a taller antenna for real distance.
- **NORTH TEXAS NOTE:** North Texas sits in Tornado Alley with a active
  severe-weather season roughly March–June, so a S.A.M.E.-programmed NOAA
  Weather Radio is a higher-than-average priority purchase here — outdoor
  warning sirens in this region are meant for people outdoors and are often
  inaudible indoors, and NOAA radio is frequently the only alert that wakes
  a sleeping household ahead of a nighttime tornado. Ice storms (roughly
  December–February) are the other major regional threat and can knock out
  power and cell service simultaneously across whole counties for days;
  keep radio batteries and a hand-crank option on hand for both seasons.
  The Dallas–Fort Worth metroplex has an active amateur radio and ARES/RACES
  community with strong repeater coverage — a good local resource for
  training and mutual aid.

## ⚠️ Safety

- Never rely on a phone alone as your only emergency communication tool —
  towers fail, batteries die, and networks congest exactly when you need
  them most.
- Do not transmit on amateur or GMRS frequencies without the appropriate
  license except in a genuine life-threatening emergency — misuse can bring
  fines and, more importantly, can interfere with real emergency traffic.
- Keep spare, correctly-sized batteries for every radio, stored separately
  from the radios themselves (removed batteries prevent corrosion damage
  during long storage).
- Do not use a hand-crank radio's crank mechanism to try to directly charge
  a phone unless the unit specifically supports it — check the manual, as
  forcing this can damage both devices.
- If broadcasting on CB or FRS/GMRS during an actual emergency, keep
  transmissions short, state your location and the nature of the emergency
  clearly, then release the channel so others can respond or relay.
- During lightning/severe storms, avoid using corded landline phones and
  keep antennas of handheld radios away from your face when thunderstorms
  are directly overhead.
- Never give out a household's real-time location and vulnerability details
  (e.g., "we are alone, no working locks") over open, unencrypted radio
  channels like FRS/GMRS/CB, which anyone can monitor.

## Troubleshooting

- **NOAA radio has power but plays only static:** you're likely on the
  wrong WX channel for your area, or too far from the nearest transmitter;
  cycle through all 7 channels and try moving near a window or outdoors.
- **NOAA radio never alarms during a known warning:** check that
  S.A.M.E. is programmed with the correct county FIPS code, and that
  "alert" mode (not just "weather" mode) is switched on.
- **FRS/GMRS radios not reaching each other at expected range:** confirm
  both radios are on the same channel AND the same privacy/sub-code (a
  common beginner mistake); move to higher ground or remove obstructions;
  remember stated "mile" ranges assume flat open terrain.
- **Cell phone shows full bars but calls won't connect:** this usually means
  tower congestion, not a coverage problem — hang up, send a text instead,
  and wait a few minutes before retrying a call.
- **Cell phone shows "no service" but you need 911:** try dialing anyway;
  phones will attempt to route emergency calls through any available
  carrier's tower, even ones you're not subscribed to.
- **Amateur handheld can't reach a known repeater:** check you've
  programmed the correct offset and PL/CTCSS tone for that repeater (found
  in local repeater directories), and that terrain isn't blocking
  line-of-sight to the repeater site.
