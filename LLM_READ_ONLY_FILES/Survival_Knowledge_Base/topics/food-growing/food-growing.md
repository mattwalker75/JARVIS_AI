# Growing Food for Survival — From Windowsill Sprouts to a Full Garden

## Read this first (the 30-second version)
1. **Foraged and stored food runs out. Grown food renews itself** — a garden is the only "food source" that keeps producing as long as you tend it. Start now, even in a crisis; most crops take weeks, not days.
2. **Fastest wins for nutrition:** **sprouted seeds in a jar** are ready in **2–5 days** with zero soil, zero sun. **Leafy greens** (lettuce, spinach, kale) are ready in **25–35 days**. **Radishes** in **21–30 days**.
3. **Highest-calorie crops** (what actually keeps you fed long-term): **potatoes, sweet potatoes, dry beans, winter squash, corn** — these store well and deliver real calories, not just vitamins.
4. **You don't need a farm.** A **5-gallon bucket, a window, a few containers, or a 4×8 ft raised bed** can produce meaningful food. Start small, expand as you learn.
5. **Water and soil are the two things that make or break a garden.** Improve poor soil with compost; water deeply and less often; mulch everything.
6. **North Texas specific:** the native clay soil is difficult but fixable, summers are brutally hot and dry, and the planting calendar has two main windows (spring and fall) — see the Regional section.

The rest of this guide walks a total beginner through **seven methods** of growing food (from a jar on a counter to a full in-ground plot), soil building, watering with limited water, pest control without chemicals, seed saving, and season extension — with a detailed North Texas planting calendar.

---

## Why this matters
Stored food is finite. Foraged food is unpredictable and requires skill and travel. **Grown food is the only calorie source you can scale, plan, and renew indefinitely** with nothing but seed, sun, soil, and water — all four of which are available almost everywhere, including a city apartment windowsill. In a short-term emergency (days to weeks), fast crops like sprouts and greens deliver vitamins and freshness that stored food lacks (scurvy and other deficiencies show up within weeks on canned/dry food alone). In a long-term situation (a season or longer), calorie-dense staples — potatoes, beans, squash, corn — become the difference between surviving and merely delaying hunger. Growing food also reduces dependence on supply chains, gives you something productive to do that supports mental health, and — critically — **teaches skills and builds soil that only get better every year you keep at it.** A garden started on day one of a crisis is a garden that's feeding you by week four; a garden you wait to start is calories you never get back.

## Key terms (plain language)
- **Direct sow** — planting a seed straight into its final growing spot (vs. starting it elsewhere and transplanting).
- **Transplant** — a young plant (seedling) moved from a pot/tray into its growing bed.
- **Days to maturity (DTM)** — how many days from planting (or transplanting) until you can harvest. Printed on seed packets.
- **Cool-season crop** — tolerates light frost, bolts (flowers and turns bitter) in heat. Examples: lettuce, spinach, kale, peas, radish, broccoli.
- **Warm-season / heat-tolerant crop** — needs warm soil, killed by frost. Examples: tomato, pepper, squash, beans, okra, sweet potato, black-eyed peas.
- **Bolting** — a plant switching to flowering/seed production early (usually from heat or long days), making leaves bitter and tough.
- **Frost date** — the average date of the last spring frost and first fall frost for your area; the safe planting window sits between them for tender crops.
- **Compost** — decomposed organic matter (food scraps, leaves, manure) turned into rich, dark soil food.
- **Amend / amendment** — material worked into soil to improve it (compost, sand, manure, peat/coir).
- **Mulch** — a layer of material (straw, leaves, wood chips) on top of soil to hold moisture, block weeds, and moderate temperature.
- **Hardening off** — gradually exposing indoor-started seedlings to outdoor sun/wind/temperature over 7–10 days before permanent transplant, so they don't die of shock.
- **Open-pollinated (OP) seed** — a variety that, if isolated from other varieties, grows true from its own saved seed (unlike hybrids, labeled "F1").

## What you need
**Minimum to start something today (sprouting):**
- Any dry seeds meant for sprouting/eating (mung beans, lentils, alfalfa, radish, chickpeas, whole raw sunflower seeds) — even seeds from a bag of dry beans in the pantry work.
- A clean wide-mouth jar, a piece of cloth/mesh (or nylon stocking) and a rubber band, or a fine-mesh strainer.

**For container/bed/in-ground growing:**
- **Seeds** (vegetable seeds; save your own once you have a harvest — see Method 7) or **starts/seedlings**.
- **A growing container** of some kind — buckets, totes, grow bags, raised bed frame, or just ground soil.
- **Soil/growing medium** and **compost** or other amendment.
- **Sunlight** — most vegetables need **6+ hours of direct sun** (leafy greens tolerate 4).
- **Water** and something to carry/apply it (watering can, jug with holes poked in the cap, hose).
- **Basic tools**: digging tool (trowel, shovel, or a sturdy stick), something to cut with, gloves if available.
- **Mulch material**: straw, dry leaves, grass clippings, cardboard, wood chips.
- Optional but very helpful: a thermometer, a notebook to track planting/harvest dates, containers for seed saving (envelopes, jars).

# MAIN CONTENT — Building Your Food Supply, Step by Step

## Step-by-step overview (the order to do things in)
1. **Start sprouts today** (Method 1) — food in days, teaches you nothing can go wrong easily.
2. **Assess your space and light** — windowsill, balcony, yard, or field? This decides which methods below apply.
3. **Build or improve your soil** (composting, Method 6) in parallel — it takes weeks to become useful, so start it immediately even before you plant.
4. **Plant fast crops first** — leafy greens and radishes — for quick wins and morale (Methods 2–4, depending on space).
5. **Plant calorie crops** for the long game — potatoes, beans, squash, corn (same methods, different crop choice).
6. **Water wisely and mulch** everything (see the watering section) — this is often the actual limiting factor, not seed.
7. **Watch for pests/disease early** and act at the first sign (Troubleshooting section).
8. **Save seed** from your best plants for the next planting (Method 7) so you are never dependent on a new seed supply.
9. **Extend your season** with covers/cold frames/indoor growing so you aren't shut down by the first frost or the worst heat.

## Method 1: Sprouting seeds in a jar (fastest nutrition — 2 to 5 days, no soil, no sun)
**What it does:** Soaking and rinsing seeds triggers germination; you eat the whole young sprout (seed, root, and shoot) raw or lightly cooked. This is the single fastest way to get fresh vitamin C, folate, and easily-digested protein when you have no growing space at all — it works on a kitchen counter, in a bunker, or on a boat.

**You need:** a wide-mouth jar (quart size is easiest), a piece of breathable mesh/cloth or cheesecloth + rubber band (or a canning-jar ring with mesh instead of a lid), and 2–3 tablespoons of dry sprouting seed (mung bean, lentil, alfalfa, radish, chickpea, whole peas, or whole sunflower seed). **Do not use seeds treated with fungicide/pesticide for planting** — use ones sold as food/sprouting seed.

**Steps:**
1. Put 2–3 tbsp of seed in the jar. Rinse once, drain.
2. Cover with room-temperature water, 2–3 times the volume of the seeds. **Soak 8–12 hours** (overnight is easiest).
3. Drain completely through the mesh. Set the jar on its side, tilted downward, somewhere out of direct sun (a dish rack or bowl works) so it drains and air circulates.
4. **Rinse and drain 2–3 times a day** with clean water — this is the only real "work," and it's the step people skip and then get moldy sprouts. Every rinse, swirl the seeds, drain thoroughly (standing water is what causes rot).
5. **How to tell it's working:** by 24 hours you'll see tiny white root tips ("tails") emerging. By day 2–3, pale shoots appear and the mass roughly doubles or triples in size. Alfalfa/radish sprouts are ready in 3–5 days when 1–2 inches long; mung/lentil sprouts are ready in 2–4 days when the tail is about as long as the seed to double that.
6. When ready, give a final rinse, drain very well (spin in a clean towel if needed — excess water shortens storage life), and refrigerate if possible; eat within a few days. Rinse once daily even in the fridge.

**Signs of trouble:** sour or moldy smell, slimy texture, or fuzzy growth = discard that batch and wash the jar with hot soapy water before the next try (usually caused by not draining fully or rinsing often enough).

## Method 2: Windowsill / indoor container growing (works with zero outdoor space)
**What it does:** Grows real plants to harvest size using a sunny window (south-facing is best in the Northern Hemisphere) or a grow light, in any container that holds soil and drains.

**Best crops:** leafy greens (lettuce, spinach, mustard greens), green onions/scallions (regrow from kitchen scraps — stand root ends in a shallow glass of water, changed daily, and they resprout in days), herbs, microgreens, and radishes if the window gets strong light.

**Steps:**
1. Poke or drill **drainage holes** in the bottom of any food-safe container (yogurt tubs, buckets, plastic totes) — without drainage, roots drown and rot. Set on a tray to catch runoff.
2. Fill with a light growing mix (potting soil, or 2 parts garden soil : 1 part compost : 1 part sand for drainage) to within an inch of the rim.
3. Sow seed at the depth on the packet (rule of thumb: **plant a seed about twice as deep as it is wide**). Very tiny seeds (lettuce) are barely covered — just pressed into the surface.
4. Water gently until it drains from the bottom; keep the top inch of soil consistently moist (not soggy) until seedlings emerge — usually **3–10 days** depending on crop and temperature.
5. Rotate the container a quarter turn every day or two so plants don't lean permanently toward the light.
6. **How to tell it's working:** even, uniform sprouting across the container within the expected window; leaves that are green (not pale yellow, which signals too little light); steady new-leaf growth week over week.
7. Harvest leaf lettuce/greens by cutting outer leaves and letting the center keep growing ("cut and come again") rather than pulling the whole plant — this can give you harvests every few days for weeks from the same planting.

## Method 3: Container gardening outdoors (patios, balconies, driveways, rooftops)
**What it does:** Same principle as Method 2 but outdoors with full sun, in larger containers that support fruiting crops (tomatoes, peppers, squash) as well as greens and roots.

**Steps:**
1. Choose containers by size to crop: **5-gallon buckets** (with drainage holes drilled in the bottom) suit tomatoes, peppers, and potatoes (one plant per bucket); **shallower wide tubs** (8–12 inches deep) suit greens, radishes, and herbs (several plants each).
2. Fill with a mix of compost, garden soil, and something for drainage/aeration (perlite, coarse sand, or even broken-up packing foam in the very bottom).
3. Place where the crop gets its needed sun (6+ hours for fruiting crops, 4+ for greens); in extreme heat, afternoon shade is a bonus, not a problem, for greens.
4. Sow or transplant per the crop's depth/spacing rules; container crops can generally be spaced a bit tighter than in-ground since you control the soil quality directly.
5. Because containers dry out faster than ground soil, check moisture daily by pushing a finger 1–2 inches in — water when it feels dry at that depth.
6. **How to tell it's working:** steady height/leaf growth weekly; flowering on schedule for the variety; no wilting by mid-morning (afternoon wilt in full sun is normal for squash/tomato leaves and usually recovers by evening — wilting that persists into the cool morning means underwatering or root problems).

## Method 4: Raised beds (the best beginner "real garden" method, especially over bad soil)
**What it does:** A framed, soil-filled bed built on top of the ground lets you install **good soil directly** without fighting native dirt — the single best fix for hard clay or rocky ground, and it warms up faster in spring and drains better after rain.

**Steps:**
1. Pick a level, full-sun spot. Mow or cut vegetation short; you can leave a layer of cardboard or several layers of newspaper down first to smother grass/weeds (it breaks down over time and worms love it).
2. Build or place a frame — untreated wood boards, cinder blocks, stacked logs, or even a bed with no frame at all (a raised mound of soil, called a "hügel" or simply a mounded row, works too). A common beginner size is **4 ft × 8 ft, 8–12 inches deep** — 4 ft wide keeps every part reachable from the sides without stepping in the bed (stepping in compacts the soil).
3. Fill with a simple beginner mix, roughly: **half compost/finished manure, half topsoil**, plus a shovelful of sand or coarse material if it seems too dense. (Note: this topsoil-based mix is different from **"Mel's Mix,"** the specific soilless recipe used in square-foot gardening — **⅓ compost, ⅓ peat moss/coir, ⅓ coarse vermiculite, with no topsoil and no sand**. Either approach gives you loose, dark, crumbly soil; don't confuse the two names.)
4. Rake level. Sow/transplant according to spacing rules (see crop table below); a common beginner layout is the **square-foot method** — divide the bed into 1-ft squares and plant a set number of one crop per square (1 tomato; 4 lettuce; 9 bush beans; 16 carrots/radishes) so you don't have to calculate row spacing.
5. Mulch immediately after planting (straw, shredded leaves) 1–2 inches deep, kept an inch back from seedling stems.
6. **How to tell it's working:** germination within the seed packet's stated window; even color and height across the bed (patchy pale areas suggest a low spot in the soil mix or uneven watering); a bed that stays visibly moist a day or two longer than bare native soil after watering (raised beds with good compost hold water far better than clay).

## Method 5: In-ground gardening (largest scale, once soil is workable)
**What it does:** Planting directly into native ground is the lowest-cost, highest-capacity method once you've improved the soil enough to support it — essential for space-hungry calorie crops like corn, dry beans, winter squash, and sweet potatoes at any real scale.

**Steps:**
1. **Test drainage:** dig a hole 1 ft deep and wide, fill with water, let it drain, fill again — if the second fill takes more than a few hours to drain, you have a drainage problem (very common in heavy clay) that needs correcting with amendment and/or raised rows before planting.
2. **Loosen the soil** at least 8–12 inches deep (double-digging or a broadfork/digging fork) rather than just the top few inches — roots (especially potatoes, sweet potatoes, carrots) need depth to expand into.
3. **Amend heavily** with 2–4 inches of compost or aged manure worked into the top 6–8 inches, every season, especially in poor native soil (see the North Texas clay note below — this step is not optional there).
4. Form **raised rows or hills** 4–8 inches high even in-ground; this improves drainage and warms the soil faster, and matters even more in heavy clay.
5. Sow/transplant per spacing (see table). Space larger calorie crops generously — e.g., winter squash and corn need real room to spread or stand in blocks (corn is wind-pollinated and should be planted in **blocks of at least 4×4 plants**, not a single long row, or it won't pollinate well and ears will be poorly filled).
6. **How to tell it's working:** uniform germination in rows; deep green color; visible new growth week to week; for root/tuber crops, foliage that stays vigorous through the growing season (yellowing foliage on potatoes late in the season is normal and signals harvest time, not failure).

**Approximate days-to-maturity and spacing for key crops** (varies by variety — always check the seed packet):

| Crop | Days to maturity | Spacing (plant/row) | Why grow it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Radish | 21–30 days | 1 in / 6 in rows | Fastest root crop, breaks up soil |
| Lettuce/leaf greens | 25–35 days for baby-leaf (cut-and-come-again longer); 45–55 days for full-size heads | 4–6 in / 12 in | Fastest leafy nutrition |
| Spinach, mustard greens | 30–45 days | 4–6 in / 12 in | Cool-season nutrition |
| Bush beans | 50–60 days | 3–4 in / 18–24 in | Fast protein, easy, fixes nitrogen in soil |
| Summer squash/zucchini | 45–55 days | 24–36 in centers | Very high yield per plant |
| Winter squash/pumpkin | 80–110 days | 3–4 ft centers | Stores for months, good calories |
| Sweet potato | 90–120 days | 12–18 in / 3 ft rows | High-calorie storage root, heat-loving |
| Potato (regular) | 70–110 days | 12 in / 30–36 in rows | Highest calories-per-area of common crops |
| Dry beans (pinto, black-eyed pea) | 90–120 days | 4–6 in / 24–30 in | Stores for years, protein |
| Corn | 60–100 days | 8–12 in, blocks of 4+ rows | High calories, needs block planting to pollinate |
| Okra | 55–65 days | 12–18 in / 36 in | Extremely heat/drought tolerant |
| Tomato | 60–85 days from transplant | 24–36 in | Vitamin C, very productive |

## Method 6: Building soil — composting (the engine behind every other method)
**What it does:** Turns kitchen scraps, yard waste, and manure into the single best soil amendment there is — free, renewable, and it improves any soil (clay or sand) by adding organic matter, nutrients, and the microbial life that makes nutrients available to plants.

**Steps (simple "cold" pile — slower but nearly foolproof):**
1. Pick a spot with some shade/wind protection, direct on bare ground (not concrete) so worms and organisms can move in.
2. Layer **"greens"** (nitrogen-rich: vegetable scraps, coffee grounds, fresh grass clippings, manure from plant-eating animals) with **"browns"** (carbon-rich: dry leaves, straw, shredded cardboard/paper, sawdust) at roughly a **1:2 to 1:3 ratio of greens to browns** by volume. Too many greens and no browns = a slimy, smelly pile; too many browns alone barely breaks down.
3. Keep the pile **about as damp as a wrung-out sponge** — water it if it's dry and dusty, add more dry browns if it's soggy/smelly.
4. Turn the pile every 1–2 weeks with a fork or shovel to add air (this is what separates "fast" compost from "slow" compost — turning speeds it up a lot).
5. **How to tell it's working:** the pile heats up noticeably in the middle within days (you can feel this by hand a foot down) — that's the microbes working. **For pathogen safety** (especially if manure is included), the pile's hot core needs to reach **131–170°F**, sustained for at least **15 days with 5 turnings** (turned/windrow method) or **3 days undisturbed** (static aerated-pile method) to reliably kill pathogens like E. coli — a cold pile that never heats up, or one that's left unturned and cool throughout, does not reliably do this. To finish into dark, crumbly, earthy-smelling material with no recognizable scraps: a well-managed hot, turned pile can finish in a couple of months; the simple cold/unturned pile described above realistically takes **6–12 months or longer**.
6. **Never compost:** meat, dairy, oily/greasy food, diseased plants, pet/human waste (attracts pests, can carry pathogens, and cat/dog waste specifically can carry parasites dangerous to humans). Chicken, rabbit, and other herbivore manures are excellent, but treat raw manure as a pathogen risk, not just a plant-burn risk: fully compost it (reaching the pathogen-kill temperatures above) before applying near food crops, or if using raw/aged-but-uncomposted manure directly on soil, **incorporate it at least 120 days before harvest for crops that touch the soil (root vegetables, lettuce, low-growing greens) and at least 90 days before harvest for other crops**.
7. **No yard at all?** A worm bin ("vermicompost") in a lidded tote with drainage holes, bedding (shredded paper), and red wiggler worms turns kitchen scraps into rich castings indoors with almost no smell if maintained correctly (don't overfeed; if it smells bad, you're feeding it too much).

## Method 7: Saving seed (make your garden self-renewing)
**What it does:** Lets you grow next season's crop from this season's best plants — critical in a survival scenario where you may not be able to buy new seed.

**Steps:**
1. Choose **open-pollinated (OP)** varieties when possible (not hybrid/"F1") — OP seed grows true to the parent plant; hybrid seed often does not.
2. Let the fruit/pod **fully ripen** past the point you'd normally eat it (a cucumber for seed goes yellow and fat; beans/peas are left to dry and rattle in the pod on the plant).
3. For dry seeds (beans, peas, lettuce, radish gone to seed): let pods/seed heads dry on the plant, then thresh (crush/rub) to separate seed from chaff, and winnow (pour between containers in a breeze, or blow gently) to remove the light debris.
4. For wet-seeded fruit (tomato, squash, cucumber): scoop seeds with pulp into a jar of water, let sit 2–4 days to ferment (this kills some seed-borne disease and separates good seed, which sinks, from pulp and bad seed, which float) — stir daily, then rinse and dry.
5. **Dry seed thoroughly** on a plate or screen (not paper towel — seeds stick) out of direct sun for a week or more before storing, or they'll mold in storage.
6. Store in paper envelopes or jars, **labeled with crop, variety, and year**, somewhere cool, dark, and dry; most vegetable seed stays viable 2–5 years stored this way. **Exceptions: onion, leek, and parsnip seed lose viability fast and are only reliably good for 1–2 years — grow or trade these seeds sooner, or replenish them more often.** A pinch of dry rice or a silica packet in the jar helps absorb residual moisture.
7. **Keep varieties separate** if you want pure seed — some crops (squash, corn) cross-pollinate easily between varieties if grown close together; isolate by distance or timing, or simply accept some variation if purity isn't critical for survival food.

## Watering wisely with limited water
- **Water deeply and infrequently** rather than a little every day — this trains roots to grow deep, making plants far more drought-tolerant. A good soak that wets 6–8 inches down, then let the top inch or two dry before watering again, beats daily shallow sprinkling.
- **Mulch, always.** A 2–3 inch layer of straw, shredded leaves, or wood chips over soil can cut water needs dramatically by blocking evaporation and keeping soil temperature down — this is the single highest-value, lowest-cost water-saving step available.
- **Water at soil level, not overhead**, and **in early morning** — this reduces evaporation loss and reduces the wet-leaf conditions that breed fungal disease. A simple drip system can be improvised from a bottle: poke 1–2 small holes in the cap of a plastic bottle, bury the bottle neck-down next to a plant, fill it, and it slowly drips water to the roots over hours.
- **Greywater (used household water) caution:** lightly used water (e.g., rinse water with no grease, harsh soap, or bleach) can be used on **non-edible parts of ornamental plants or fruit trees at the root zone**, but avoid greywater with soap/detergent, grease, or food residue on **edible leafy greens or root crops you'll eat raw or unpeeled**, since residues and pathogens can be taken up or left on the surface. When in doubt, don't put greywater directly on anything you'll eat without cooking, and never on sprouts/microgreens.
- **Catch rainwater** off any roof into barrels/buckets for garden use — this is clean, free water and should be your first choice for irrigation before using drinking-quality stored water on plants.
- **Group plants by water need** so you're not over- or under-watering some to satisfy others (e.g., don't plant thirsty lettuce right next to drought-tough okra and water them the same amount).

## Regional & scenario variants

### North Texas (blackland prairie clay, hot dry summers) — detailed growing note
North Texas (Dallas–Fort Worth and surrounding towns such as Anna, McKinney, and Sherman) sits on **blackland prairie**: a heavy, dark clay soil that is naturally fertile but **drains poorly, cracks hard when dry, and turns to sticky "gumbo" mud when wet**. Summers are hot (afternoon highs regularly 95–105°F/35–40°C, July–August) with periodic drought; winters are mild with occasional hard freezes.

**Fixing blackland clay:**
- **Never work clay soil when it's wet** — digging or tilling wet clay destroys its structure and creates hard clods that take seasons to undo. Work it when it crumbles rather than smears.
- Add **2–4 inches of compost** worked into the top 6–8 inches every season; over a few years this transforms clay from concrete-like to workable loam. Add **coarse sand or expanded shale** (not fine sand, which can make clay worse) to improve drainage further if needed.
- **Raised beds are strongly recommended** as the default method here — building 8–12 inches of good soil on top of the clay sidesteps years of amendment and gives immediate results, while the clay below still helps retain moisture in summer.
- Mulch heavily (3 inches) — it's doing double duty here: conserving scarce water and preventing the bare clay surface from baking into a hard crust.

**Frost dates and planting windows:**
- **Average last spring frost:** roughly **mid-to-late March** (around March 15–25 for the DFW area, a bit later — early April — moving north toward the Red River).
- **Average first fall frost:** roughly **late November** (around November 21–22 for the DFW/Anna/Collin County area).
- This gives roughly a **245–250 day frost-free growing season**, but the real limiter is **summer heat**, not frost: many cool-season crops and even tomato/pepper fruit-set shut down when nights stay above ~75°F (24°C).

**Two-season planting calendar:**
- **Cool-season crops (spring window: mid-February to March; fall window: September):** lettuce, spinach, kale, mustard greens, radish, carrots, beets, English peas, broccoli, cabbage. Spring-planted greens should go in **as early as the soil can be worked** (mid-Feb, with frost protection ready) because they bolt fast once May heat arrives; the **fall planting (September)** is often more reliable in North Texas since the crop matures into cooling, not warming, weather.
- **Warm-season crops (plant after last frost, roughly early-mid April through May):** tomato, pepper, squash, cucumber, bush/pole beans, and the heat-tolerant standouts for this climate — **okra, black-eyed peas (a type of cowpea), sweet potato, and peppers** — all of which shrug off the worst of a Texas summer better than tomatoes or squash do. Sweet potato slips go in once soil is reliably warm (May); okra and black-eyed peas can be direct-sown as late as June and still produce well into fall.
- **The "summer stall":** many crops (tomatoes especially) stop setting fruit in the hottest weeks (nighttime lows above ~75°F/24°C) even though the plant looks healthy — this is normal, not disease; they resume setting fruit as nights cool in September. Okra, black-eyed peas, and peppers keep producing through this stall, which is exactly why they're the backbone of a North Texas summer survival garden.
- **Second fall crop:** many warm-season plantings (bush beans, squash) can go in again in **late July–early August** for a fall harvest before the November frost, in addition to the fall cool-season greens planting.

**Drought/water conservation specifics:** blackland clay actually holds water fairly well once amended, which helps it outperform sandy soil in drought — lean into deep, infrequent watering and heavy mulch; prioritize water for transplants and fruit-set stages over established, deep-rooted crops like okra and black-eyed peas, which tolerate real dry spells once established.

### Other scenario variants
- **Urban apartment / no yard:** rely on Methods 1–2 (sprouts, windowsill containers) plus a balcony if available (Method 3); community garden plots are a strong option if accessible.
- **Short-term emergency (days to a few weeks):** prioritize sprouts (2–5 days) and greens (25–35 days) for fast nutrition; don't expect calorie crops to help before help arrives, but plant them anyway in case the situation extends.
- **Long-term/grid-down scenario:** prioritize calorie crops (potato, sweet potato, dry beans, winter squash, corn) and seed saving from day one, and start composting immediately since good soil takes months to build.
- **Cold climates/short season:** use cold frames (a box with a clear/plastic top over a bed) and indoor starting to extend the effective season; choose fast, cold-tolerant varieties.
- **Arid/desert regions:** container and raised-bed growing with heavy mulch and drip/bottle irrigation conserves water far better than open in-ground rows; okra, beans, and squash tolerate heat and dry spells better than lettuce/greens, which may need to be grown almost exclusively in shaded containers.

## ⚠️ Safety — warnings & mistakes to avoid
- **Do not eat sprouts, greens, or any produce that smells sour, moldy, or slimy** — discard and start over; this is far more common with sprouts than with garden vegetables, and it's the most common way beginners get sick from home-grown food.
- **Never use seed treated with fungicide/pesticide (often dyed pink, blue, or green) for sprouting or eating** — those are for planting only, and only crops grown from them to maturity, not sprouted and eaten raw.
- **Do not use fresh (un-composted) manure directly on food crops without a waiting period** — it can carry pathogens (E. coli, etc.). Fully composted manure (heated through the pathogen-kill temperatures in the composting section) is safest; if applying raw or merely-aged manure, wait at least **120 days before harvest for crops that touch the soil** (root vegetables, lettuce, low-growing greens) and at least **90 days before harvest for other crops**, and keep it away from edible parts until well broken down.
- **Never compost or use greywater containing meat, dairy, grease, pet/human waste, or strong chemicals** near food crops.
- **Do not overwater** — more container/raised-bed plants die from soggy, oxygen-starved roots (root rot) than from underwatering; always ensure drainage holes.
- **Watch for lookalike toxic plants** if collecting any seed or volunteer plants from the wild rather than a known packet — when in doubt, don't eat an unidentified plant (cross-reference the [Wild Edibles guide](../food-foraging-wild-edibles/food-foraging-wild-edibles.md)).
- **Green potatoes and potato sprouts/eyes contain solanine (toxic)** — always hill soil over developing potatoes to keep tubers covered and dark, cut away any green skin before eating, and never eat green potatoes. **Potato foliage, stems, and berries are also toxic (glycoalkaloids) — never eat the leaves, stems, or berries of a potato plant; only the tuber (with any green parts cut away) is food.**
- **Rhubarb leaves are toxic** (the stalks are fine) — never eat the leaf blade.
- **Raw kidney beans (and some other dry beans) contain a toxin destroyed only by a full hard boil** — never eat raw or undercooked dry beans; sprouted kidney beans specifically should not be eaten raw either. Common sprouting seeds (mung, lentil, alfalfa, radish, chickpea) are safe raw; when in doubt about a specific bean, cook it.
- **Don't plant into soil you suspect is contaminated** (old industrial sites, near heavily trafficked roads, near old painted structures, treated lumber runoff) without testing or using raised beds with clean, brought-in soil as a barrier. **Lead is the most common urban/suburban soil contaminant** (from old paint and past leaded-gasoline exhaust settling near roads and building foundations) — a low-cost soil test through your local agricultural extension office is the best way to check before growing food in ground soil at an unfamiliar site.
- **Corn needs block planting**, not a single row, or it will pollinate poorly and produce mostly empty or partial ears — a common and avoidable beginner disappointment.

## Troubleshooting
| Problem | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Seeds never sprout | Old/dead seed, planted too deep, soil too dry or waterlogged | Test seed viability (a few seeds on a damp paper towel); replant at correct depth; adjust watering |
| Sprouts (jar method) turn slimy/smell sour | Not rinsed/drained often enough, standing water in jar | Discard, wash jar thoroughly, rinse 2–3x daily and drain completely each time |
| Seedlings tall, thin, and falling over ("leggy") | Not enough light | Move closer to a window/stronger light, or begin hardening off for outdoor sun sooner |
| Yellowing lower leaves | Often overwatering/poor drainage, sometimes nitrogen deficiency | Check drainage holes/soil moisture first; if soil is fine, add compost or diluted manure tea |
| Plant wilts even when soil is moist | Root rot (overwatering) or root damage/disease | Let soil dry more between waterings; improve drainage; remove badly affected plants |
| Leaves have holes, chewed edges | Caterpillars, beetles, grasshoppers | Hand-pick pests in early morning/evening; introduce or attract birds; use row covers over young plants; a mix of water + a few drops of dish soap sprayed on soft-bodied pests (aphids) can help |
| Sticky residue + curled leaves, ants present | Aphids (ants "farm" them for their sugary excretion) | Spray off with a strong jet of water; soapy water spray; encourage ladybugs; remove badly infested growth |
| White powdery coating on leaves | Powdery mildew (fungal disease, common in humid/crowded conditions) | Improve air circulation (space plants better), avoid overhead watering/wet leaves, remove affected leaves, a diluted baking-soda-and-water spray can slow spread |
| Plant flowers/bolts very early, leaves turn bitter | Heat or day-length stress on a cool-season crop | Expected in heat — harvest what you can, replant that crop in the cooler fall window instead |
| Squash/corn plants flower but no fruit forms, or fruit shrivels | Poor pollination (not enough bees, or corn not block-planted) | Hand-pollinate squash (dab pollen from a male flower — thin stem, no baby fruit behind it — into a female flower's center, which has a tiny fruit behind the bloom); replant corn in blocks next time |
| Tomatoes stop setting fruit in midsummer | Nights too hot (common in North Texas summer) | Normal "summer stall" — keep plants healthy and watered; fruit set resumes as nights cool in September |
| Whole bed dries out fast, cracks in soil | Native clay, insufficient organic matter/mulch | Add compost, add 2–3 in of mulch, water deeply and less often, consider converting to a raised bed |
| Seeds saved from last year didn't grow true to the parent plant | Started from a hybrid ("F1") variety, or cross-pollination occurred | Use open-pollinated varieties for seed saving; isolate cross-pollinating crops (squash, corn) by distance/timing |

## Quick-reference checklist
- [ ] Started a jar of sprouts today (fastest food, 2–5 days).
- [ ] Identified available growing space (window, balcony, yard) and matching method.
- [ ] Started a compost pile or worm bin — even before you plant anything.
- [ ] Chosen fast crops (greens, radish) for quick wins and calorie crops (potato, beans, squash, corn) for the long term.
- [ ] Amended soil with compost; built or improved drainage (raised bed if clay/poor soil).
- [ ] Planted at correct depth and spacing; watered in well.
- [ ] Mulched 2–3 inches over all beds/containers to save water.
- [ ] Set a deep-and-infrequent watering routine, at soil level, in early morning.
- [ ] Checked plants every few days for pests/disease and treated at first sign.
- [ ] Planned seed saving from open-pollinated varieties for next season.
- [ ] Checked local frost dates and planted within the correct seasonal window (see North Texas calendar above for that region).
- [ ] Planned season extension (cold frame, indoor starts, or fall replanting) so a single frost or heat wave doesn't end food production.
