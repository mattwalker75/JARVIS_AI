# Survival Logistics & Resource Management

## Read this first (the 30-second version)
1. **You cannot manage what you don't measure.** The moment a disaster starts, start a **daily log** of every gallon of water, every calorie of food, every unit of fuel, every watt-hour of battery, and every dose of medication your household uses. Without numbers, you're guessing — and guessing is how households run out with no warning.
2. **Do the math daily: Days Remaining = Amount Left ÷ Daily Use Rate.** This single formula, applied to water, food, fuel, power, and medicine, tells you exactly how much runway you have for each resource.
3. **Ration BEFORE you're desperate, not after.** If the math says a resource will run out before help arrives, cut consumption **early and gradually** — a planned 20% cut in week one is survivable; a forced 100% cut in week three is a crisis.
4. **Protect vulnerable people first.** Infants, pregnant/nursing people, young children, the elderly, and the sick or injured get their full share. Ration adults' and healthy able-bodied members' portions first — never a child's.
5. **Never ration water to stretch food, and never skip or split doses of critical medication (insulin, heart, seizure, psychiatric) to stretch a supply.** Both are hard rules, not judgment calls — see Safety below.
6. **When your days-remaining number for any resource drops below your safety buffer, that's your trigger to resupply — or, if you can't, to start evacuation planning now, not later.**

This guide covers **managing consumption during an event**: tracking, math, rationing, and forecasting. Building and stocking the stockpile in the first place (what to buy, how much, shelf life, rotation while nothing is happening) is covered in [Supplies, Inventory & Rotation](../supplies-inventory-rotation/supplies-inventory-rotation.md). Finding and purifying water is in [Water](../water/water.md). Preserving and cooking food is in [Food Preservation & Cooking](../food-preservation-cooking/food-preservation-cooking.md). Generator and solar equipment detail is in [Power & Energy](../power-energy/power-energy.md). The full shelter-vs-evacuate decision framework is in [Emergency Planning & Triage](../emergency-planning-triage/emergency-planning-triage.md) — this guide tells you *when the numbers say it's time* to have that conversation; that guide tells you *how to make and execute the decision*.

## Why this matters
In the first hours of an emergency, most households have plenty of everything. The danger isn't day one — it's day nine, when nobody has been tracking, three people have been drinking however much they wanted, the propane tank is "probably still got some in it," and suddenly the water is gone with no warning and no time to react. Resource crises are almost always **slow and mathematical**, not sudden. A household that tracks consumption and does simple daily arithmetic can see a shortage coming from a mile away and adjust gradually — cutting portions by 10%, driving to the store a day early, calling the pharmacy before the last dose. A household that doesn't track anything finds out it's out of water when the last container is empty, out of insulin when the last vial is used, or out of propane mid-meal. The math in this guide is simple on purpose — it needs to work with a pencil, a notebook, and no calculator, run by someone tired, stressed, and possibly by candlelight.

## Key terms (plain language)
- **Consumption rate** — how much of something you use per unit of time, usually per day (e.g., "6 gallons of water per day" for a household).
- **Days remaining** — how many more days a resource will last at the current rate of use. Formula: **Days Remaining = Amount Left ÷ Daily Use Rate.**
- **Ration / rationing** — deliberately limiting how much of something is used per person per day, usually because supply is limited and the event's end date is unknown.
- **Ration floor** — the minimum safe amount below which health, especially in children, the sick, and the elderly, is put at serious risk. Going below the floor is an emergency, not a rationing strategy.
- **Priority persons** — household members whose needs must be met in full before anyone else's, even under rationing: infants, pregnant/nursing people, young children, the elderly, and the sick, injured, or recovering.
- **Calorie (kcal)** — the unit of food energy printed on nutrition labels as "Calories." Adults typically need roughly 1,600–3,000 kcal/day depending on age, size, sex, and activity level.
- **Watt-hour (Wh)** — the unit batteries and power stations are (or can be) rated in; it tells you how much energy is stored. **Wh = Volts × Amp-hours** (or Volts × mAh ÷ 1000).
- **Burn rate** — how much fuel (propane, gasoline, kerosene, wood) a stove, heater, or generator uses per hour of operation.
- **FIFO (First In, First Out)** — using the oldest stock first so nothing quietly expires at the back of a shelf; critical for rotation both before and during an event.
- **Buffer / safety margin** — the number of days of a resource you want to always have in reserve, below which you take action rather than wait and see.
- **Malnutrition** — health harm from too little food (or too little of specific nutrients) over time; it's a slower, sneakier danger than starvation and can be caused by "sensible-looking" rationing that goes on too long or is too deep.
- **Resupply trigger** — a days-remaining number, decided **in advance**, at which you commit to restocking (a store run, a pharmacy refill, a generator-fuel run) rather than waiting until the resource is nearly gone.

# PART 1 — Water: Consumption, Forecasting, and Rationing

Full detail on **finding and purifying** water is in the [Water guide](../water/water.md). This section is about **tracking how much you have and how long it will last.**

## 1A. Baseline consumption per person
| Situation | Gallons/person/day |
|---|---|
| Baseline (temperate, low activity) | **1.0 gal** (≈ 0.5 gal drinking + 0.5 gal cooking/hygiene) |
| Hot climate or physical exertion (add) | **+0.5 to +1.0 gal** |
| Feverish illness, vomiting, or diarrhea (add — rehydration) | **+0.5 to +1.0 gal** |
| Pregnant or nursing (add) | **+0.3 to +0.5 gal** |
| Young child (age 4–12, base amount) | **0.5 to 0.75 gal** — but budget a full 1.0 gal to stay safe |
| Small dog (≤ 20 lb) | **≈ 0.25 gal** |
| Large dog (> 60 lb) | **≈ 0.5 to 1.0 gal** |
| Cat | **≈ 0.05 to 0.1 gal** (roughly half a liter) |

> ⚠️ **Ration your activity (sweat), not your water.** In heat, cutting water intake to "stretch the supply" causes heat exhaustion and heat stroke faster than it saves anything. If water is genuinely short, reduce exertion (rest in shade, work at night/early morning) instead of reducing intake.

## 1A-2. Where the "1 gallon/person/day" number comes from — and how it compares internationally
The **1 gallon (≈3.8 L) per person per day** planning figure (FEMA/Ready.gov/CDC/Red Cross) is a **US household storage-planning number**, not a bare survival minimum — it already bundles roughly a half-gallon for drinking with a half-gallon for cooking and hygiene. It's worth knowing where it sits against the international humanitarian standard, because that range explains *why* the guide's rationing rules below protect drinking water first and cut hygiene/laundry water first:

| Standard | Amount | What it covers |
|---|---|---|
| **Absolute survival minimum** (drinking only, temperate climate) — WHO/Sphere | **2.5–3 L/day** (≈ 0.7–0.8 gal) | Drinking + food moisture only. Rises significantly in heat or with physical exertion — this is the floor, not a target. |
| **US household planning figure** — FEMA/Ready.gov/CDC | **1 gal (≈3.8 L)/day** | Drinking (~0.5 gal) + cooking/basic hygiene (~0.5 gal) combined. |
| **Sphere humanitarian minimum standard** (relief camps, first weeks) | **7.5–15 L/day** (≈ 2–4 gal) | Survival (2.5–3 L) + basic hygiene (2–6 L) + basic cooking (3–6 L) — the full breakdown relief agencies use is in the table below. |
| **WHO "minimum for health and hygiene," fuller standard** | **~20 L/day** (≈ 5.3 gal) | Target relief agencies work up to once initial survival needs are met — not a first-week expectation. |

**The Sphere/WHO breakdown** (from WHO/WEDC Technical Note 9, adapting the Sphere Handbook) shows exactly what stacks up to the 7.5–15 L range — useful for understanding *which* uses to cut first if your own supply runs short of the 1 gal/day US planning figure:

| Need | Litres/person/day | Gallons/person/day | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Survival (drinking + food) | 2.5–3 L | ≈0.7–0.8 gal | Depends on climate and individual physiology — **never cut this tier** |
| Basic hygiene | 2–6 L | ≈0.5–1.6 gal | Depends on cultural/personal norms — **cut here first** |
| Basic cooking | 3–6 L | ≈0.8–1.6 gal | Depends on food type — choose low-water-prep foods to shrink this |
| **Total (Sphere minimum standard)** | **7.5–15 L** | **≈2–4 gal** | |

Relief agencies also use a **staged relief timeline** worth borrowing for your own event-length planning: in the first 2 weeks–1 month of a large-scale emergency response, the working target is only **5 L/person/day**; it rises to **10 L/day** by 1–3 months, and **15 L+/day** by 3–6 months, as supply logistics catch up. If your household's own stored water is tight, this confirms the guide's instinct: **protect the survival (drinking) tier at 100%, and let hygiene/cooking water absorb the early cuts** — exactly what Part 1C below tells you to do.

> **FEMA's rule is stronger than "ration carefully" — it's "don't ration water at all."** FEMA 477 *Food and Water in an Emergency* states it plainly: *"If supplies run low, never ration water. Drink the amount you need today, and try to find more for tomorrow. You can minimize the amount of water your body needs by reducing activity and staying cool."* This guide's approach (cut non-drinking uses first, never cut drinking below the floor) implements that rule in practice — treat "ration the water itself" as off the table, and "find more / cut activity" as the two real levers.

## 1B. Worked example — sizing today's household need
Household: 2 adults, 2 children (ages 8 and 11), 1 dog (40 lb). It's August in North Texas, the AC is out, and Adult 2 has mild diarrhea.

| Person | Base | Adjustments | Today's need |
|---|---|---|---|
| Adult 1 (healthy) | 1.0 | +0.5 heat | 1.5 gal |
| Adult 2 (mildly ill) | 1.0 | +0.5 heat, +0.75 illness | 2.25 gal |
| Child 1 | 0.75 | +0.3 heat (rounded up) | 1.0 gal |
| Child 2 | 0.75 | +0.3 heat (rounded up) | 1.0 gal |
| Dog (40 lb) | 0.25 | — | 0.25 gal |
| **Household total** | | | **6.0 gal/day** |

If the household has **84 gallons stored**: **Days Remaining = 84 ÷ 6.0 = 14 days.**

Recompute this total **every day** — it changes as heat, illness, and activity change. A single sick or feverish household member can add a full day's worth of water demand by themselves.

## 1C. Rationing water — the rules that keep it safe
1. **Never ration below survival need.** A healthy adult needs a hard minimum of about **½ gallon (2 quarts) of drinking water per day** even at complete rest in mild conditions — FEMA's own figure (FEMA 477) — and the WHO/Sphere absolute floor is close: **2.5–3 L/day (≈0.7–0.8 gal)** for drinking and food combined (see 1A-2). Neither is a target — both are emergency floors. In heat, that floor rises.
2. **Cut non-drinking uses first**, in this order: laundry → bathing (switch to sponge baths/wet-wipe hygiene) → dishwashing (use minimal water, or paper plates) → cooking water (choose foods that need less water to prepare) → drinking (cut last, and only by reducing activity, never by rationing thirst).
3. **If the days-remaining number is falling short of your expected event length**, that's your signal to intensify water-finding efforts (see the [Water guide](../water/water.md)) rather than simply drinking less.
4. **Track separately: "safe/treated" water vs. "raw/untreated" water.** Only count treated water in your days-remaining math unless you have fuel and time budgeted to treat more.

# PART 2 — Food: Calorie Accounting and Ration Planning

Preserving and cooking food is covered in [Food Preservation & Cooking](../food-preservation-cooking/food-preservation-cooking.md). This section is about **counting what you have, what you need, and rationing it fairly if the math comes up short.**

## 2A. Daily calorie need by person (rough planning figures)
| Person | Approx. kcal/day |
|---|---|
| Adult, sedentary/moderate activity | 1,800–2,200 |
| Adult, hard physical labor (hauling water, clearing debris, no AC in heat) | 2,500–3,000 |
| Child, age 4–8 | 1,200–1,400 |
| Child, age 9–13 | 1,600–2,000 |
| Teen, age 14–18 | 1,800–2,400 |
| Pregnant (2nd/3rd trimester, add to normal need) | +300 to +450 |
| Nursing | +450 to +500 |
| Elderly, lower activity | 1,600–1,800 |

**For a fast household-average estimate** (useful when you don't have time to individualize every member), the humanitarian relief standard used by WHO/WFP/Sphere for general population food-aid planning is **2,100 kcal/person/day average** across a mixed population of adults and children — close to, and a reasonable stand-in for, the per-person figures above when averaged across a typical household. This is a *planning average*, not a floor — see 2E for the actual minimum floor.

## 2B. Counting your inventory in calories, not just "cans"
Nutrition labels list **Calories per serving** and **servings per container**. Multiply and add:

```
Total kcal in a container = (Calories per serving) × (servings per container)
```

**Worked example — pantry audit:**

| Item | Cal/serving | Servings/container | Containers | Total kcal |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Canned beans | 110 | 3.5 | 12 | 4,620 |
| White rice (bulk bag) | 160 | 30 | 2 | 9,600 |
| Peanut butter | 190 | 15 | 3 | 8,550 |
| Canned tuna | 90 | 2 | 20 | 3,600 |
| Pasta | 200 | 8 | 6 | 9,600 |
| Powdered milk | 80 | 25 | 2 | 4,000 |
| *(continue for every item)* | | | | |
| **Total pantry** | | | | **≈ 154,000 kcal** |

## 2C. Worked example — days of food remaining
Same household as above (2 adults @ 2,000 kcal, 2 kids @ 1,500 kcal average):

```
Household demand = 2,000 + 2,000 + 1,500 + 1,500 = 7,000 kcal/day
Days Remaining   = 154,000 ÷ 7,000 = 22 days
```

## 2D. Ration planning — when the math falls short
Say your best estimate is the event/outage will last **30 days**, but your pantry audit only supports **22 days at full ration**. You have two choices, and usually you do both:
1. **Extend supply** — forage safe wild edibles (see [Food Foraging](../food-foraging-wild-edibles/food-foraging-wild-edibles.md) if available), fish/trap, grow quick crops, or plan a resupply run.
2. **Ration** — stretch the existing supply across more days by reducing kcal/day.

> **Unlike water, food can be rationed safely — with one exception.** FEMA 477 states: *"If activity is reduced, healthy people can survive on half their usual food intake for an extended period, and without any food for many days. Food, unlike water, may be rationed safely, except for children and pregnant women."* This is the opposite rule from water (Part 1C) — it's why this guide has you cut healthy adults' calories first and protect children and pregnant/nursing members' rations completely, rather than spreading a cut evenly across everyone.

**Reduced-ration math:**
```
New household kcal/day target = Total kcal remaining ÷ Days you need it to last
                               = 154,000 ÷ 30 = 5,133 kcal/day (household)
```
That's a cut from 7,000 to 5,133 kcal/day — about a **27% reduction**. Applied fairly:
- **Do not cut children, pregnant/nursing members, the elderly, or the sick.** Their kcal targets from the table above stay intact.
- **Take the entire cut from healthy adults.** In the example, the two kids still get ~1,500 each (3,000 total), leaving 2,133 kcal for both adults combined (≈1,065 each) — a real cut, uncomfortable but short-term survivable for healthy adults, versus the danger of cutting a growing child.
- **Re-run this calculation every few days** as inventory and forecast both change; don't set it once and forget it.

## 2E. Rationing fairly without causing malnutrition
- **Set a floor and don't go below it.** For healthy adults, avoid sustained intake below roughly **1,200–1,500 kcal/day** for more than a few days — below that, energy, judgment, and immune function drop fast, which is dangerous precisely when you need to think clearly and stay healthy.
- **Never reduce children's rations to match adult cuts.** Children have less reserve, and inadequate calories during growth causes harm faster than in adults.
- **Watch diabetics closely.** Consistent carbohydrate intake matched to medication timing matters more than total calories — a diabetic household member skipping meals while still taking their usual medication dose is a medical emergency risk, not a rationing detail. Coordinate meal timing with their medication schedule (see Part 5).
- **Stretch volume with dense, cheap staples, not by diluting calories.** Adding water to a soup makes it feel more filling but does not add calories — bulk out meals with rice, beans, oats, and pasta (cheap, calorie-dense, shelf-stable) rather than "thinning" what you have. Add foraged greens or garden vegetables for vitamins, not as a calorie substitute.
- **Spread smaller meals across the day rather than skipping meals**, especially for children, the elderly, and anyone on medication tied to meals — steadier blood sugar and steadier morale beats one large meal and long gaps.
- **Rotate variety and "treat" items** (the last of the good snacks, a preferred flavor) evenly across household members so no one person quietly does without every time — write it down so it's visibly fair.
- **Hold a short family meeting to explain the plan.** Rationing causes far less conflict and hoarding when everyone understands the math and sees it's applied fairly — post the log where everyone can see it.
- **Watch for the slow signs of malnutrition** over more than a couple of weeks of rationing: unusual fatigue, irritability, poor wound healing, hair/skin changes, a child not gaining or losing weight. These are signals to prioritize resupply immediately, not to "push through."

# PART 3 — Fuel: Cooking, Heating, and Generator Burn Rates

Equipment selection and safe generator setup are covered in [Power & Energy](../power-energy/power-energy.md). This section is the **math for how long your fuel will last.**

## 3A. Typical burn rates
| Equipment | Typical burn rate | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Single-burner propane camp stove, high flame | ≈ 1 lb propane/hour | A standard 20 lb cylinder (≈ 4.6 gal liquid propane) run continuously on high ≈ 20 hours; realistic intermittent cooking (a few hours/day, mostly simmer) stretches this to several weeks |
| Propane camp stove, simmer/low | ≈ 0.3–0.5 lb/hour | |
| Portable kerosene/propane radiant heater | ≈ 0.2–0.3 gal/hour (kerosene) | Only ever run in a properly vented space — see Safety |
| Small inverter generator (2,000–3,000W), light load (~25%) | ≈ 0.2–0.3 gal gasoline/hour | Load = what's actually plugged in (fridge + a few lights ≈ light load) |
| Same generator, ~50% load | ≈ 0.4–0.5 gal/hour | |
| Same generator, ~75–100% load | ≈ 0.6–1.0+ gal/hour | Running near max load burns fuel fast and shortens engine life |
| Wood cookstove/campfire for cooking | Highly variable | Budget in armloads/logs per meal rather than by weight; see [Fire](../fire/fire.md) |

## 3B. The formula
```
Days of Fuel Remaining = Fuel Stored ÷ (Hours Used per Day × Burn Rate per Hour)
```

**Worked example — generator:**
Household runs a 3,000W generator 8 hours/day to power the fridge and some lights (≈25% load, 0.25 gal/hour) and has **40 gallons of stored gasoline**.
```
Daily use = 8 hours × 0.25 gal/hour = 2.0 gal/day
Days Remaining = 40 ÷ 2.0 = 20 days
```
If they instead ran it 12 hours/day (still light load): daily use = 3.0 gal/day → days remaining = 13.3 days. **Cutting run-hours is the single biggest lever on generator fuel life** — run it only for scheduled blocks (e.g., morning and evening) rather than continuously.

**Worked example — cooking propane:**
Two 20 lb cylinders (40 lb total) for a camp stove used ~1.5 hours/day at a mix of high and simmer (~average 0.6 lb/hour):
```
Daily use = 1.5 × 0.6 = 0.9 lb/day
Days Remaining = 40 ÷ 0.9 ≈ 44 days
```

## 3C. Stretching fuel safely
- **Batch-cook** — cook a full day's meals in one heating session and keep food warm in an insulated hay-box cooker (see [Food Preservation & Cooking](../food-preservation-cooking/food-preservation-cooking.md)) rather than reheating multiple times.
- **Run generators only in scheduled blocks** (e.g., 2 hours morning, 2 hours evening) rather than continuously; use that window to refrigerate, recharge batteries, and run any well pump — all at once.
- **Cook multiple items per heating cycle** and pre-cut/prep food so burner time is minimized.
- **Track fuel exactly like water and food** — weigh propane cylinders if possible (full 20 lb cylinder weighs about 37–38 lb including the ~17–18 lb tank; subtract tank weight to get remaining propane) or mark gas cans with a fill line and dip-stick check.

> ⚠️ **Never run a generator, camp stove, charcoal grill, or kerosene heater indoors, in a garage, or near an open window/vent** — carbon monoxide is invisible, odorless, and kills quickly. See [Power & Energy](../power-energy/power-energy.md) for full CO safety.

# PART 4 — Battery and Power Budgeting

Choosing and setting up power equipment (solar panels, power stations, generators) is in [Power & Energy](../power-energy/power-energy.md). This section is the **math for how long stored power will last against your actual devices.**

## 4A. Convert everything to watt-hours (Wh)
```
Watt-hours (Wh) = Volts × Amp-hours
                 = Volts × (milliamp-hours ÷ 1000)
```
**Worked example — power bank:** a 20,000 mAh power bank at 3.7V:
```
20,000 mAh ÷ 1,000 = 20 Ah
20 Ah × 3.7V = 74 Wh
```
A phone battery (~3,000 mAh at 3.85V ≈ 11.5 Wh) needs roughly **14 Wh from the bank per full charge** once you account for ~20% conversion loss. So a 74 Wh power bank gives roughly **74 ÷ 14 ≈ 5 full phone charges** before it's empty.

## 4B. Typical device draw (for budgeting, not precision)
| Device | Typical draw |
|---|---|
| Phone charge (one full charge) | ≈ 12–15 Wh |
| LED lantern/headlamp | ≈ 1–5 W (so 5–25 Wh for a 5-hour evening) |
| Small fan | ≈ 20–50 W |
| CPAP machine | ≈ 30–60 W |
| Laptop | ≈ 40–65 W |
| Mini fridge/cooler (compressor cycling) | ≈ 40–80 W average |
| Radio (battery or hand-crank) | ≈ 0.5–2 W |

## 4C. The formula
```
Days of Power Remaining = Stored Wh ÷ Daily Wh Use
```
**Worked example — 300 Wh portable power station**, running an LED lantern 5 hours/night (5W → 25 Wh) and charging two phones daily (2 × 14 Wh = 28 Wh):
```
Daily use = 25 + 28 = 53 Wh/day
Days Remaining = 300 ÷ 53 ≈ 5.7 days
```
If a solar panel recharges some of that back in daily, subtract the recharge from daily use before dividing — e.g., 20 Wh/day of solar input against 53 Wh/day use nets to 33 Wh/day actual drain, extending the runway to 300 ÷ 33 ≈ 9 days. (See [Power & Energy](../power-energy/power-energy.md) for solar setup.)

## 4D. Managing a power budget
- **Rank devices by necessity**: medical devices (CPAP, medication refrigeration) and communication (radio, one phone) first; comfort items (fans, extra lights) last.
- **Charge in one deliberate batch**, not continuously topping off — plan a daily "power window" (often paired with your generator run window) to charge everything at once, then power down.
- **Track battery-powered flashlights/radios by spare-battery count**, not just "it still works" — count remaining batteries and divide by how many you swap per day.

# PART 5 — Medication Inventory and Countdown

## 5A. The countdown formula
```
Days of Medication Remaining = Pills/Doses Remaining ÷ Doses per Day
```
**Worked example:** 45 tablets remain of a twice-daily medication (2 doses/day):
```
45 ÷ 2 = 22.5 → 22 full days remaining
```

## 5B. Tracking every medication in the household
| Medication | Person | Dose | Doses/day | Qty remaining | Days remaining | Refill/resupply trigger |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| *(example)* Lisinopril 10mg | Adult 2 | 1 tab | 1 | 28 | 28 | Refill at 10 days remaining |
| *(example)* Insulin (vial) | Adult 1 | per sliding scale | ~4 units/day avg | 300 units | ~75 days | Refill at 21 days remaining — **critical, plan early** |

Update the "days remaining" column **every time you take a dose**, not just weekly — medication countdowns are the least forgiving math in this whole guide.

## 5C. Rules specific to medication
- **Set your refill/resupply trigger earlier than for anything else** — 14–30 days remaining, not "when it's low." Pharmacies, insurance early-refill rules, and travel to a pharmacy all take time you may not have during a disaster.
- **Never split, skip, or stretch doses of critical medication** (insulin, heart medication, anti-seizure medication, blood thinners, psychiatric medication) to make a supply last longer. If the countdown shows you will run out, that is a **medical emergency to solve now** — contact a pharmacist, doctor, or emergency services the moment it's reachable, not a rationing decision to make alone.
- **Track alongside food for medications tied to meals** (diabetes medication, some thyroid and blood-pressure medications) — a missed or shifted meal changes when the dose should be taken; coordinate the two logs.
- **Keep medication cool if required** — insulin and some others need refrigeration; if power is out, this becomes part of your power/ice budgeting (Part 4) and food-safety cold-chain planning (see [Food Preservation & Cooking](../food-preservation-cooking/food-preservation-cooking.md)).
- **Building the buffer in the first place** (30-day rolling supply, extra vials, etc.) is covered in [Supplies, Inventory & Rotation](../supplies-inventory-rotation/supplies-inventory-rotation.md) — this section is about tracking the countdown once you're in an event.

# PART 6 — Rotating Supplies During a Long Event

Rotation while nothing is happening (FIFO shelving, expiration checks) is covered in [Supplies, Inventory & Rotation](../supplies-inventory-rotation/supplies-inventory-rotation.md). During an active event, rotation looks slightly different:
1. **Open and consolidate oldest/most perishable items first** — a half-used bag of rice goes before a sealed one; food nearing its best-by date is used before shelf-stable staples that will keep regardless.
2. **Consolidate partial containers** — pour two quarter-full water jugs into one, combine two partial propane cylinders' worth of estimated remaining fuel into your tracking sheet, so your inventory sheet reflects reality, not a shelf full of odd partial containers that are easy to lose track of.
3. **Re-check anything opened for spoilage daily** — once a seal is broken, shelf life changes; smell, look, and (for canned/jarred food) check for bulging, foam, or off odor before use every single time, not just the first time.
4. **Move the "in use" items to a visible, easy-to-reach spot**, and keep the deeper reserve untouched and clearly separate, so the daily log tracks what's actually being drawn down versus what's held in reserve for later/worse days.
5. **Re-audit the whole household sheet (Part 7) on a fixed schedule** — daily for water/food/fuel/power, and every time a dose is taken for medication — so rotation decisions are based on current numbers, not memory.

# PART 7 — Tracking Everything: Templates You Can Copy

**You need:** something to write with and a flat surface to keep the sheet, checked at the same time each day. **Improvised substitutes:** no notebook — use a whiteboard, a chalk wall, a laminated sheet with a wipe-off marker, or even chalk marks on the pantry door; no calculator — every formula in this guide is simple division, doable with pencil and paper.

Notice how Day 3's "notes" column explains *why* every number ticked up slightly (illness raises both water and food need) — that's the habit that makes tomorrow's numbers predictable instead of a surprise. This is the same data feeding the 3-day trailing average in 7C-2.

Tips for filling it in accurately:
- **Estimate consistently, not perfectly** — e.g., mark water jugs with a permanent-marker fill line so you can read off gallons used without weighing anything.
- **If you don't have exact daily numbers, use a trailing 3-day average** instead of guessing from a single unusual day.
- **Note WHY, not just HOW MUCH** — "heat wave, no AC" or "Adult 2 sick" explains a spike and helps you predict tomorrow's need, not just record today's.

**The one formula behind this whole guide:**
```
Days Remaining = Remaining Quantity ÷ Daily Consumption Rate
```

## 7C-2. Forecasting more accurately — trends, not just one day's number
A single day's Days-Remaining number can mislead you in either direction. Two households can have the identical "12 days remaining" today and be in completely different situations — one is stable, one is one heat wave away from a crisis. Track the **trend**, not just the snapshot.

**Worked example — the same 84-gallon water supply, tracked over a week:**

| Day | Qty remaining (gal) | That day's use (gal) | 3-day trailing avg (gal/day) | Days Remaining (qty ÷ 3-day avg) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 84 | 6.0 | 6.0 (only 1 day of data) | 14.0 |
| 2 | 78 | 6.0 | 6.0 | 13.0 |
| 3 | 72 | 6.5 | 6.2 | 11.6 |
| 4 | 65.5 | 8.0 (heat wave started) | 6.8 | 9.6 |
| 5 | 57.5 | 8.5 | 7.7 | 7.5 |
| 6 | 49 | 8.5 | 8.3 | 5.9 |
| 7 | 40.5 | 8.0 | 8.3 | 4.9 |

**How to read this table:** Day 1–3 looked stable at ~13–14 days remaining. By Day 4, a heat wave pushed daily use up, and the days-remaining number **dropped by more than a full day per day** for three days running (14.0 → 13.0 → 11.6 → 9.6 → 7.5 → 5.9 → 4.9) — that accelerating drop is the signal, not just the absolute number. A household that only checked the number on Day 1 and Day 7 would see "14 days" then suddenly "5 days" and feel blindsided; a household tracking daily would have seen the trend turn on Day 4 and had three extra days of warning to act (start conserving, arrange a resupply, or begin evacuation planning).

**Two simple rules for trustworthy forecasting:**
1. **Use a trailing 3-day average for the daily rate, not a single day** (as Part 7B already advises) — a single hot day or a single low-use day both distort the true rate. The table above uses this.
2. **Watch the direction the Days-Remaining number is moving, not just its current value.** A number that is falling faster each day (rate accelerating — like the table above) means your buffer is eroding faster than planned and you should act on the **early** trend, not wait for the number itself to hit your buffer target. A number holding steady or recovering (e.g., after a cool front, or after a rationing cut takes effect) confirms your current plan is working — recheck daily either way.

**A quick "how much runway did that trend cost me" gut-check:** compare the days-remaining forecast from 3 days ago to today's. In the example: Day 4's forecast was 9.6 days; Day 7's is 4.9 — the household lost **4.7 days of runway in 3 calendar days** because of the heat wave. That gap size tells you how urgently to act: losing less than a day of runway per calendar day is a trend to watch; losing close to (or more than) a full day of runway per calendar day, as in this example, is a trend to act on immediately (see 7D).

# North Texas / regional notes (Anna, TX and Collin County)
- **Summer heat raises water demand fast and quietly.** Blackland-prairie summers regularly hit 100°F+; without AC, a household's water math (Part 1) can jump 30–50% overnight. Re-run the water calculation daily during a summer outage, not weekly.
- **Ice storms (e.g., the multi-day 2021 winter grid failure) stress fuel and power tracking simultaneously.** Heating demand spikes fuel burn (Part 3) at the same time cold slows solar recharge and some battery chemistries lose capacity in the cold — budget power (Part 4) more conservatively in freezing conditions and check on elderly/medically dependent neighbors, whose medication cold-chain (Part 5) may also be at risk if refrigeration fails.
- **Rural properties on this prairie often run separate propane tanks for the house and a generator** — track them as **separate line items** on the inventory sheet (Part 7A), since a full house tank does not help a generator that draws from its own tank.
- **Grid-failure events here tend to be regional and multi-day**, not resolved in hours — plan buffer targets (7–14 days minimum for water/food, earlier for medication) accordingly rather than assuming a quick fix.
- **Flash floods and tornado outbreaks** can cut off road access to stores/pharmacies for days even when supplies exist nearby — factor "can I actually get there" into your resupply trigger, not just "is the store open."

# ⚠️ Safety — the most important warnings
- **Never ration water to stretch food, or vice versa treat water as less urgent because food is fine.** They are independent countdowns — track and act on each one on its own terms.
- **Never cut a child's, pregnant/nursing person's, elderly person's, or sick/injured person's ration below the targets in Part 2A to stretch supply for others.** Take rationing cuts from healthy adults first.
- **Never split, skip, delay, or "stretch" doses of critical medication** (insulin, heart, seizure, blood thinner, psychiatric) to make a supply last longer — a shrinking medication countdown is a medical emergency to escalate immediately (pharmacist, doctor, ER), not a logistics problem to solve with math.
- **Never run a fuel-burning device (generator, camp stove, charcoal, kerosene heater) indoors, in a garage, or near a window/vent** — carbon monoxide kills quickly and without warning. See [Power & Energy](../power-energy/power-energy.md).
- **Sustained calorie restriction below roughly 1,200–1,500 kcal/day (adults) for more than a few days risks real harm** — treat a ration plan that requires this as a signal to prioritize resupply or evacuation, not to "push through."
- **Watch for the slow signs of malnutrition** over multi-week rationing (fatigue, irritability, poor healing, weight loss in children) — these mean act now, not wait and see.
- **Set resupply/medication triggers early — 14–30 days out, not "when it's almost gone."** Roads, pharmacies, and stores may not be reachable the day you actually need them.
- **Don't trust memory under stress** — write every number down. Sleep-deprived, worried people reliably misremember how much is left; the log exists precisely for this reason.

# Troubleshooting
| Problem | Fix |
|---|---|
| Don't know an exact daily use rate | Use a trailing 3-day average from the log rather than a single day; refine as you collect more data. |
| No scale to weigh propane/food | Mark containers with a fill line and compare visually day to day; for propane, most cylinders have a stamped empty ("tare") weight — weigh on any bathroom scale and subtract. |
| Family disagrees about fairness of rationing | Post the log and the math where everyone can see it; hold a short family meeting to walk through Part 2's priority-persons rule together. |
| Days-remaining number keeps changing wildly day to day | Expected with small households — use the 3-day average, and note the cause (heat, illness) in the log rather than reacting to one bad day. |
| Diabetic or other medication-timed household member's meals are being cut | Do not apply the general ration cut to them — coordinate meal timing and portion with their medication schedule; treat as a priority person. |
| Generator running longer than planned "just to be safe" | Set a fixed daily run-window (e.g., 2 hrs morning/evening) before starting it, and stick to it — track hours run, not just fuel level, since fuel gauges on small tanks are unreliable. |
| Medication countdown hits your trigger and no refill is possible | Contact a pharmacist or doctor immediately (phone/text if power/cell allow); this is a medical emergency, escalate — do not self-ration critical medication. |
| Any resource's days-remaining drops at or below its buffer target | Treat as your resupply/evacuate decision point — see Part 7D and the [Emergency Planning & Triage](../emergency-planning-triage/emergency-planning-triage.md) guide. |
