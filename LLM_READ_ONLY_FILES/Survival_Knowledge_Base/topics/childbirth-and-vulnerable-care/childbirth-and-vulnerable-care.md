# Emergency Childbirth and Care of Vulnerable People

## Read this first
1. **This guide is for the situation where labor has started (or a vulnerable person needs care) and no doctor, midwife, ambulance, or hospital is reachable.** It is not a substitute for professional maternity or medical care. If you can reach help by any means — phone, radio, a neighbor with a vehicle, walking out — do it, even mid-task. Someone should be trying to get help while others assist.
2. **Most births, even unplanned ones, go normally.** The mother's body knows what to do. Your job is mainly to keep things clean, keep the baby warm and breathing, support — not pull — and watch for the specific danger signs listed below.
3. **The two events that kill mothers and babies in emergency birth are heavy bleeding after delivery and a baby that does not start breathing.** Learn those two sections (Controlling the Mother's Bleeding, and Newborn Care) before anything else.
4. **Do not cut the cord immediately, do not pull on the cord to "help" deliver the placenta, and do not pull on the baby.** These are the three most common harmful things an untrained helper does. Wait, support, and let the body work.
5. **This guide's second half — infants, elderly, pregnant women, chronic illness, disability, pets — matters just as much in most disasters** as the birth section, because these are the people most likely to be hurt by heat, cold, dehydration, or interrupted medication when systems break down.

---

## Why this matters
Babies are sometimes born faster than help can arrive — during a storm, a road closure, a power outage that traps an ambulance, or simply because labor moves faster than expected. In that moment, an untrained but calm helper who keeps things clean, supports the baby as it emerges, and knows what to do in the first minutes after birth can be the difference between a normal, healthy outcome and a tragedy. At the same time, any large emergency (a hurricane, a multi-day power outage, an extended evacuation) puts the most fragile members of a household at the highest risk: infants who dehydrate in hours, elderly people whose medications and mobility routines are disrupted, pregnant women whose bodies are already under strain, people with diabetes or breathing conditions who depend on supplies that can run out, and pets who cannot tell you what is wrong and depend entirely on you. This guide gives a beginner a clear, step-by-step way to help in both situations, and clearly marks the point where the situation is beyond home care and outside help is needed however it can be reached.

## Key terms
- **Labor** — the process of contractions and cervix opening (dilating) that leads to birth, in three stages: dilation, delivery of the baby, delivery of the placenta.
- **Contraction** — a tightening/cramping of the uterus; timed from the start of one to the start of the next.
- **Crowning** — the moment the baby's head is visible and stays visible at the vaginal opening between contractions — birth is imminent (minutes away).
- **Bag of waters / amniotic sac** — the fluid-filled membrane around the baby; "water breaking" is this sac rupturing.
- **Cord prolapse** — the umbilical cord slips out ahead of the baby; a true emergency (cuts off the baby's oxygen).
- **Breech** — baby positioned to come out feet, knees, or buttocks first instead of head first; needs professional help if at all possible.
- **Placenta (afterbirth)** — the organ that fed the baby via the cord; delivered after the baby, usually within 5–30 minutes.
- **Postpartum hemorrhage** — heavy bleeding after birth; the leading cause of maternal death worldwide and the top danger sign to watch for.
- **Fundus** — the top of the uterus, felt as a firm mass in the lower abdomen after birth; massaging it helps it contract and control bleeding.
- **APGAR-style check** — a quick look at a newborn's breathing, color, and activity in the first minutes of life.
- **Vernix** — the white, waxy coating on a newborn's skin; do not scrub it off, it protects the skin.
- **Hypothermia** — dangerously low body temperature; newborns, infants, and the elderly lose heat far faster than healthy adults.
- **Heat exhaustion / heat stroke** — progressive heat illness; infants and the elderly are the two highest-risk groups.
- **Continuity of care** — keeping a chronic condition (diabetes, asthma, heart disease, dementia) stable when the normal medical routine is disrupted.

## What you need
Gather what you can — do not delay delivery to search for a "complete" kit. Improvise with clean cloth/paper if needed.

**For the birth:**
- Clean sheets, towels, or the cleanest cloth available (several — for the surface, for the mother, for drying/wrapping the baby)
- Soap and clean water (or hand sanitizer) for handwashing
- Clean/disposable gloves if available
- Two clean shoelaces, cotton string, or strips of clean cloth (to tie the cord) — boiled or alcohol-wiped if possible
- Clean scissors, a knife, or a razor blade (to cut the cord) — sterilized in boiling water or wiped with alcohol if possible
- A bulb syringe (if available) to clear the baby's mouth/nose — a clean cloth-wrapped finger works if not
- A blanket or extra clothing to wrap the baby (a hat if available — babies lose huge amounts of heat through the head)
- Something absorbent (towels, a plastic sheet, newspaper under cloth) to protect the surface underneath
- A container for the placenta (it will need to be looked at, and eventually disposed of or transported for medical review)
- A watch or phone to time contractions and note the birth time
- A phone, radio, or a person assigned to go for help the moment labor is recognized

**For vulnerable-person care generally:**
- Extra water and oral rehydration supplies (see the emergency-medicine-herbal guide for the ORS recipe)
- A written list of every household member's (and pet's) medications, doses, allergies, and conditions
- A minimum 2-week backup supply of critical medications where possible, and a plan for insulin/refrigerated drugs
- Thermometer, extra blankets, and a way to create shade/airflow (fans, damp cloth) for both heat and cold response
- Pet food, water, leash/carrier, and any pet medications

## Part A — Emergency Childbirth

## 1. Recognizing labor and its stages
Labor has three stages. A beginner mainly needs to recognize Stage 1 (time to prepare and try for help) and Stage 2 (birth is close — prepare to assist).

- **Stage 1 — Dilation.** Contractions start irregular and mild, then become regular, longer, and closer together. Early labor can last many hours, especially with a first baby. Signs: regular tightening/cramping of the belly, lower back pain, possibly a "bloody show" (thick pink/blood-tinged mucus), possibly the water breaking (a gush or slow trickle of clear/pale fluid).
- **Stage 2 — Delivery of the baby.** Contractions are strong, close together (2–3 minutes apart), and the mother feels a strong, involuntary urge to push, or rectal pressure "like a bowel movement." This is often fast. **Crowning (seeing the baby's head at the opening and it staying visible between pushes) means birth is minutes away — do not travel, do not delay, prepare to assist now.**
- **Stage 3 — Delivery of the placenta.** After the baby is born, mild contractions continue and the placenta is delivered, usually within 5–30 minutes.

**Try to get professional help the moment you recognize labor has started**, even if birth ends up happening before help arrives. A phone call for guidance, even mid-labor, is valuable if any signal is available.

## 2. Preparing a clean area and supplies
1. Choose a flat, stable surface — a bed, the floor, or a car seat reclined — that the mother can lie on with knees bent and legs apart, or in whatever position is most comfortable (semi-sitting, hands-and-knees, and side-lying are all acceptable if the head still emerges normally).
2. Layer clean sheets/towels/blankets under her hips and legs. Use a plastic sheet or tarp underneath the cloth if available to protect the surface.
3. Wash your hands thoroughly with soap and water for at least 20 seconds, or use hand sanitizer. Put on gloves if you have them.
4. Lay out your supplies within reach: cord ties, cutting tool, towels for the baby, a blanket, the bulb syringe.
5. Keep the room warm if possible — a newborn chills fast, and warmth helps the mother relax.
6. Have someone continue trying to reach help by any means throughout.

## 3. Step-by-step: assisting a normal, head-first delivery
Your role is to **support, not pull.**

1. As the head crowns, place a clean, gentle hand near (not gripping) the head to guide — not force — its emergence, and support the perineum (the skin between vagina and anus) with a folded cloth to reduce tearing.
2. Let the head emerge slowly between contractions if possible; if the mother has a very strong urge to push, let her push but discourage frantic straining — slow and steady reduces tearing.
3. **Once the head is out, check immediately whether the umbilical cord is wrapped around the baby's neck.** Slide a finger along the neck. If it is loose, gently loop it up and over the head. If it is tight and cannot be slipped over, do not panic — most babies still tolerate this briefly; keep the birth moving and address it once the body is delivered (double-tie and cut the cord promptly if it remains tight and is delaying the body's delivery — see Section 4).
4. The head will typically rotate to the side on its own — do not force this.
5. Support the head and neck with both hands as the shoulders deliver, one at a time, usually with gentle downward guidance for the top shoulder then upward for the lower one, following the mother's own pushing — **do not pull the baby out.**
6. Once the shoulders are through, the rest of the body usually slides out quickly — be ready to catch and support a slippery baby with both hands and a towel.
7. Immediately note the time of birth.
8. Hold the baby level with or slightly below the mother until the cord is cut, and immediately begin drying and stimulating (next section) — this cannot wait.

## 4. Newborn care: airway, warmth, and stimulating breathing
1. **The instant the baby is out, dry it vigorously with a clean towel** — this itself stimulates breathing and prevents dangerous heat loss. Discard the wet towel and wrap the baby in a dry one, including covering the head.
2. Place the baby on the mother's bare chest, skin-to-skin, and cover both with a blanket — this is the best way to keep a newborn warm and stimulates breathing and bonding.
3. Most babies cry or breathe within seconds of drying/stimulation. If the baby is not breathing or crying:
   - Rub the back firmly and flick the soles of the feet.
   - Gently wipe/suction the mouth then nose with a bulb syringe or a clean cloth-wrapped finger if there is visible fluid/mucus blocking the airway.
   - Tilt the head slightly back to a neutral "sniffing" position — not overextended — to open the airway.
   - If there is still no breathing after this stimulation, begin newborn rescue breaths/CPR immediately if you know how (see your first-aid guide for infant CPR) and continue attempts to reach emergency help — this is a true emergency.
4. Check color: pink/normal color on the body is reassuring; blue lips or face after stimulation is a danger sign (see Safety section).
5. Keep the baby warm at all times afterward — hat on, wrapped, skin-to-skin — newborns cannot regulate their own temperature well and chill dangerously fast, even in a warm room.

## 5. The umbilical cord — when and how to tie and cut
1. **There is no emergency reason to cut the cord immediately.** It is normal and beneficial to wait until the cord stops pulsing (typically 1–5 minutes after birth) — this lets extra blood pass from the placenta to the baby.
2. Once the cord has stopped pulsing and looks pale/flattened:
   - Tie it tightly in two places using clean string, shoelace, or cloth strips: one tie about 4 fingers-width (roughly 10 cm) from the baby's belly, a second tie about 2 more finger-widths beyond that.
   - Cut the cord between the two ties using a clean/sterilized (boiled or alcohol-wiped) scissors, knife, or razor blade.
   - There are no nerve endings in the cord — cutting does not hurt the baby or mother.
3. Check the cut end afterward for any bleeding or oozing; if it oozes, add another tie closer to the baby's body.
4. Keep the cord stump clean and dry going forward; do not apply anything to it unless directed by medical guidance later. Watch for redness, swelling, foul smell, or pus in the following days as signs of infection needing medical care.

## 6. Delivering the placenta
1. Mild contractions continue after the baby is born; the placenta typically detaches and delivers within 5–30 minutes.
2. Signs it is ready: a gush of blood, the cord lengthening at the opening, the uterus (belly) becoming firmer and rising.
3. Let the mother push gently when she feels the urge — **do not pull on the cord to speed this up**, which can cause severe bleeding or turn the uterus inside out.
4. Once delivered, save the placenta in a bag or container — medical staff will want to examine it later to confirm it is complete, since retained pieces can cause dangerous bleeding or infection.
5. After the placenta delivers, gently massage the mother's lower abdomen (the fundus, just below the navel) in a circular motion — this helps the uterus contract firmly and is one of the most effective ways to control bleeding (see next section).

## 7. Controlling the mother's bleeding after birth
1. Some bleeding after birth is normal (similar to a heavy period, roughly filling a few pads over the first hour).
2. **Immediately after the placenta delivers, massage the fundus (lower abdomen, at/below the navel) firmly in a circular motion until it feels like a firm grapefruit-sized mass** — this is the single most effective home action to prevent dangerous bleeding.
3. Encourage the baby to nurse/breastfeed as soon as possible — this triggers the mother's body to release a hormone (oxytocin) that helps the uterus contract and slows bleeding.
4. Have the mother empty her bladder (a full bladder can prevent the uterus from contracting well) as soon as she is able.
5. Keep checking the fundus every 10–15 minutes for the first hours and re-massage if it feels soft/boggy.
6. Watch pad/cloth soaking rate closely: soaking through one large pad/cloth in under an hour, or passing clots larger than a golf ball, is heavy bleeding — a true emergency (see Safety section).
7. Keep the mother lying down, warm, and hydrated; give her water and food as she is able.

## Part B — Caring for Vulnerable People in an Emergency

## 8. Infants and children
1. **Hydration is the top priority.** A baby or toddler can become dangerously dehydrated in a matter of hours, especially with vomiting, diarrhea, or heat. Continue breastfeeding on demand — breast milk is the safest food/fluid source in an emergency and needs no clean water or sterilization. For formula-fed babies, use the cleanest available water, boiled and cooled, and the least amount of substitution/stretching possible — never dilute formula to "make it last," which can cause dangerous electrolyte imbalance.
2. If clean water or formula supplies are limited, prioritize them like medication — this is a genuine emergency-priority item.
3. Watch temperature at both extremes: infants overheat and chill far faster than adults. Dress in layers you can add/remove; check the back of the neck (not hands/feet) to judge if a baby is too hot or cold.
4. Offer small, frequent feedings/fluids rather than large infrequent ones, especially if the child is unwell.
5. Keep a familiar comfort item (blanket, pacifier) — stress and disrupted routines are hard on small children; consistency and calm adult behavior help enormously (see the psychological-first-aid guide).
6. For older children, keep explaining what is happening in simple, calm, honest terms appropriate to their age; fear of the unknown is often worse than the event itself.

## 9. The elderly
1. **Medication continuity is the single biggest risk factor.** Keep an up-to-date written list of every medication, dose, and time; keep at least a 2-week backup supply where possible; know which medications (heart, blood pressure, diabetes, blood thinners) cannot be safely skipped even for a day.
2. Mobility: falls become far more likely when routines, lighting, and familiar layouts are disrupted. Keep pathways clear, use a flashlight/lantern rather than moving furniture in the dark, and assist rather than let someone rush.
3. Temperature regulation weakens with age — the elderly are, alongside infants, the highest-risk group for both heat stroke and hypothermia. Check on elderly household members and neighbors at least twice daily during extreme heat or cold.
4. Hydration: thirst sensation declines with age, so an elderly person may not feel thirsty even while dehydrating. Offer fluids on a schedule rather than waiting to be asked.
5. Watch for confusion, unusual quietness, or behavior change — this can be an early sign of dehydration, heat illness, low blood sugar, or infection in older adults, even before other symptoms appear.

## 10. Pregnant women (not currently in labor)
1. Prioritize hydration and regular small meals — nausea, blood sugar swings, and dehydration all worsen faster in pregnancy.
2. Avoid heavy lifting, overheating, and prolonged standing where possible; encourage rest lying on the left side, which improves blood flow.
3. Keep prenatal vitamins and any prescribed medications continuous if at all possible.
4. Know the warning signs that need urgent professional help even outside active labor: vaginal bleeding, severe or sudden swelling (face/hands), severe headache with vision changes, a sudden gush of fluid, or a significant drop in the baby's movement — all need medical evaluation as soon as it can possibly be reached.

## 11. People with disabilities or chronic conditions
1. Medication continuity is again the top priority — insulin, inhalers, heart medications, seizure medications, and psychiatric medications should never be stopped abruptly without medical guidance; running low is itself an emergency worth active problem-solving (rationing carefully, seeking any pharmacy/medical contact possible).
2. For insulin-dependent diabetics: keep insulin as cool as possible (a cooler with ice packs, or a clay-pot/wet-cloth evaporative cooler if no ice); watch closely for signs of low blood sugar (shakiness, confusion, sweating) and high blood sugar (excessive thirst, confusion, fruity breath).
3. For those with mobility equipment (wheelchairs, walkers, oxygen concentrators), plan backup power or manual alternatives in advance — an oxygen concentrator failing during a power outage is a true emergency requiring immediate backup (spare tanks) or evacuation.
4. Maintain routines as closely as possible — predictability reduces distress for many cognitive and developmental conditions.
5. Keep written, portable records of diagnoses, medications, and emergency contacts with the person at all times during a disaster.

## 12. Pets
1. Water and food: keep several days of pet food and clean water set aside; heat and stress increase a pet's water needs.
2. Temperature: pets cannot regulate heat as well as humans in many cases (especially flat-faced/brachycephalic breeds, very young, very old, or overweight animals) — never leave a pet in a parked vehicle, and provide shade/cool water in heat and shelter/insulation in cold.
3. Sanitation: manage waste away from living/water areas to prevent illness spreading in a household already under stress.
4. Keep pets calm and secured — a leash, harness, or carrier at all times during evacuation or chaos, since frightened animals commonly bolt and are difficult to recover afterward.
5. Keep a pet's medications, vaccination records, and a recent photo (for identification if separated) with your emergency supplies.

## Regional & scenario variants
- **North Texas summer heat (infants and elderly):** In Dallas–Fort Worth-area summer heat, indoor temperatures without air conditioning can climb into dangerous ranges within an hour of a power outage. Infants and the elderly are the two groups least able to regulate body temperature and most likely to be harmed first. Move both groups to the coolest available room, use damp cloths on the neck/wrists/forehead, maximize airflow (even hand fans), and prioritize any available battery/generator power for a fan or small AC unit over other uses. Watch infants for fewer wet diapers, a sunken soft spot, or unusual drowsiness — all dehydration signs; watch elderly household members for confusion, weakness, or hot/dry skin — signs of heat stroke that need urgent cooling and, if possible, medical evaluation.
- **North Texas winter cold snaps:** Grid failures during rare North Texas ice events have caused deaths from hypothermia and carbon monoxide (from improvised indoor heating). Keep newborns and the elderly in the warmest interior room, layer blankets over clothing rather than one heavy layer, and never use a generator, grill, or gas stove for heat indoors.
- **Rural/remote birth with no phone signal:** If no communication is possible, send one capable adult to the nearest neighbor, road, or high ground for signal/help while the birth is assisted, rather than waiting until after delivery.
- **Urban apartment/high-rise:** Plan how to move a laboring mother or an elderly/disabled person down stairs if elevators fail; identify this route in advance rather than during the emergency.
- **Multi-person households sheltering together:** Assign one person specifically to "vulnerable person watch" (checking on infants, elderly, and chronic-condition household members on a schedule) so no one is overlooked during a chaotic event.

## ⚠️ Safety — danger signs & when it's an emergency
**Get professional help however possible — radio, phone, sending a runner, flagging down any vehicle — the moment any of these appear, even if birth or care must continue while help is sought:**
- **Breech or any body part other than the head presenting first** (foot, hand, buttocks) — do not pull; support what has emerged and get help urgently.
- **A visible umbilical cord at the opening before the baby** (cord prolapse) — a true emergency; keep the mother's hips elevated above her chest (knee-chest or hips-up position) and avoid pressure on the cord while seeking help immediately.
- **Baby not breathing or crying after drying and stimulation**, or persistent blue color of the lips/face — begin infant rescue breathing/CPR if trained and seek emergency help immediately.
- **Heavy maternal bleeding**: soaking through a large pad/cloth in under an hour, continuous heavy flow, or passing large clots — massage the fundus, keep the mother lying down and warm, and get help immediately; this can become life-threatening quickly.
- **Placenta not delivered within about 30 minutes**, or any part appearing incomplete/torn when examined — needs medical evaluation; do not pull on the cord to force it.
- **Mother with seizures, severe headache with vision changes, or very high blood pressure symptoms** (this can indicate eclampsia) — emergency, needs urgent evacuation/help.
- **Multiple births (twins) or labor before roughly 37 weeks** — higher risk; get help however possible.
- **Any household member (infant, elderly, chronically ill) showing confusion, unresponsiveness, difficulty breathing, chest pain, signs of stroke (face drooping, arm weakness, slurred speech), or a diabetic emergency** — these are always emergencies regardless of how the wider disaster is unfolding.
- **A person with a cut medication supply for insulin, heart, seizure, or psychiatric conditions running out with no replacement in sight** — treat this as an active emergency to solve, not a wait-and-see situation.

## Troubleshooting
- **Cord is tightly wrapped around the neck and won't slip over the head:** Do not panic or yank. If the body is not delivering and the loop is truly too tight to loosen, double-tie and cut the cord promptly between the ties, then continue delivering the body immediately and proceed straight to drying/stimulating the baby.
- **Baby is slippery and hard to hold:** Use a dry towel for grip, support the head/neck in one hand and the body along your forearm — do not squeeze the torso.
- **Bleeding continues despite fundal massage:** Keep massaging, have the mother nurse the baby if possible, keep her lying flat with legs elevated, keep her warm, and continue trying every avenue to reach emergency help — this situation can worsen quickly.
- **No clean string/tie available for the cord:** A boiled/alcohol-wiped shoelace, strip of clean cotton cloth, or even clean dental floss can work in a pinch; the key is it must be tied tightly enough not to slip.
- **Formula supply is running low for an infant:** Prioritize the infant's fluid needs above almost everything else in the household; contact any reachable aid source, and never dilute formula to stretch it.
- **An elderly person refuses to drink or eat during a heat event:** Offer small sips on a schedule rather than a large glass at once, use familiar cups/routines, and treat a sudden refusal combined with confusion as a heat-illness warning sign, not simple stubbornness.
- **A pet becomes aggressive or bolts from fear:** Do not chase into danger; use a calm voice, a leash/carrier, and familiar treats/toys; secure other pets and people first.

## Quick-reference checklist
- [ ] Someone assigned to seek outside help the moment labor or a medical emergency is recognized
- [ ] Hands washed; clean surface and supplies laid out before delivery
- [ ] Cord checked and freed from baby's neck immediately after head delivers
- [ ] Baby dried, stimulated, and skin-to-skin within seconds of birth; breathing/color confirmed
- [ ] Cord tied in two places and cut only after pulsing stops
- [ ] Placenta delivered without pulling on the cord; saved for medical review
- [ ] Fundus massaged firmly after placenta delivers; bleeding monitored closely for hours afterward
- [ ] Mother and baby kept warm, mother hydrated/fed, baby nursing if possible
- [ ] Written medication/allergy/condition list kept current for every household member
- [ ] 2+ week backup medication supply where possible; insulin/refrigerated items kept cool
- [ ] Infants, elderly, and chronically ill checked on a fixed schedule for temperature, hydration, and confusion
- [ ] Pets secured with leash/carrier, and supplied with food, water, and shelter from heat/cold
- [ ] Danger-sign list reviewed by every capable adult in the household before an emergency happens
