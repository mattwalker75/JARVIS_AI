# Emergency Medicine Without a Doctor — Home Medical Kit, Illness Management, and Herbal Remedies

## Read this first
1. **This guide is for a situation where a doctor, pharmacy, or hospital is not reachable** — not a replacement for real medical care when it IS available. If you can get to help, get to help.
2. **Build the kit before you need it.** Most of what matters here (medications, ORS ingredients, splints) is useless if you only think about it during the emergency.
3. **The single most dangerous "small" illness in a disaster is diarrhea and vomiting**, because dehydration kills faster than almost anything else treatable at home. Learn the Oral Rehydration Solution (ORS) recipe below and use it early.
4. **Never guess at prescription drug doses or start antibiotics you are not sure about.** Wrong antibiotic use can make things worse and breeds resistant infection. This guide tells you the warning signs that mean "this needs real medical care," even if that care is days away.
5. **Chronic conditions (diabetes, asthma, heart disease) are the highest-risk category in any disaster** — plan their continuity before anything else in this guide.

---

## Why this matters
In a normal week, a stomach bug, a toothache, or a bad cut is an inconvenience — you call a nurse line, go to urgent care, or fill a prescription. When those systems are gone (extended power outage, road washouts, hospital overwhelmed, or you are isolated for another reason), those same ordinary problems become the things most likely to hurt or kill someone in your household. Infectious disease and dehydration have historically caused more deaths after disasters than the disaster event itself. A stocked kit, a few memorized procedures (especially the ORS recipe), and clear rules for "when this is an emergency" let you handle 90% of what comes up and correctly recognize the 10% that truly needs outside help.

## Key terms
- **OTC** — over-the-counter medication; no prescription needed.
- **ORS (Oral Rehydration Solution)** — a precise mix of water, salt, and sugar that replaces fluid and electrolytes lost to vomiting/diarrhea faster and more effectively than water alone.
- **Electrolytes** — salts (sodium, potassium, chloride) the body needs in the right balance to function; lost heavily during vomiting, diarrhea, and heavy sweating.
- **Systemic infection / sepsis** — an infection that has spread beyond the original site into the bloodstream or whole body; life-threatening and needs emergency care.
- **Cellulitis** — a spreading bacterial skin infection (redness, warmth, swelling spreading outward).
- **Antipyretic** — a fever-reducing medicine (e.g., acetaminophen, ibuprofen).
- **Analgesic** — a pain-relieving medicine.
- **Contraindication** — a reason NOT to use a drug or remedy (e.g., an allergy, another condition, a drug interaction, an age restriction).
- **Reye's syndrome** — a rare but severe illness linked to giving aspirin (or willow bark, which is chemically similar) to children/teens recovering from viral illness.
- **Splint** — a rigid support that immobilizes an injured limb.
- **Continuity of care** — the plan for keeping a chronic condition (diabetes, asthma, etc.) stable when your normal medical routine is disrupted.

## What you need
**Home medical kit — core contents:**
- Digital thermometer, spare batteries
- Blood pressure cuff (if anyone has heart/BP issues), pulse oximeter if available
- Bandaging: adhesive bandages (assorted sizes), sterile gauze pads and rolls, medical tape, non-stick pads, an elastic (ACE) wrap, triangular bandages
- Wound care: antiseptic (povidone-iodine or chlorhexidine), antibiotic ointment, saline for irrigation, tweezers, small scissors, safety pins, disposable gloves (several pairs), a few N95/surgical masks
- Instruments: tweezers, splinter probe, small sharp scissors, a needle (for splinter/blister care, sterilized by flame + alcohol), safety razor
- Splinting/immobilization: SAM splint or rigid padding material, triangular bandages/slings, an instant cold pack
- Medication measuring: oral syringe or dosing cup (dosing by teaspoon is unreliable)
- Documentation: a written list of every household member's allergies, conditions, and current medications with dosages; a small notebook and pen to log symptoms/temperatures/medication times

**OTC medications to stock (check expiration dates yearly; always keep original boxes with printed dosing):**
- Fever/pain: acetaminophen (e.g., Tylenol) and ibuprofen (e.g., Advil/Motrin) — both adult and children's/liquid forms if kids are in the household
- Anti-diarrheal: loperamide (Imodium)
- Antacid/anti-nausea: an antacid (e.g., calcium carbonate), an H2 blocker or PPI if someone uses one regularly, oral rehydration salts (commercial packets) or the sugar/salt to make your own (below)
- Allergy: an antihistamine (diphenhydramine and/or a non-drowsy one like loratadine)
- Topical: hydrocortisone cream, antifungal cream, antibiotic ointment, calamine lotion
- Other: oral electrolyte powder/tablets, cough drops, saline nasal spray, a laxative/stool softener

**Prescription medications:**
- A minimum 2–4 week extra supply of every regularly-taken prescription, rotated so it doesn't expire (ask your pharmacist about "vacation overrides" or emergency supply policies in advance — this must be arranged before the emergency)
- A written list of exact drug names, doses, and the prescribing reason, in case someone else has to manage care
- **Never share prescription drugs between people or take someone else's medication.**

**Improvised supplies worth knowing:** clean cloth/bandanas as bandages, plastic wrap as an occlusive dressing, duct tape, a belt or magazine as a splint, a clean sock filled with rice or dry beans as a cold/heat pack (chill or gently warm it), honey as a wound dressing (see Herbal section), and clean water as the basis of every treatment below.

## Building and Stocking a Medical Kit

1. **Start with a container** that is waterproof, portable, and clearly labeled — a hard-sided tackle box or a labeled tote works well.
2. **Sort into categories** (wound care, medications, tools, documentation) using zip bags or small pouches so anyone can find what they need under stress.
3. **Stock OTC medicines listed above**, buying store brand (identical active ingredients, cheaper) where possible.
4. **Talk to your doctor/pharmacist now** about a small emergency backup supply of prescriptions — most insurance and pharmacies have a process for this if you ask before disaster strikes.
5. **Add improvised-supply items** (see above) that don't expire and take no space.
6. **Write down every household member's allergies, conditions, blood type (if known), and medications** on a laminated card in the kit.
7. **Check and rotate the kit twice a year** — replace expired medicine, restock used bandages, replace batteries.

## Managing Common Illness Without a Doctor

### Fever
1. Fever is usually the body fighting infection, not the danger itself — treat the person's comfort, not just the number.
2. Encourage fluids constantly; dehydration makes fever feel worse.
3. Follow the **package label dosing exactly** for acetaminophen or ibuprofen based on age/weight — do not combine both without checking labels, and never exceed the daily maximum printed on the box.
4. Use lukewarm sponging and light clothing to help comfort; avoid ice baths or alcohol rubs (can cause shivering or, with alcohol, be absorbed through skin).
5. **Seek real medical help if:** fever is above 103°F (39.4°C) in an adult and not responding to medication, any fever in an infant under 3 months, fever with stiff neck/severe headache/confusion/rash, a seizure occurs, or fever lasts more than 3 days.

### Diarrhea and Vomiting — the ORS recipe
This is the most important procedure in this entire guide. Dehydration from diarrhea/vomiting is a leading cause of preventable death in disasters, especially in children and the elderly.

1. **Stop feeding solids to a vomiting person temporarily** — sips of fluid come first.
2. **Make Oral Rehydration Solution (ORS):**
   - **1 liter (about 1 quart / 4 cups) of clean, safe drinking water** (boiled and cooled, or otherwise disinfected — see the water guide)
   - **6 level teaspoons of sugar**
   - **1/2 level teaspoon of table salt**
   - Stir until fully dissolved. Taste it — it should taste no saltier than tears; if it tastes very salty, you measured wrong — discard and remake it, because too much salt is dangerous, especially for children.
   - This is the standard emergency home ORS ratio (WHO/UNICEF-style formula). If you have a commercial oral rehydration salts packet, use it instead and follow its label exactly — commercial packets also include potassium and are more balanced.
   - Optional if available: adding a few tablespoons of orange juice or a mashed banana to a portion of the solution (drunk separately, not required) helps replace potassium — do not add to the exact ORS batch since it changes the measured ratio.
3. **Dosing:** give small, frequent sips — a few teaspoons at a time in infants, a cup every 15–20 minutes for an adult — even if vomiting continues; some fluid is absorbed even with ongoing vomiting. Continue replacing fluid roughly matching each diarrhea/vomiting episode.
4. **Diet:** once tolerating fluids, resume bland, simple foods (rice, bananas, toast, broth) in small amounts. Avoid dairy, caffeine, and very sugary or fatty foods until recovered.
5. **Hygiene:** isolate the person's utensils/linens, wash hands thoroughly, and treat this as potentially contagious (see sanitation-hygiene guide).

**When diarrhea/vomiting is dangerous — get real medical help if you can, and treat aggressively with ORS while you try:**
- Signs of severe dehydration: sunken eyes, no tears when crying, dry mouth, little or no urination for 6+ hours (infants) or 8+ hours (adults), skin that stays "tented" when pinched, extreme lethargy or confusion, rapid heartbeat, sunken soft spot in an infant's skull
- Blood in vomit or stool, black/tarry stool
- High fever with severe abdominal pain
- Vomiting that prevents keeping down ANY fluid for more than a few hours (especially in infants/young children or the elderly)
- Symptoms lasting more than 2–3 days without improvement

### Respiratory infections (colds, bronchitis-type symptoms)
1. Rest, fluids, and fever/pain control as above.
2. Use a humidifier or a bowl of hot water with a towel tent for steam inhalation to ease congestion.
3. Honey (see Herbal section) can soothe cough in those over age 1.
4. Saline nasal spray/rinse for congestion.
5. **Signs this may be more serious (pneumonia or needing real care):** difficulty breathing or breathing fast, chest pain, bluish lips/fingertips, high fever that won't come down, confusion, coughing up blood or thick discolored sputum for several days, or any breathing difficulty in an infant, elderly person, or anyone with existing lung/heart disease. These need medical evaluation and possibly antibiotics or oxygen — do not try to guess an antibiotic on your own for this.

### Urinary and skin infections — recognizing spread
1. **Skin infections:** clean the wound, keep it covered, watch daily for changes.
2. **Signs of a spreading (worsening) infection:** redness that is expanding beyond a marked border, increasing warmth/swelling/pain, red streaking running up a limb, pus, fever, chills, or feeling generally very unwell. Red streaking especially is a sign the infection is entering the lymphatic system and can become dangerous quickly.
3. **Urinary infection signs:** burning with urination, urgency/frequency, lower abdominal pain; **flank pain, fever, chills, nausea/vomiting** suggest the infection has reached the kidneys — more serious.
4. **This is the category where antibiotics may genuinely be needed** — but do NOT take leftover or old antibiotics from a prior illness for a new infection, even if you have some on hand. Per CDC guidance, this is unsafe: it's very likely the wrong drug or wrong dose for this organism, a partial/incomplete leftover course can fail to clear the infection and drives antibiotic resistance, and self-dosing can mask worsening illness and delay real care. Instead, focus on recognizing the danger signs below and doing everything possible to reach a real medical professional (even by radio or phone) for guidance — keep the wound clean, elevate/rest, and monitor closely while you try. **Danger signs that mean you must reach real care by any means possible:** spreading/expanding redness, red streaking up a limb, increasing swelling or pain, pus, fever, chills, or feeling generally very unwell — these mean the infection is worsening and needs an actual medical evaluation, not a guess.

### Pain management
1. Use acetaminophen or ibuprofen per package label dosing; ibuprofen is also anti-inflammatory (helpful for sprains/injuries with swelling), acetaminophen is gentler on the stomach and safe alongside ibuprofen (they are different drug classes) if each is dosed within its own label limits.
2. Rest, ice (first 24–48 hours) then heat, elevation, and gentle compression help musculoskeletal pain.
3. Do not exceed labeled maximum daily doses; avoid ibuprofen/NSAIDs in anyone with kidney disease, active ulcers, or on blood thinners without medical guidance.
4. **Severe, uncontrolled pain, especially chest, abdominal, or pain after major trauma, needs real medical evaluation** — it is often the body's alarm signal for something serious.

## Managing Chronic Conditions in an Emergency

### Diabetes
- Keep 2–4 weeks of insulin/oral medication on hand; know that unopened insulin needs refrigeration but many insulins tolerate room temperature (under ~86°F/30°C) for about a month once in use — check your specific product's label, and use a cooler with ice packs or a evaporative cooling wallet if refrigeration is lost.
- Keep glucose tablets/gel or hard candy on hand for low blood sugar; keep a glucose meter with extra strips and batteries.
- Stick to as regular a meal/medication schedule as possible; check blood sugar more often under stress, since illness, heat, and disrupted eating swing levels quickly.
- **Danger signs:** confusion, shaking, sweating, or fainting (possible low blood sugar — give fast sugar immediately); extreme thirst, frequent urination, fruity-smelling breath, vomiting, or rapid breathing (possible dangerously high blood sugar/diabetic ketoacidosis) — both are emergencies needing real medical care as soon as possible.

### Asthma
- Keep a rescue inhaler (and spacer) accessible at all times, plus a backup; check it isn't expired.
- Avoid known triggers (smoke, dust, mold) which are often worse after disasters (fires, flooding, debris).
- **Danger signs:** rescue inhaler not relieving symptoms, inability to speak full sentences, lips/fingertips turning blue, or breathing that looks like the ribs/neck are pulling in with each breath — this is a medical emergency.

### Heart disease / high blood pressure
- Keep a full backup supply of all heart/BP medications — do not stop these abruptly even if supply is tight; running out gradually while trying to reach more is safer than stopping suddenly for some medications (discuss with your doctor in advance which of your specific medications are dangerous to stop abruptly).
- Monitor blood pressure if a cuff is available; keep a log.
- Limit salt where BP is a concern, but do not restrict fluids/salt during active vomiting/diarrhea (ORS need overrides routine salt restriction short-term).
- **Danger signs:** chest pain/pressure, pain spreading to arm/jaw, severe shortness of breath, fainting, or sudden weakness/numbness/facial drooping/slurred speech (possible stroke) — these need emergency care immediately; do not manage these at home.

## Dental Emergencies

1. **Toothache:** rinse with warm salt water (about 1/2 teaspoon salt in a cup of warm water), floss gently to remove trapped debris, and use OTC pain medication per label. **Clove oil** applied on a small cotton swab directly to the sore tooth/gum is a well-known traditional numbing remedy (its active compound, eugenol, has topical anesthetic and antiseptic properties) — use sparingly, avoid swallowing, and avoid contact with soft tissue/gums for extended periods to prevent irritation.
2. **Lost filling or crown:** clean the area gently; a temporary dental cement or even sugar-free chewing gum pressed into the cavity can protect it short term until real dental care is available. Avoid chewing on that side.
3. **Knocked-out tooth:** handle by the crown (not the root), rinse gently if dirty (don't scrub), and try to reinsert it in the socket if possible; if not, keep it in milk or the person's saliva and get to a dentist within 30–60 minutes if at all possible — time is critical for saving the tooth.
4. **Abscess (a painful, swollen, pus-filled infection at a tooth root):** this is a serious infection, not just a toothache. Warm salt-water rinses may give some relief, but an abscess **needs real medical/dental treatment** (drainage and likely antibiotics) because it can spread to the jaw, neck, or bloodstream. **Signs it is becoming an emergency:** facial or neck swelling, fever, difficulty swallowing or breathing, or swelling spreading toward the eye or down the neck — treat this as a true emergency and seek care by any means possible.
5. **General rule:** dental pain that is worsening, accompanied by facial swelling, or fever, is not something to just "wait out" — it should push you to find real care urgently.

## Herbal & Natural Remedies

These are traditional and modestly evidence-supported remedies for minor symptom relief — they are not substitutes for the medical care and warning-sign guidance above, and none of them treat a spreading infection, severe dehydration, or a chronic-condition emergency.

- **Honey** — for minor wound dressing (raw or medical-grade honey has antibacterial and moisture-retaining properties) and for soothing cough. **Contraindication: never give honey to a child under 1 year old** (risk of infant botulism).
- **Aloe vera** — the clear gel from a broken leaf soothes minor burns and sunburn. **Contraindication:** do not use on deep or infected wounds; avoid ingesting aloe products (the latex/leaf sap, distinct from the gel) which can cause severe cramping/diarrhea, especially in pregnancy.
- **Willow bark** — contains salicin, chemically related to aspirin, traditionally used for pain/inflammation, prepared as a tea from the inner bark. **Contraindication: do NOT give to children or teenagers (Reye's syndrome risk, same as aspirin)**, and avoid if allergic to aspirin/NSAIDs, on blood thinners, or with bleeding/ulcer disorders; effects are milder and slower than actual aspirin, so do not rely on it for serious pain.
- **Ginger** — traditionally used as a tea or chewed fresh/candied for nausea and motion sickness. **Contraindication:** use caution at high doses with blood-thinning medication or gallstone disease.
- **Peppermint** — tea or diluted oil traditionally eases nausea and upset stomach. **Contraindication:** avoid peppermint oil in infants/young children (can cause choking/breathing spasm) and in those with severe acid reflux (can worsen it).
- **Garlic** — traditionally used for its antimicrobial properties, eaten raw/cooked or as a poultice on unbroken skin only. **Contraindication:** avoid medicinal (high) doses before surgery or with blood-thinning medication; can irritate skin/stomach; never place raw garlic directly into an ear or open wound.
- **Plantain (the common broadleaf weed, *Plantago major/lanceolata* — not the banana relative)** — leaves crushed or chewed into a poultice traditionally applied to minor cuts, scrapes, and insect stings/bites for soothing and mild drawing action. **Contraindication:** positively identify the plant before use; do not use on deep or heavily contaminated wounds in place of proper cleaning.
- **Yarrow** — leaves traditionally crushed and applied to minor cuts as a mild astringent/styptic (helps slow minor bleeding). **Contraindication: avoid during pregnancy**, avoid on deep wounds needing real closure, and some people develop skin irritation/allergy (related to the ragweed/daisy family) — patch-test if possible.

**General rule for all herbal remedies:** use only plants you can positively identify, use for minor/self-limited symptoms only, discontinue if any rash, worsening symptoms, or allergic reaction occurs, and never delay real medical care for a serious problem in favor of an herbal remedy.

## Improvised Medical Tools

1. **Splint (for a suspected broken bone or bad sprain):** find something rigid (a stick, rolled magazine, SAM splint, folded cardboard). Pad it with cloth. Place it against the injured limb, immobilizing the joint above AND below the injury. Tie it snugly with cloth strips, a belt, or tape — snug enough to hold still, loose enough that circulation isn't cut off (check fingers/toes stay pink and warm).
2. **Sling:** use a triangular bandage (or a folded shirt/pillowcase) to support an injured arm — one point over the shoulder on the injured side, the bandage under the forearm, tied at the opposite shoulder/neck, elbow bent roughly 90 degrees.
3. **Tourniquet (last resort — life-threatening limb bleeding that direct pressure has failed to stop):** use a wide band of cloth (at least 2 inches) or a commercial tourniquet, placed 2–3 inches above the wound (never directly on a joint), tightened until bleeding stops. **Write the exact time it was applied** on the person or a nearby note — this is critical information for anyone who treats them later. A tourniquet is painful and can damage the limb if left long; it is a bleeding-control measure of last resort when direct pressure fails, not a routine first step.
4. **Stretcher:** two sturdy poles run through the sleeves of two or three turned-inside-out jackets/shirts (buttoned/zipped), or a blanket/tarp rolled around two poles, can carry an injured person. Always support the head/neck if a spinal injury is possible, and use at least 2–4 people to carry evenly.

## Regional & scenario variants

- **North Texas note:** Summer heat (regularly 100°F+) makes heat-related illness and dehydration far more likely to compound any of the illnesses above — treat ORS and fluid replacement as even higher priority, and watch for heat exhaustion/heat stroke symptoms alongside any GI illness. Fire ant stings and stinging-insect reactions are common regionally — keep antihistamines and know the signs of an anaphylactic reaction (facial/throat swelling, difficulty breathing, widespread hives) as a true emergency needing epinephrine if available. Flash flooding and severe storms can cut off road access for days, so plan medication supply buffers around this. Winter ice storms (as in past North Texas events) can knock out power for days, affecting insulin/medication refrigeration — have a cooler and ice-pack plan ready.
- **Rural/isolated scenario:** distances to real care are longer — build a larger medication buffer, have a wider improvised-tool skill set, and pre-arrange a way to signal or transport someone (see signaling-rescue guide).
- **General/urban scenario:** hospitals may be reachable but overwhelmed — triage your own household's problems honestly using the danger signs in this guide before deciding to go in person.

## ⚠️ Safety — warnings & mistakes to avoid
- Do not guess prescription drug doses, combine medications you're unsure about, or use someone else's prescription.
- Do not give aspirin OR willow bark to children/teens with a viral illness (Reye's syndrome risk).
- Do not give honey to infants under 1 year old.
- Do not rely on herbal remedies for a spreading infection, severe dehydration, chest pain, breathing difficulty, or any of the "danger sign" lists above — these need real medical care.
- Do not stop chronic-condition medications (heart, BP, seizure) abruptly without knowing whether that specific drug is dangerous to stop suddenly — plan this with your doctor before an emergency, not during one.
- Do not leave a tourniquet on "just in case" — only use it for true life-threatening bleeding, and always note the time applied.
- Do not use expired medications if any safer alternative exists, and never use medication that has been exposed to extreme heat, contamination, or has changed color/smell.
- Do not delay care for a possible abscess, dental swelling, red-streaking infection, or any chronic-condition danger sign — these can worsen quickly.

## Troubleshooting
- **"I don't have exact measuring spoons for the ORS."** Use any consistent small spoon and be consistent — the ratio matters more than exact volume; err toward less salt if unsure, and taste-test (should taste like tears, not seawater).
- **"The person won't keep any fluid down."** Give a teaspoon at a time, waiting a minute or two between sips, even during ongoing vomiting — small amounts are absorbed even if larger amounts trigger vomiting; if this fails for hours, especially in a small child or elderly person, treat as urgent and seek care.
- **"I only have expired medication."** Most solid tablets remain partially effective somewhat past printed dates if stored cool/dry, but potency is uncertain and liquids/insulin degrade faster — use only if no alternative exists, and never for a life-threatening situation if any better option is available.
- **"I don't know if this is 'serious enough' for antibiotics."** If you're unsure, that uncertainty itself is a reason to try harder to reach any medical guidance (even remote/radio) rather than start antibiotics blind; document symptoms and their progression so you can describe them accurately if you do reach help.
- **"We're out of a specific chronic medication."** Try to stretch remaining doses evenly rather than stopping abruptly, seek any pharmacy/medical outreach available, and watch closely for that condition's specific danger signs listed above.
