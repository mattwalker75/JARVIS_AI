## Read this first

In a real survival food emergency, your first move is **not** to grab a stick and go hunt. Your first move is to set multiple passive traps and lines, then go do something else (build shelter, gather water, rest) while they work for you. Passive methods — snares, deadfall traps, set fishing lines, fish traps — collect food while you conserve calories and energy. Active hunting with improvised weapons is a last resort or a supplement, not the primary plan, because it burns more calories than it usually returns and carries a much higher failure rate for an untrained person.

This guide assumes you have no rifle, no fishing gear, and no trapping kit — everything here can be improvised from cordage, wire, wood, and things found in a pack or on the ground. If you have any manufactured hooks, snare wire, or a firearm, use them; improvised gear is what you fall back to when you don't.

Everything in this guide is written for a genuine survival emergency where normal food supply has failed. It is not a hunting or fishing how-to for recreational use, and it does not replace your local game laws in normal times (see the Regional & scenario variants section).

## Why this matters

Humans can survive roughly three weeks without food, but performance, judgment, and morale degrade fast on zero calories, and injury/illness risk rises steeply once you are weak. In a prolonged grid-down or wilderness survival scenario, water and shelter come first, but food becomes critical past the first few days, especially for children, the elderly, or anyone recovering from injury or illness.

The core lesson experienced survival instructors teach: **set it and forget it beats chase it and catch it.** A single snare or fish trap works around the clock, in the rain, at night, while you sleep — for zero ongoing calorie cost after the initial setup. Ten snares set along game trails can be checked twice a day for the cost of a short walk. Active hunting requires sustained energy, stealth, and often multiple failed attempts before a single success — a bad trade when your calorie reserves are already low.

## Key terms

- **Snare** — a loop of cordage or wire that tightens around an animal's neck or body as it moves through, usually triggered by the animal's own momentum.
- **Deadfall trap** — a trap that uses a weight (a log or rock) to crush or pin the animal, triggered by a disturbed support stick.
- **Figure-4 trigger** — a simple three-stick trigger mechanism shaped like the numeral 4 that holds up a deadfall weight until disturbed.
- **Gorge hook** — a straight or slightly curved sliver of bone, wood, or metal, sharpened at both ends and tied in the middle to the line; the fish swallows it and it lodges crosswise in the throat/gut.
- **Trotline** — a single main fishing line, staked or anchored at both ends across a stream or pond, with multiple short baited lines (snoods) hanging off it at intervals.
- **Fish weir** — a funnel-shaped fence or wall built across a stream that channels fish into a narrowing trap or holding pen.
- **Game trail** — a worn path through vegetation used repeatedly by animals moving between bedding, water, and feeding areas; identifiable by tracks, droppings, and worn dirt/matted grass.
- **Field dressing** — removing an animal's internal organs promptly after the kill to stop bacterial growth and cool the meat.
- **Trichinosis** — a parasitic roundworm disease from undercooked pork/wild hog (and some other meat) that can be serious or fatal; killed by thorough cooking.
- **Set line / trotline check** — the routine of walking your traps/lines on a schedule (ideally 2x/day) to collect catches, reset triggers, and re-bait.
- **Bank line** — thin, strong cord (like tarred nylon mason's line) used for trotlines, gorge-hook rigs, and snares; a small coil is extremely valuable survival gear.

## What you need

Minimum gear (can all be improvised):
- Cordage: paracord, bank line, wire, or plant-fiber cordage (see tools-knots-repair.md) — for snare loops and trotlines.
- Wire (picture-hanging wire, snare wire, or stripped wire from electronics) — holds its shape for snare loops, ideal for rabbit/squirrel snares.
- A sharp knife or improvised cutting edge — for carving triggers, gorge hooks, and field dressing.
- Small sharp objects for hooks: fish bones, thorns (locust, hawthorn), hardwood slivers, safety pins, sewing needles, small nails.
- Bait: worms, grubs, insects, small fish pieces, entrails, bright bits of cloth or foil (for gorge hooks/lures), scent bait (peanut butter, animal fat) for land snares.
- Straight, strong sticks in a range of sizes for deadfall triggers and throwing sticks.
- A container or bark/leaf sheet for carrying water-caught food (crawfish, mussels) without losing them.
- Fire-making capability (see fire.md) — you must cook everything thoroughly.
- Gloves or a rag if available, to handle entrails and avoid cuts during field dressing.

## Step-by-step: build a survival food plan

1. **Scout for sign before building anything.** Walk likely areas (stream banks, field edges, brush lines) looking for game trails, tracks, droppings, chewed vegetation, and burrows. Fish sign includes riffles, undercut banks, deep pools below rapids, and submerged logs/structure.
2. **Set passive traps and lines first, in volume.** One trap rarely succeeds. Set 6-12 snares and/or a trotline with 8-10 hooks the first day if materials allow. More sets = more statistical chance of a catch.
3. **Place along travel routes, not randomly.** Snares go on active game trails at narrow points (through brush, under logs, between two obstacles) where the animal has one obvious path.
4. **Bait to improve odds** where the species responds to bait (fish, some traps); rely on trail placement alone for most land snares, since bait can make animals wary of new smells at first.
5. **Check every trap and line at least twice daily** — morning and evening. This is not optional: unchecked catches spoil, are stolen by scavengers, or the animal suffers needlessly. Checking often also lets you re-set failed or sprung triggers quickly.
6. **Process every catch immediately** — see the Processing Game section below. Do not let a catch sit in the heat.
7. **Cook everything thoroughly, no exceptions**, then eat promptly or begin preservation (see food-preservation-cooking.md) if you have more meat than you can eat in one sitting.
8. **Reset and relocate traps that repeatedly fail.** If a spot produces nothing after 2-3 days, move it — sign may indicate the trail is not currently active.

## Method 1: Simple wire/cordage snare (small game)

A snare is a fixed loop that tightens on an animal as it pushes through, choking or holding it in place. This is the single highest-value beginner skill in this whole guide — cheap, fast, and passive.

**Build steps:**
1. Cut 18-24 inches of snare wire (best) or strong cordage.
2. Form a small loop (about the size of the target animal's head/neck — roughly 4 inches for rabbits, 2-3 inches for squirrels) using a slip-knot or a simple wire twist that lets the loop cinch down but not loosen back up.
3. Thread the free end of the wire/cord through the loop's anchor point so the loop can slide closed as something pulls through it.
4. Tie or anchor the free end firmly to a nearby sapling, stake, or overhanging branch above the game trail — strong enough that the animal cannot pull the whole snare away.
5. Suspend the loop across the game trail at the height of the target animal's head (roughly a fist-width above ground for rabbits, higher for larger animals), centered in a natural pinch-point (gap in brush, hole in a fence line, worn tunnel through grass).
6. Lightly brace the loop open with two small twigs on either side so it holds its shape but is knocked loose easily when something moves through it.

**Verification:** Sight down the trail — the loop should be the only obstacle-free path. Tug-test the anchor to confirm it will hold. **The loop should be set open** (held in its round shape by the twig braces, not already cinched tight) **so it can slide closed the instant something pushes through it** — check that it moves freely and isn't jammed or pre-tightened.

## Method 2: Figure-4 deadfall trap

The figure-4 uses a weight (flat rock or heavy log) balanced on a three-stick trigger shaped like the numeral "4." **The trap does not work without this weight — the sticks alone only form the trigger; you must have a rock or log heavy enough to kill or pin the animal resting on the horizontal stick.** When an animal disturbs the bait stick, the trigger collapses and the weight drops.

**Build steps (three sticks + one flat weight):**
1. **Upright stick** (~8-10 in): notched near the top to hold the diagonal stick, and carved to a chisel point at the bottom to bite into the ground.
2. **Diagonal/bait stick** (~10-12 in): notched partway along its length to lock against the upright, and pointed on the end that holds bait.
3. **Horizontal/release stick** (~6-8 in): notched to interlock with both other sticks, forming the "4" shape, with one end resting against the ground to hold the weight up.
4. Assemble the three sticks so they interlock under tension — this takes practice; the connections should be delicately balanced, holding themselves together only through pressure, not tied.
5. Rest a flat, heavy rock or short heavy log on the horizontal stick so its weight is what holds the whole trigger under tension.
6. Bait the point of the diagonal stick (peanut butter, seeds, a bit of fruit, or fatty meat scraps).
7. When an animal tugs or gnaws the bait stick, the trigger collapses and the weight falls straight down onto the animal.

```
    FIGURE-4 DEADFALL TRIGGER (side view, weight not shown resting on top)

               ______ horizontal/release stick
              /
             /_________ diagonal/bait stick (baited tip ->) [bait]
            /
      _____/
     |
     | upright stick (point driven into ground)
     |
   __|________________________ ground
```
*Caption: Three notched sticks interlock into a "4" shape under the weight of a flat rock or log; disturbing the baited diagonal stick collapses the whole trigger and drops the weight.*

**Verification:** Set the trigger with a light test object (not your hand) first — nudge the bait stick and confirm the weight drops cleanly and fast, with nothing hanging up. Place the whole assembly so the weight will fall on a flat, clear patch of ground with no obstruction. Never set a deadfall so the trigger could drop on a hand, foot, or curious child.

## Method 3: Fishing with improvised hooks & hand lines

1. **Improvise a hook.** Options: bend a thorn (hawthorn, locust) into a barb; carve a small "V" or curved hook from hardwood or bone; bend a safety pin, sewing needle, or piece of wire into a hook shape; or use a **gorge hook** if no curved hook is possible.
2. **Gorge hook (works when you cannot make a curved barb):** carve or grind a straight sliver of bone, hardwood, or metal about 1-1.5 inches long, sharpened at both ends, with a notch or hole in the center. Tie your line tightly to the center notch. Embed the gorge inside a small bait ball or piece of bait so the fish swallows it whole; when the fish tries to swim off, a tug on the line turns the gorge sideways and lodges it crosswise in the throat.
3. **Rig a hand line:** tie your hook/gorge to 3-6 feet of line, add a small weight (pebble, nut, washer) a few inches above the hook to sink it, and tie the other end around a stick, can, or your hand.
4. **Bait with what's available:** worms, grubs, insects, small pieces of fish or meat, freshwater mussels out of the shell.
5. **Fish where fish hold:** undercut banks, submerged logs and brush piles, the slack water below riffles and rapids, deep pools, drop-offs at the edge of a channel, and shaded areas in hot weather.

## Method 4: Trotline & set lines (passive, multiplies your catch rate)

1. Tie a length of strong cordage (bank line) across a stream, pond edge, or between two anchor points (stakes driven into the bank, or a stake and a rock/tree).
2. Along the main line, tie short "snood" lines every 2-4 feet, each rigged with a hook or gorge and bait.
3. Weight the main line so it sits a foot or two under the surface, or stake it just above the bottom in slow water.
4. Anchor both ends solidly — a fish fighting the line should not be able to pull a stake loose.
5. Check the trotline at least twice daily; re-bait empty hooks and remove catches promptly (a fish left too long can be stolen by turtles, other fish, or scavengers, or it can spoil).

## Method 5: Fish traps & weirs

1. In a narrow, shallow stream, build a **V-shaped or funnel weir** from stakes and woven brush, walls angled to funnel fish toward a narrow gap.
2. At the gap, place a basket-style trap (woven from flexible green branches, tapering to a narrow, hard-to-reverse-out-of opening) or simply a holding pool the fish cannot easily swim back out of.
3. Fish moving downstream (or up, depending on season/species) get funneled into the trap and cannot find their way back out through the narrow entrance.
4. Check weirs at least once or twice daily; in good locations they can produce fish continuously with zero ongoing effort.

## Method 6: Gathering — crawfish & mussels

1. **Crawfish:** turn over rocks and submerged debris in shallow, slow-moving creek sections; grab from behind the head/thorax to avoid the pincers, or use a small dip net/basket. Crawfish traps can also be baited with meat scraps and left overnight in a weighted, sunk container with a narrow entrance.
2. **Freshwater mussels:** feel along muddy/sandy stream bottoms and creek banks by hand, especially in shallow riffle areas; look for partially buried shells. Cook thoroughly; discard any that do not open when cooked, and any found already open/dead before cooking.

## Method 7: Basic small-game hunting & improvised weapons

Active hunting is your backup, used opportunistically alongside your trap lines, not instead of them.

1. **Throwing stick (rabbit stick):** a heavy, slightly curved hardwood stick about 2-3 feet long, thrown side-arm at rabbits or squirrels on the ground or low in a tree. Effective only at close range (10-20 ft) with practice.
2. **Sling:** a simple pouch of cloth/leather with two cords, used to hurl a fist-sized stone. Requires significant practice to be accurate; useful on birds and small game in open ground.
3. **Improvised spear:** a straight sapling 5-6 feet long, sharpened and fire-hardened at the tip (char the point lightly in fire, then scrape it sharp again — this hardens the wood). Useful for fish in shallow water or as a defensive/finishing tool, less so for hunting mobile land game.
4. **Stalking basics:** move slowly and only when the animal's head is down or attention is elsewhere; stay downwind (scent carries with the wind toward the animal, so approach from upwind of your position relative to them, i.e., wind blowing from them to you); keep your silhouette broken by brush; freeze completely if the animal alerts, then resume only once it relaxes.
5. Small game (rabbits, squirrels) is a far more realistic target for improvised weapons than deer or hogs — do not expend energy stalking large game without a proper weapon.

## Processing game — field dressing & safe cooking

**Field dressing (step-by-step, general small-to-medium game):**
1. Work as soon as possible after the kill — bacteria and gas buildup start immediately, especially in warm weather.
2. Lay the animal on its back on a clean surface (leaves, bark, a flat rock) — never on bare dirt if avoidable.
3. Make a shallow cut from the lower belly up toward the ribcage, cutting only through skin and muscle — angle the blade edge-up and use two fingers to lift the skin away from the gut so you do not puncture the intestines or bladder (puncturing these contaminates the meat).
4. Reach in and pull out the internal organs, cutting connective tissue as needed; save the liver and heart if you can identify them confidently and they look healthy (firm, uniform color, no spots, cysts, or abscesses) — these are nutrient-dense and safe once cooked thoroughly.
5. If organs look discolored, spotted, swollen, or foul-smelling, or if you see cysts/worms, discard the entire animal — do not eat it.
6. Remove the skin/fur (skinning) after gutting; keep the meat away from fur, dirt, and the outside of the hide, which carry bacteria and parasites.
   - **Rabbits and hares — wear gloves while skinning.** Tularemia ("rabbit fever") spreads through skin contact with infected blood/tissue and is a real risk in wild cottontails/jackrabbits. Inspect the liver and spleen: white spots or lesions are a warning sign of infection — discard the entire animal if you see them. Cook rabbit meat well-done regardless.
7. Rinse the body cavity with clean water if available; do not use contaminated water.
8. Cool the meat as fast as possible — hang it in shade, in moving air, off the ground; do not leave field-dressed meat sitting in sun or a warm pack.
9. For fish: gut similarly (slit belly, remove innards), scale or skin, and rinse; process within a couple of hours of catching, sooner in hot weather.

**Cooking — non-negotiable safety rule:** cook all wild meat and fish thoroughly and completely. There is no such thing as safe rare wild game in a survival context. **A meat thermometer is the primary, reliable way to check doneness — color and "juices run clear" are NOT reliable indicators of safety** (meat can look fully cooked and still harbor live parasites, or look pink and be perfectly safe). If you have a thermometer, wild game meat should reach at least **165°F (74°C)** throughout at the thickest part, and pork/feral hog meat should reach at least **160–165°F (71–74°C)** throughout with no pink remaining, given trichinosis risk (see Safety section). If you have no thermometer, cook well past the point where juices run clear and no pink remains anywhere at the bone/thickest part, and err toward overcooking. Boiling and thorough roasting/smoking are your most reliable methods in the field (see food-preservation-cooking.md).

**Quick preservation after cooking:** if you have more meat than you can eat immediately, smoke or dry it into strips (jerky-style) over a low fire, or keep it hot/re-boil daily if you lack smoking capability — see food-preservation-cooking.md for full technique.

## Regional & scenario variants

- **North Texas note:** Common local targets include whitetail deer, feral hogs, cottontail/jackrabbits, fox and gray squirrels, mourning doves, and in ponds/creeks/rivers — catfish, largemouth/spotted bass, sunfish (bream), and crawfish. Feral hogs are widespread in North Texas and are a genuine trichinosis and other-parasite risk — **cook hog meat to at least 160–165°F throughout with no pink anywhere**, and consider it higher-risk than deer. **Deer — Chronic Wasting Disease (CWD):** never handle, field dress, or eat a deer that appears visibly sick (stumbling, drooling, extreme thinness, no fear of humans), and never eat brain or spinal cord tissue from any deer; Texas Parks & Wildlife (TPWD) offers free/low-cost CWD testing in many areas — use it if you have any doubt about a harvested deer. Creeks and stock tanks across the region hold catfish and sunfish that respond well to simple hand lines and trotlines baited with worms or stinkbait-style scraps; crawfish are abundant in slow creek sections almost statewide. Summer heat means processed meat spoils very fast — cool and cook or preserve within a couple of hours, and prefer smoking/drying over holding raw meat any length of time.
- **Cold climates:** meat left outside in freezing temperatures can be safely stored raw far longer than in heat; still cook thoroughly before eating.
- **Coastal/tidal areas:** tidal fish traps and weirs work on the same funnel principle as stream weirs, built to hold fish as the tide recedes.
- **Desert/arid regions:** water sources concentrate all game activity — set snares and lines near any available water rather than randomly across open ground.
- **Legal/ethical note:** the methods in this guide (unlicensed hunting, trapping, and fishing without gear or permits, taking game out of season) are intended strictly for a genuine survival emergency where normal food supply and legal access to food have broken down. In normal times, hunting and fishing seasons, licensing, and species/method restrictions apply and exist for good conservation reasons — follow your state and local game laws.

## ⚠️ Safety — warnings & mistakes to avoid

- **Never eat undercooked wild game or fish.** Parasites (trichinosis in hogs, tapeworms, flukes) and bacteria are common in wild animals and are killed reliably only by thorough cooking, not by rare/medium cooking. **Use a meat thermometer as your primary doneness check — color and clear juices are not reliable indicators of safety.**
- **Feral hogs are a specific trichinosis risk** — cook hog meat to at least 160–165°F throughout with no pink anywhere.
- **Rabbits and hares carry tularemia ("rabbit fever")** — wear gloves when skinning, inspect the liver/spleen for white spots or lesions (discard the whole animal if present), and cook rabbit meat well-done.
- **Deer can carry Chronic Wasting Disease (CWD)** — never handle or eat a visibly sick deer or its brain/spinal tissue; TPWD offers CWD testing for harvested deer in Texas.
- **Discard any animal with abnormal organs**: cysts, spots, discoloration, swelling, foul smell, or visible worms in the liver/gut mean the whole animal should be discarded, not just the organ.
- **Never set a deadfall trap where the falling weight could land on a hand, foot, or person** — test triggers with a stick, never your fingers.
- **Do not leave traps/lines unchecked for more than about 12 hours** — animals suffer needlessly, catches spoil, and you lose food to scavengers/decomposition.
- **Avoid handling sick-looking or unusually tame wild animals** — unusual behavior can indicate rabies or other disease; do not approach or handle.
- **Wash hands and clean tools after field dressing** before touching food, your face, or wounds — organ contents and blood carry bacteria.
- **Do not eat freshwater mussels or crawfish that are already dead/open before cooking**, and discard any that fail to open during cooking.
- **Be extremely cautious with wire snares near trails used by pets, livestock, or people** — mark or avoid areas where non-target animals or humans could be caught.
- **Never taste-test raw meat or blood to check freshness.**
- **Do not overextend energy on active hunting when calorie reserves are already low** — prioritize passive trapping/fishing, which costs almost nothing to maintain.

## Troubleshooting

- **Snares keep getting sprung with nothing caught:** loop may be too large/small, or set at the wrong height — re-measure to the target species' head height, and check that the loop isn't being brushed by wind-blown vegetation.
- **Deadfall trigger won't hold or falls prematurely:** the three-stick notches need cleaner, flatter contact surfaces; shave the notches so they seat together more precisely, and confirm the ground under the upright stick is firm.
- **Deadfall trigger too stiff, animal doesn't trip it:** shave the trigger stick thinner or file the notches shallower so less force is needed to collapse it.
- **No bites on the fishing line:** move to a new hole (undercut banks, log piles, drop-offs), change bait, try early morning/dusk when fish feed more actively, and check the line isn't tangled or the hook fouled with debris.
- **Trotline catches keep getting stolen or half-eaten:** you are checking too infrequently — increase to checking every few hours if predation/scavenging is heavy, or move the line to less accessible water.
- **Catch spoiling before you can eat it:** you are not field dressing and cooling fast enough — gut immediately, hang meat in shade with airflow, and begin smoking/drying (food-preservation-cooking.md) rather than holding raw meat.
- **Can't find game trails at all:** look near water sources at dawn/dusk, along field/forest edges, and any narrow gaps in dense brush — animals almost always take the path of least resistance.
- **Improvised hook keeps bending straight or breaking:** use a harder wood/bone, or a smaller, less aggressive barb angle; wire hooks need a tighter bend radius to hold shape under a fish's pull.

## Quick-reference checklist

- [ ] Scout for game trails, tracks, and fish-holding water before building traps.
- [ ] Set multiple snares (6-12) and/or a multi-hook trotline the first day — passive methods first.
- [ ] Place snares at head-height in narrow, forced-path pinch points on active trails.
- [ ] Build figure-4 deadfall(s) on flat, clear ground; test trigger with a stick, never a hand.
- [ ] Rig hand lines/gorge hooks and fish where structure/current suggests fish are holding.
- [ ] Check every trap and line at least twice a day, morning and evening.
- [ ] Field dress every catch immediately; keep meat clean, cool, and off the ground.
- [ ] Discard any animal with abnormal organs, cysts, or foul odor.
- [ ] Cook all meat and fish thoroughly — no pink, especially in feral hog meat.
- [ ] Preserve (smoke/dry) any surplus meat promptly rather than holding it raw.
- [ ] Reserve active hunting for opportunistic small game; don't overspend energy on it.
- [ ] Know and, in normal times, follow your local hunting/fishing/trapping laws.
