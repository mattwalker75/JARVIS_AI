# Personal & Home Security During a Crisis

## Read this first (the 30-second version)
1. **Avoidance beats every other tactic.** The best "fight" is the one you never have. Situational awareness — noticing trouble early and steering away from it — prevents more harm than any lock, alarm, or confrontation skill ever will.
2. **Don't advertise what you have.** In a crisis, visible supplies, running generators, cooking smells, and lit windows tell everyone nearby "there's something worth taking here." Blend in (be a "gray man"), not out.
3. **Security is layers, not a single wall.** Perimeter awareness → yard/approach → doors → windows → an interior safe room. Each layer buys you time and information; none of them needs to be perfect alone.
4. **Have a lockdown plan and a safe room decided BEFORE you need them.** In an emergency you will not think clearly — you need a rehearsed plan, not a decision.
5. **If a confrontation happens anyway: de-escalate, create distance, retreat to your safe room or leave, and call for help.** This guide is about protection, not aggression. Fighting is the last resort after every other option is gone.
6. **There is safety in numbers.** A few trusted neighbors sharing watch shifts and a communication plan will out-perform any one household trying to do it all alone.
7. **Know your local self-defense and use-of-force laws now, while things are calm** — they still apply during a disaster in the United States and most countries. This guide does not teach or endorse offensive tactics or weapon-making.

The rest of this guide walks through awareness, operational security (OPSEC), physically hardening a home in layers, building a lockdown/safe-room plan, organizing neighborhood cooperation, and handling a confrontation safely if one occurs.

---

## Why this matters
During and after a disaster — hurricane, earthquake, extended blackout, civil disturbance, or economic shock — normal social controls weaken for a time: police response is slower, streetlights and neighbors' security cameras may be dark, and desperate or opportunistic people may target homes that look occupied, well-stocked, or undefended. This is not about assuming the worst in people; most neighbors will be as scared and cooperative as you are, and most disasters never produce a security incident at all. But basic preparation costs little and pays off enormously if the rare bad situation does occur.

Good home security in a crisis is overwhelmingly about **reducing your target profile and buying time**, not about confrontation:
- **Avoidance and awareness** stop most problems before they start — this is the single highest-value skill in this entire guide.
- **Operational security** (not advertising supplies, light, noise, or routine) reduces the chance your home is ever selected as a target in the first place.
- **Layered hardening** slows down or discourages an intruder long enough for them to give up, for you to reach a safe room, or for help to arrive.
- **A rehearsed lockdown plan** means your household reacts correctly under stress instead of freezing or panicking.
- **Community cooperation** multiplies your eyes, ears, and response capability far beyond what one household can do alone.
- **De-escalation and retreat** protect you, your family, and the other person — violence carries legal, physical, and psychological risks that vastly outweigh the value of "standing your ground" over property.

Protecting your household's safety and calm during a frightening time is also a form of psychological first aid — see `../psychological-first-aid/psychological-first-aid.md` for supporting children and adults through fear and stress.

## Key terms (plain language)
- **Situational awareness** — actively noticing your surroundings, the people in them, and anything unusual, instead of moving through the world on autopilot ("head down in a phone").
- **Gray man / gray rock** — the practice of blending in and appearing unremarkable, so you don't stand out as someone worth targeting, watching, or robbing. The opposite of the "gray man" is someone who is loud, visibly armed, visibly stocked with supplies, or otherwise draws attention.
- **OPSEC (operational security)** — protecting information about what you have, what your routines are, and what your capabilities are, so that information can't be used against you. In a home-security context this mostly means noise, light, and supply discipline.
- **Hardening** — physically strengthening a structure's weak points (doors, windows, locks) so that forcing entry takes longer, makes more noise, or becomes impractical.
- **Layers of defense (defense in depth)** — the idea that no single barrier needs to stop everything; a series of barriers (perimeter, yard, doors, windows, interior room) each slow down or discourage a threat and buy time.
- **Safe room / hardened room** — a single, pre-designated interior room (or closet) reinforced and stocked so a household can lock down, communicate, and wait for a threat to leave or for help to arrive.
- **Lockdown plan** — a rehearsed, written plan for what every household member does the moment a threat is identified (where to go, who calls whom, what to bring).
- **De-escalation** — verbal and behavioral techniques (calm voice, open hands, distance, giving an "out") used to lower the tension of a confrontation so it does not turn violent.
- **Force continuum / proportionality** — "force continuum" is a term and framework from law-enforcement training (a step-by-step escalation model officers are taught), not a civilian legal standard you'll find written into most self-defense statutes; the general legal/ethical principle for a civilian is simpler: any response to a threat should be the minimum necessary to stay safe, and in most states "standing your ground" over property alone is treated very differently by law than protecting your life (a minority of states carve out narrower exceptions — see the Safety section).
- **Improvised alarm** — a simple, non-electronic early-warning device (cans on a string, gravel path, tripwire bell) that makes noise when someone approaches or enters, giving you advance warning.
- **Neighborhood watch** — an informal or organized group of neighbors who agree to watch out for each other's homes and streets and share information, often with shared watch shifts during a prolonged emergency.
- **Target hardening vs. target denial** — hardening makes entry harder; denial (via OPSEC — not looking worth targeting at all) avoids the attempt altogether. Denial is cheaper and usually more effective.

## What you need

### Awareness & OPSEC (cost: mostly free — habits and discipline)
- A habit of scanning your surroundings (see "Awareness" section below) — no equipment required
- Blackout curtains, dark bedsheets, or cardboard+foil for window light discipline
- A battery- or hand-crank radio for information without broadcasting your own signal (see `../communication/communication.md`)
- Quiet-running behaviors: generators muffled/distanced, cooking scents minimized or masked, trash/food waste managed so it doesn't advertise activity

### Hardening supplies (cost: low-to-moderate, mostly hardware-store items)
- Longer screws (3–3.5 inch) for door strike plates and hinges
- A door reinforcement bar, drop bar, or heavy-duty deadbolt with a 1-inch (or longer) throw bolt
- Door and window security bars/pins, or a simple dowel/broom-handle cut to fit a sliding door or window track
- Motion-sensor lights (solar or battery powered work without grid power)
- Materials for improvised alarms: empty cans, fishing line/string, gravel or dry leaves, small bells, a whistle
- A physical (non-electronic) whistle or air horn for each household member, for signaling and for scaring off an intruder with noise
- A basic first-aid kit staged in the safe room (see `../first-aid/first-aid.md`)

### Safe room / lockdown supplies (staged in advance, inside the designated room)
- A solid or reinforced door with a functioning interior lock
- A charged phone or radio, plus a backup battery/power bank
- A flashlight (avoid ones that only work plugged in)
- Water, snacks, any critical medications
- A whistle and a written list of emergency contacts (in case phones are dead — see `../communication/communication.md`)
- Comfort items for children (see "Protecting children and elderly household members" below)

### For community/neighborhood cooperation
- A paper or written contact list of trusted neighbors (phone lines and cell networks can fail — do not rely on memory or phones alone)
- An agreed simple signal system (e.g., porch light pattern, a specific flag, a knock code) for "I'm okay" / "I need help" without needing power or phones
- A shared watch-shift schedule if your group organizes one

## Threat assessment & situational awareness

Awareness is the foundation everything else in this guide is built on. It is free, it works in every environment, and it is the number one reason most people never need any of the hardening or confrontation advice below.

1. **Keep your head up and your senses free.** Avoid both earbuds in, phone-down walking, especially at night or in an unfamiliar area. You cannot avoid a threat you never notice.
2. **Learn your baseline, then watch for what's different.** Every neighborhood, street, or store has a normal rhythm — who's usually around, what's usually parked where, how loud it usually is. A change from that baseline (an unfamiliar car circling the block, someone loitering without a clear reason, a normally busy street suddenly empty) is worth noticing, not panicking over.
3. **Trust the feeling before you can explain it.** Humans are very good at picking up on subtle danger cues before consciously identifying them. If a place or person makes you uneasy, treat that as real information and act on it (cross the street, go back inside, leave) even if you can't immediately say why.
4. **Watch hands and exits, not just faces.** In any encounter, briefly note where the nearest exits are and what the other person's hands are doing. This is a habit, not paranoia.
5. **Plan your routes with security in mind during a crisis.** Prefer routes that are populated, well-lit if possible, and have multiple ways out over shortcuts through isolated areas.
6. **Trust your instincts about a location, not just a person.** If an area feels wrong (too quiet, signs of recent trouble, unclear sightlines), it is fine to change your plan and avoid it entirely.
7. **De-prioritize confrontation over "principle."** If a situation is escalating over something replaceable (a parking spot, a queue position, supplies), disengage. Avoidance is a win, not a loss.

## Operational security (OPSEC): don't become the target

Most looting, break-ins, or opportunistic crime during a disaster is a crime of **opportunity and visibility** — it targets homes that look occupied-but-unaware, obviously well-stocked, or undefended, rather than homes chosen at random. Reducing your visible profile reduces your odds of being chosen at all.

1. **Practice "gray man" behavior.** Dress and act unremarkably; avoid clothing, gear, or conversation that signals you are heavily stocked, armed, or otherwise a high-value target. The goal is to be forgettable, not impressive.
2. **Control light discipline after dark.** Use blackout curtains, dark sheets, or cardboard-and-foil taped over windows so interior light (flashlights, candles, screens) isn't visible from outside. A single lit window in an otherwise dark neighborhood stands out sharply.
3. **Control noise discipline.** Generators are loud and audible for a long distance — run them during accepted daytime hours if possible, place them away from the street side of the home, and consider a sound-dampening enclosure (with proper ventilation — see carbon monoxide warning below). Keep voices, music, and radios low, especially at night.
4. **Control scent discipline.** Cooking smells travel and signal "food and people here." Cook during busier daytime hours when many households are also cooking, vent smoke away from the street, and avoid cooking foods with very strong smells if operating in a lower-profile mode.
5. **Don't discuss your supplies or plans publicly.** Avoid posting on social media, or discussing loudly in public, what you have stockpiled, what security measures you've installed, or when your home will be empty.
6. **Keep supplies out of sight from outside.** Store visible-from-window stockpiles, generators, water containers, and fuel out of direct view from the street or sidewalk.
7. **Vary and downplay your routine when possible.** Predictable patterns (always leaving at the same time, always the same porch light behavior) are easier to plan around than irregular ones.
8. **Make the home look occupied even if it sometimes isn't.** Timers on a light, a car in the driveway, or a neighbor watching the property can all help maintain the appearance of occupancy.

## Hardening the home in layers

Think of your home's security as concentric rings. No single ring has to be impenetrable — each one is meant to slow an intruder down, make noise, or make the effort clearly not worth it, while giving your household more warning and more time.

```
        OUTER PERIMETER (property line, street-facing awareness)
      ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
      │   YARD / APPROACH (lighting, sightlines, alarms) │
      │   ┌───────────────────────────────────────────┐  │
      │   │   DOORS (locks, strike plates, bars)       │  │
      │   │   ┌─────────────────────────────────────┐  │  │
      │   │   │   WINDOWS (locks, bars, coverings)   │  │  │
      │   │   │   ┌───────────────────────────────┐  │  │  │
      │   │   │   │  SAFE ROOM (final fallback)    │  │  │  │
      │   │   │   │  people + comms + supplies     │  │  │  │
      │   │   │   └───────────────────────────────┘  │  │  │
      │   │   └─────────────────────────────────────┘  │  │
      │   └───────────────────────────────────────────┘  │
      └─────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
Figure: "Layers of home defense." Each ring slows or discourages an
intruder and buys time/warning for the ring inside it. The safe room
is the last layer, not the first response.
```

### Step 1: Perimeter & yard
1. **Improve sightlines.** Trim bushes and hedges below window height near entry points so an intruder has no place to hide while working on a door or window, and so you (or a neighbor) can see the approach.
2. **Add motion-sensor lighting** at likely approach points (driveway, side gates, back door). Solar or battery-powered units work without grid power and are simple to install.
3. **Build improvised early-warning alarms** at gates, fence gaps, and low-traffic approach points:
   - Tie a string across a path at ankle/shin height with empty cans threaded on it — movement rattles the cans.
   - Spread a layer of gravel or dry leaves/twigs on likely approach paths — footsteps become audible.
   - Hang a small bell on gates or the string tripwire.
   These require no power, are cheap, and give you audible warning time.
4. **Secure gates and outbuildings** with working locks; don't leave ladders, tools, or anything that could be used to reach a window or roof accessible in the yard.
5. **Keep vehicles locked and consider parking to block a driveway approach** if vehicle theft or ramming is a local concern.

### Step 2: Doors
1. **Upgrade to a deadbolt with at least a 1-inch throw bolt** on every exterior door.
2. **Replace short strike-plate screws with 3–3.5 inch screws** that reach into the wall framing, not just the door jamb trim. This single $5 fix dramatically increases resistance to kick-ins.
3. **Install a door reinforcement bar or drop bar** across the interior of primary entry doors for a lockdown situation.
4. **Check hinges** — if hinges are on the outside of an exterior door, add non-removable hinge pins or hinge-bolt hardware so the door can't be lifted out even if hinges are exposed.
5. **Secure sliding glass and patio doors** with a cut-to-fit dowel or metal bar laid in the track, plus an auxiliary pin lock through the frame.
6. **Add a peephole or door viewer** if one is not already present, so you can identify who is at the door without opening it.
7. **Reinforce the door itself** if it's hollow-core — a solid-core or metal door is far more resistant to force than a hollow interior door often used for exterior openings in older construction. **Quick knock-test:** knock on the door's face — a light, hollow, tinny sound means hollow-core (a thin wood or hardboard skin over a cardboard/honeycomb interior); a dull, solid thud means solid-core or metal. If an exterior door tests hollow, prioritize replacing it or adding a solid-core door for the safe room instead (Step 4).

### Step 3: Windows
1. **Ensure every window lock functions** and is engaged, including secondary pin locks on double-hung windows (a nail or pin through a drilled hole in the frame, angled slightly downward, stops the window from being lifted).
2. **Use security film or added glazing** on ground-floor windows where affordable — it doesn't stop breakage but resists shattering-through entry and slows an intruder down.
3. **Add security bars or grilles** on vulnerable ground-floor windows, ensuring at least one point of quick-release from inside for fire escape — never install bars that trap occupants inside.
4. **Use blackout curtains or coverings** on all windows visible from outside (also serves OPSEC light discipline above).
5. **Consider basement and garage windows,** which are frequently overlooked but are common entry points; secure and cover them the same as ground-floor windows.

### Step 4: Interior safe room
1. **Choose the room in advance**, ideally: an interior room (fewer exterior walls/windows), upstairs if the house has multiple floors, with a solid door and working interior lock. A primary bedroom closet, an interior bathroom, or a similar small interior room often works well.
2. **Reinforce the door** the same way as exterior doors (longer strike-plate screws, solid-core door if possible, a simple slide bolt or bar on the inside).
3. **Stage supplies inside permanently:** phone/radio + backup battery, flashlight, water, snacks, medications, a whistle, a written contact list, and comfort items for children (see below). Rotate consumables like a small emergency kit.
4. **Practice reaching it.** Every household member — including children old enough to understand — should know the fastest route to the safe room from every part of the house, in the dark if needed.
5. **Plan communication from inside it.** Decide in advance who gets called first (a trusted neighbor, then emergency services if reachable) and what you'll say. See `../communication/communication.md` for options when phone networks are down.

## Building a lockdown plan

A lockdown plan is a short, written, rehearsed set of steps everyone in the household knows by heart, so a frightening moment doesn't become a chaotic one.

1. **Decide the trigger.** Agree in advance on what situations activate the plan (e.g., someone attempting to force a door, a credible threat identified nearby, a call from a neighbor).
2. **Assign roles.** Decide who gathers children/elderly members, who locks which doors, who grabs the phone/radio, and who leads the way to the safe room. Roles prevent everyone from doing the same thing (or nothing) under stress.
3. **Move to the safe room and lock the door.** Lights off if it's dark outside (don't silhouette yourselves), stay low and away from windows if any are in the room.
4. **Communicate.** Call or radio a trusted neighbor and, if reachable, emergency services. Keep your voice calm and quiet; state your address, the nature of the threat, and that you are locked down.
5. **Stay put until the threat has passed or help arrives.** Do not leave the safe room to investigate noises — that defeats its purpose.
6. **Debrief afterward.** Once safe, talk through what happened as a household, especially with children (see `../psychological-first-aid/psychological-first-aid.md`), and note anything that should improve the plan.
7. **Rehearse the plan when things are calm**, the same way you'd rehearse a fire escape plan — a two-minute walk-through every few months means the plan is second nature if it's ever needed for real.

## Community security: there is safety in numbers

Cooperation with trusted neighbors consistently outperforms solo security efforts, because a group has more eyes, more sleep rotation, and more combined resources.

1. **Identify a small circle of trusted neighbors before a crisis**, not during one. Trust built in calm times is far more reliable than trust formed under stress with strangers.
2. **Agree on a simple communication/signal system** that doesn't depend on phones: a specific porch-light pattern, a flag or sign in a window, or an agreed knock code to mean "we're okay" or "we need help."
3. **Organize shared watch shifts** during a prolonged emergency so no single household has to stay awake and alert around the clock. Even two or three households rotating two-hour shifts dramatically improves collective alertness.
4. **Share information, not just labor.** Pass along what you've observed (unfamiliar vehicles, suspicious activity, useful information from radio broadcasts) to the group promptly.
5. **Pool resources sensibly without broadcasting them.** Community stockpile-sharing arrangements should still be discussed privately, not in ways that advertise total group supplies to outsiders.
6. **Support vulnerable neighbors.** Elderly neighbors living alone, single parents, and disabled neighbors benefit enormously from being included in a watch/communication network — and their inclusion also means more people know your household is there to help.
7. **Set clear, calm expectations for handling disputes within the group** (e.g., over resource sharing) so the community security effort itself doesn't become a source of conflict.

## Dealing with a confrontation: de-escalate, retreat, call for help

If awareness, OPSEC, and hardening fail and you do end up face-to-face with a threatening person, the priority order is always: **de-escalate → create distance/retreat → call for help.** Physical confrontation is the last resort after every other option has been exhausted, and this guide does not teach or endorse offensive tactics.

1. **Stay calm and keep your voice low and steady.** A calm, non-threatening tone reduces the chance the other person feels cornered or challenged, which lowers the odds of escalation.
2. **Keep your hands visible and open.** This signals you are not a threat and helps prevent the other person from perceiving aggression where none is intended.
3. **Give the other person a clear way out.** Don't block an exit or corner someone, even unintentionally — a person who feels trapped is far more likely to become violent.
4. **Create physical distance.** Step back, move behind cover (a door, a car, a wall), and increase the space between you and the threat whenever possible.
5. **Retreat to your safe room or leave the area entirely** rather than staying to defend property. Possessions are replaceable; people are not.
6. **Make noise and call for help.** A whistle, air horn, or shouting can startle an intruder and alert neighbors; call emergency services or your neighbor network as soon as you safely can.
7. **Never pursue a fleeing intruder.** Once a threat is leaving, let them go — pursuing turns a resolved situation back into an active, dangerous one and can also create serious legal exposure.
8. **Know that self-defense laws still apply during a disaster.** In the United States (and most jurisdictions), use-of-force law does not suspend during an emergency; using force must still be proportionate to the threat and in most states is justified only to protect life, not property (a minority of states, e.g. Texas Penal Code §9.41–9.42, permit force — including in narrow cases deadly force — to protect property, but only under specific, limited conditions such as at night or when other means would expose you to substantial risk). Learn your local laws now, while calm, rather than trying to figure them out during a crisis.

## Protecting children and elderly household members

1. **Teach children the safe-room location and the calm version of the plan** ("if I say the code word, we walk — not run — to the safe room together") without frightening them with worst-case detail.
2. **Give children a simple job** in the lockdown plan (carrying a specific comfort item, holding a sibling's hand) — having a task reduces panic.
3. **Stage comfort items in the safe room**: a favorite small toy, a book, something familiar that helps a child stay calm during a long wait.
4. **Plan for mobility and medical needs of elderly or disabled household members** in advance — make sure the safe room is reachable for them, and that critical medications live there or can be grabbed in seconds.
5. **Assign a specific person to elderly/child care during a lockdown** so that responsibility isn't ambiguous in the moment.
6. **Practice the plan gently** with children the way you'd practice a fire drill — matter-of-fact, not frightening, done occasionally so it stays familiar without becoming a source of ongoing anxiety.
7. **After any real incident, prioritize emotional debriefing** for children and vulnerable adults — see `../psychological-first-aid/psychological-first-aid.md` for age-appropriate ways to talk through a frightening event.

## Regional & scenario variants

**Urban / apartment:**
- Your "perimeter" may only be a hallway and a front door — focus hardening effort there (strike plate, peephole, secondary lock) since yard-layer defenses often don't apply.
- Coordinate with building neighbors and, where one exists, building security/management for a shared watch approach.
- Noise/light discipline matters even more in close quarters — sound and smell travel through shared walls and hallways.
- Identify an interior room away from the hallway-facing wall as your safe room if a true windowless room isn't available.

**Suburban:**
- Yard-layer defenses (motion lights, sightline trimming, improvised alarms at gates) are usually the highest-value addition, since suburban lots typically have more approach routes than urban units.
- Neighborhood watch cooperation tends to be easiest to organize here — most suburban streets already have some existing social network to build on.

**Rural:**
- Longer response times from emergency services make community cooperation and self-sufficiency even more important — coordinate with the nearest neighbors even if they're a distance away.
- Perimeter awareness covers more ground (driveways, tree lines, outbuildings); motion lighting and gravel/noise-alarm approaches at the driveway entrance are especially effective early warning.
- Isolated properties may need a longer-duration safe-room supply cache since help may be much further away.

**Extended/no-power scenario:**
- Rely on solar/battery motion lights and mechanical (non-electronic) alarms, since powered alarm systems and cameras may be offline.
- OPSEC (light/noise/scent discipline) becomes more important, not less, since fewer working streetlights and more darkness make a lit or noisy home stand out more sharply.

**Civil unrest / widespread disorder scenario:**
- Gray-man OPSEC and avoidance of crowds/hotspots matter more than ever — do not go out to observe or "protect" property outside the home.
- Community coordination (shared watch shifts, agreed signals) is especially valuable when police response is delayed across a wide area.

See also `../disaster-specific/disaster-specific.md` for security considerations tied to specific disaster types (hurricane evacuation/return, earthquake aftermath, etc.).

## ⚠️ Safety & legal/ethical notes
- This guide is intentionally **defensive and de-escalation-focused**. It does not cover weapon-manufacturing, offensive tactics, or how to use force against another person — those topics are out of scope and are not appropriate for a general preparedness reference.
- **Self-defense and use-of-force laws apply during a disaster the same as in normal times** in the United States and most countries. Force must generally be proportionate to the threat and in most states is justified only to protect life, not property — though a minority of states permit force to protect property in narrow, specific circumstances (e.g., Texas Penal Code §9.41–9.42), so this is not an absolute rule everywhere. Learn your specific local and state/provincial laws now, while calm.
- **Never install window bars or security devices that trap occupants inside** without a fast interior release — fire escape must always remain possible.
- **Generators and any combustion engine must run outdoors, away from windows and doors, and never in a garage or enclosed space** — carbon monoxide is colorless, odorless, and can kill quickly in enclosed or poorly ventilated spaces. See `../shelter/shelter.md`.
- **De-escalation and retreat are not weakness** — they are the option with the best safety outcome in the overwhelming majority of confrontations, and they carry far less legal and psychological risk than physical force.
- **Avoid vigilantism.** Patrolling to confront strangers, detaining people, or "standing guard" aggressively outside your own property creates legal liability and increases danger rather than reducing it. Community security means shared awareness and communication, not armed patrols.
- **Protect vulnerable people first.** Any security plan should explicitly account for children, elderly, disabled, or medically dependent household members — their safety needs shape where the safe room is and how the lockdown plan runs.
- **False alarms and misidentification are real risks.** Improvised alarms and heightened alertness can lead to mistaking a neighbor, delivery, or family member for a threat — always verify (peephole, calling out, agreed signal) before reacting.
- **Mental health matters.** Prolonged high alert is exhausting and can distort judgment; rotate watch shifts, rest, and lean on `../psychological-first-aid/psychological-first-aid.md` for the household's emotional wellbeing.

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Fix |
|---|---|
| Door still feels kickable after adding a deadbolt | Check strike-plate screw length (need 3+ inches into framing) and confirm hinges aren't exposed/liftable from outside; add hinge-bolt hardware if they are |
| Motion lights keep false-triggering | Aim sensor away from tree branches/street traffic, reduce sensitivity, or narrow the detection zone |
| Improvised can-alarm keeps getting triggered by wind/animals | Move the tripwire lower/tighter to the ground, use fewer but heavier cans, or relocate away from a wind-exposed or animal-traveled path |
| Household freezes or argues during a lockdown drill | Simplify the plan (fewer steps, clearer single leader role) and re-practice more often, in short low-pressure sessions |
| Can't agree on a safe-room location | Prioritize fewest exterior walls/windows and a working interior lock over size or comfort; a closet beats a large room with big windows |
| Neighbors are reluctant to organize a watch group | Start small (one or two trusted households), focus first on information-sharing rather than formal patrols, and let it grow organically |
| Confrontation is escalating despite calm tone | Increase physical distance immediately, move toward an exit or cover, and call/signal for help rather than continuing to engage verbally |
| Cooking smells or generator noise still noticeable despite precautions | Shift timing to align with when neighbors are also active/cooking, increase physical distance from the street side, and check for gaps in noise/scent containment (vents, open windows) |
| Children panic during actual lockdown despite drills | Keep your own voice and body language calm above all else — children take their emotional cue from the calmest adult present; use the comfort item staged in the safe room |
| Unsure if local self-defense laws allow a specific action | Do not guess under pressure — research your jurisdiction's laws now, in calm conditions, and default to the most conservative, life-protecting, retreat-first interpretation if unsure |

## Quick-reference checklist

**Awareness & OPSEC (ongoing, free):**
- [ ] Household practices "gray man" behavior — no visible flaunting of supplies, security measures, or routines
- [ ] Blackout curtains or coverings on all street-visible windows
- [ ] Generator/cooking noise and scent minimized and timed sensibly
- [ ] No public discussion (in person or online) of supplies or security plans

**Hardening (set up once, check periodically):**
- [ ] All exterior doors have deadbolts with 1"+ throw and 3"+ strike-plate screws
- [ ] Door reinforcement bar available for primary entries
- [ ] All windows lock and have secondary pin locks; ground-floor windows covered/reinforced with a fire-safe release
- [ ] Motion lighting and at least one improvised alarm at key approach points
- [ ] Sightlines clear (trimmed hedges) near entry points

**Safe room & lockdown plan (set up once, rehearse regularly):**
- [ ] Safe room chosen and door reinforced
- [ ] Phone/radio, flashlight, water, snacks, medications, whistle, and contact list staged inside
- [ ] Every household member knows the route and their role
- [ ] Plan rehearsed at least twice a year, including with children

**Community:**
- [ ] Trusted neighbor circle identified and a non-phone signal system agreed
- [ ] Watch-shift rotation plan exists for a prolonged emergency
- [ ] Vulnerable neighbors included in the information/communication loop

**If a confrontation occurs:**
- [ ] De-escalate first (calm voice, open hands, give an exit)
- [ ] Create distance / retreat toward safe room or away from the area
- [ ] Call or signal for help; never pursue a retreating threat
