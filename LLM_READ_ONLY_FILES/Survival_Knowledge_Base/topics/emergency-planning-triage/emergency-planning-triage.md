# Emergency Planning, Triage & Decision-Making

## Read this first (the 30-second version)
1. **Do the 5-minute situation check first** (below) — is anyone hurt, is there immediate danger (fire, gas, water, structural, armed threat), and is the danger getting worse right now? Fix or flee the immediate threat before anything else.
2. **Think in three time windows** — the next **3 hours** (survive right now), the next **3 days** (stabilize), the next **3 weeks** (sustain). Don't let a 3-week worry (like food) distract you from a 3-hour threat (like a gas leak).
3. **Decide shelter-in-place vs. evacuate early, using pre-set triggers**, not in the moment — panic decisions are bad decisions. If the danger is inside/at your location (fire, structural collapse, gas) or approaching fast with an official evacuation order, **go**. If the danger is outside and your building is sound (most severe weather, most utility outages), **stay put**.
4. **Everyone in the family has a job and knows the plan** — assigned roles, two rally points (near + far), one out-of-town contact, and a written copy of it all, before the emergency, not during.
5. **If the event drags on, run a short daily briefing** — same time, same questions, every day — so the family stays coordinated instead of drifting.

The rest of this guide walks through each of these in detail with checklists you can fill in and hang on the fridge. **This guide covers the thinking and the decisions — for the physical mechanics of leaving, packing, and driving see the [Evacuation & Bug-Out guide](../evacuation-bugout-vehicle/evacuation-bugout-vehicle.md); for what supplies to have on hand see [Supplies, Inventory & Rotation](../supplies-inventory-rotation/supplies-inventory-rotation.md); for tracking consumption and rationing once you're in it see [Survival Logistics & Resources](../survival-logistics-resources/survival-logistics-resources.md); for kids' drills and reunification see [Children & Family Operations](../children-family-operations/children-family-operations.md); for what to actually do in a specific disaster (tornado, flood, ice storm, etc.) see [Disaster-Specific](../disaster-specific/disaster-specific.md); and for keeping your head straight under stress see [Psychological First Aid](../psychological-first-aid/psychological-first-aid.md).**

## Why this matters
Most people don't die in disasters because they lacked supplies — they die or get hurt because they made a bad decision under pressure, or made no decision and froze, or made the right decision too late. A plan made calmly, in advance, on paper, turns a crisis into a checklist. It removes arguments ("should we go or stay?") at the worst possible moment, keeps a family from scattering and losing each other, and gives you a way to re-check your decisions as a situation evolves instead of locking onto the first idea you had. Planning is cheap. Panic is expensive.

## Key terms (plain language)
- **Situation assessment** — a fast, structured look at what's actually happening before you act, so you respond to reality instead of assumption or fear.
- **Triage** — sorting problems (or people) by urgency so you address the most life-threatening ones first. Borrowed from medicine, applied here to *decisions*, not just injuries.
- **Rule of threes** — a survival memory aid: roughly 3 minutes without air, 3 hours without shelter in harsh conditions, 3 days without water, 3 weeks without food. Used here as a **planning horizon**, not just a physiology fact: plan your next 3 hours, then your next 3 days, then your next 3 weeks.
- **Shelter-in-place** — staying at your current location (home or another building) because it is safer than moving.
- **Evacuate** — leaving your current location because staying is more dangerous than moving.
- **Decision trigger** — a pre-agreed, specific condition that automatically means a specific action ("if water reaches the third porch step, we leave") — decided calmly in advance so you don't have to debate it in the moment.
- **Rally point** — an agreed meeting place. A **near** rally point is just outside the home (e.g., the mailbox) for a sudden event like a fire. A **far** rally point is outside the neighborhood, for when the home or block itself is unreachable or unsafe.
- **Out-of-area contact** — a relative or friend in a different city/region who everyone calls or texts to report status; useful because long-distance calls/texts often get through when local circuits are jammed.
- **Family Communication Plan** — the written document listing contacts, rally points, roles, and key account/medical info (FEMA and Red Cross both provide fillable templates; this guide gives you the content to put in one).
- **Situation briefing** — a short, repeated check-in (daily, or more often) during a prolonged event where the family reviews status, threats, and the plan for the next period.
- **Go bag / bug-out bag** — a pre-packed bag of essentials for evacuation; covered in depth in the [Evacuation guide](../evacuation-bugout-vehicle/evacuation-bugout-vehicle.md).

# PART 1 — THE 5-MINUTE SITUATION ASSESSMENT

When something goes wrong — a storm hits, the power dies, an alert sounds, you smell smoke — your first job is not to solve the problem. It's to **understand** it. Do this in order, out loud if you're with others. It should take under 5 minutes.

## Step 1 — Life threats, right now (60 seconds)
Ask, in this order:
1. **Is anyone hurt or missing?** Look around, call names, do a headcount. An unaccounted-for person becomes the top priority immediately.
2. **Is there an active, worsening danger at THIS location?** — fire, smoke, the smell of gas, water rising inside the structure, visible structural damage (cracking, sagging, leaning), downed power lines nearby, an armed or violent threat, a chemical smell.
3. **Is anyone in immediate medical danger** — severe bleeding, not breathing, unresponsive? If yes, that overrides everything else in this guide — go straight to the [First Aid guide](../first-aid/first-aid.md) and start care/call for help now.

> ⚠️ If you find an active fire, a gas smell, or structural collapse risk, **get everyone out first and assess from outside.** Do not stop to gather belongings. See [Fire, Smoke & Air Emergencies](../fire-smoke-air-emergencies/fire-smoke-air-emergencies.md) and [Home & Structural Assessment](../home-structural-assessment/home-structural-assessment.md).

## Step 2 — What kind of event is this? (60 seconds)
Identify the category — it changes everything downstream:
- **Sudden and localized** (house fire, gas leak, vehicle crash, medical emergency) — usually short, needs immediate action, often resolves in hours.
- **Sudden and area-wide** (tornado, earthquake, explosion, active-shooter/security event) — immediate danger, but the *aftermath* may last days.
- **Slow-building and area-wide** (hurricane, winter storm, flood watch, wildfire approach, extended power grid failure) — you likely have hours to days of warning; use that time.
- **Ongoing/no clear end** (pandemic, civil unrest, prolonged utility failure, economic disruption) — plan in weeks, not hours; this is where the daily briefing routine (Part 7) matters most.

## Step 3 — What resources do you have RIGHT NOW? (90 seconds)
- Who is present (adults, kids, elderly, pets, anyone with a disability or medical need)?
- What's working: power, water, phone signal/internet, natural gas, vehicle(s) with fuel?
- What's on hand: first aid kit, food/water stock, flashlights, a charged phone/radio? (Full inventory practices are in [Supplies, Inventory & Rotation](../supplies-inventory-rotation/supplies-inventory-rotation.md).)
- What information do you have: any official alert, news, weather radio, or is it word-of-mouth/guesswork? Know **before** an emergency which official channels reach you, and sign everyone up in advance (per FEMA's *Are You Ready?* guide): **Wireless Emergency Alerts (WEA)** (built into most cell phones by default — no sign-up needed, but confirm they're enabled in your phone's settings), a **NOAA Weather Radio** (battery/hand-crank, works with no cell signal), your county/city's opt-in text-or-email alert system, and the **FEMA app**. A word-of-mouth report ("someone said the creek's rising") is real information, but treat it as **unconfirmed** until an official source corroborates it.

## Step 4 — Is it getting better, worse, or unknown? (60 seconds)
This determines urgency. Look for trend, not just a snapshot:
- **Worse** (water rising, smoke thickening, wind increasing, more people reporting the same problem) → act now, favor the safer/more conservative choice, likely evacuate.
- **Better/stable** (rain easing, power just needs to be restored, isolated event already contained) → you likely have time to plan carefully; shelter-in-place is more viable.
- **Unknown** → treat as worsening until you get real information. Don't assume "it's probably fine."

## Step 5 — Decide your immediate next action (30 seconds)
By now you should be able to finish this sentence: **"The immediate threat is ___, so in the next few minutes we are going to ___."** If you can't finish that sentence, you don't have enough information yet — get more (a radio, a neighbor, an official source) before doing anything except addressing an obvious life threat.

**5-Minute Assessment Card (keep this near your go-bag or on the fridge):**
```
1. HURT/MISSING?  ......................  Y / N  -> if Y, first aid / find them NOW
2. ACTIVE DANGER HERE (fire/gas/water/structure/threat)? Y / N -> if Y, GET OUT, assess from outside
3. EVENT TYPE:  sudden-local / sudden-wide / slow-building / ongoing
4. RESOURCES:  people ___  power Y/N  water Y/N  phone Y/N  vehicle+fuel Y/N
5. TREND:  better / worse / unknown
6. NEXT ACTION (one sentence): ___________________________________
```

# PART 2 — THE RULE OF THREES AS A PLANNING TOOL

The classic survival "rule of threes" (roughly: ~3 minutes without air/in icy water, ~3 hours without shelter in harsh exposure, ~3 days without water, ~3 weeks without food) is usually taught as a physiological survival limit. Use it here as **three concentric planning horizons** so you don't spend your first hour arguing about food while someone is bleeding, and don't spend week two still living out of a go-bag when you could have restocked.

## Horizon 1 — Next 3 hours: SURVIVE
Focus only on things that kill quickly: bleeding, airway, exposure (heat or cold), immediate structural/fire/flood danger, drowning, electrocution. Nothing else matters until this horizon is under control.
- Stop the bleeding, clear the airway, get people breathing.
- Get out of extreme heat or cold (see [Shelter](../shelter/shelter.md) and [Clothing & Footwear](../clothing-footwear-protection/clothing-footwear-protection.md)).
- Get away from fire, floodwater, unstable structures, or downed lines.
- Do the 5-minute assessment (Part 1) if you haven't already.

## Horizon 2 — Next 3 days: STABILIZE
Once nobody is in mortal danger this minute, turn to what will hurt you within a few days:
- **Water** — locate and start making it safe (see [Water](../water/water.md)). This is the single most time-critical resource after the first hours.
- **Shelter from ongoing exposure** — is your location tenable for multiple nights? Heat, cold, rain?
- **Medical needs** — chronic medications, insulin, oxygen, wounds needing ongoing care.
- **Communication** — get a status message to your out-of-area contact and check for official information (see [Communication](../communication/communication.md)).
- **Make the shelter-in-place vs. evacuate decision** (Part 3) using real information gathered in this window.

## Horizon 3 — Next 3 weeks: SUSTAIN
If the event is ongoing, shift to sustainment:
- **Food** rationing and supply — see [Food Preservation & Cooking](../food-preservation-cooking/food-preservation-cooking.md) and [Survival Logistics & Resources](../survival-logistics-resources/survival-logistics-resources.md) for tracking and rationing math.
- **Sanitation and hygiene** to prevent disease from becoming the second disaster (see [Sanitation & Hygiene](../sanitation-hygiene/sanitation-hygiene.md)).
- **Security** of your home/supplies if normal services are down for an extended period (see [Security & Defense](../security-defense/security-defense.md)).
- **Mental endurance** — this is where morale and routine (Part 7's daily briefing) start to matter as much as physical supplies (see [Psychological First Aid](../psychological-first-aid/psychological-first-aid.md)).
- **Recovery planning** begins even while you're still sustaining — see [Recovery & Rebuilding](../recovery-rebuilding/recovery-rebuilding.md).

> **The triage rule:** when two problems compete for your attention, the one from the *earlier* horizon always wins. A 3-week food-supply worry never outranks a 3-hour bleeding injury or a 3-day water shortage.

# PART 3 — SHELTER-IN-PLACE VS. EVACUATE: THE DECISION FRAMEWORK

This is the single highest-stakes decision in most emergencies, and the one people most often get wrong by deciding too late or by refusing to reconsider once they've decided. Decide it with a framework, not a feeling.

## The core question
**Is the greater danger inside your current location, or outside it?**
- Danger **inside** (fire, gas leak, structural failure, floodwater entering the building, a threat inside the building) → **you must evacuate**, even if it's unpleasant or scary outside.
- Danger **outside** and your building is structurally sound and not directly threatened (most tornado warnings once the storm has passed nearby, most winter storms, most short power outages, most air-quality events with a sealable room) → **shelter-in-place** is usually safer than being caught outside or on the road.

## Decision framework — walk through in order
1. **Is there an official evacuation order for your area?** If yes, evacuate — do not wait to "see how bad it gets." Officials issue mandatory orders based on information you don't have (dam status, storm track, chemical plume modeling). Voluntary/advisory orders mean start preparing to leave and re-check triggers below.
2. **Is the threat already inside your structure, or will it be within your decision window?** (Active fire, gas smell, water entering the lowest floor, visible structural failure.) If yes → evacuate now, don't wait for it to worsen.
3. **Will your location remain safe and tenable for the expected duration of the event?** Consider: structural soundness, flood elevation, distance from the hazard (wildfire, chemical release, storm surge), and whether you can shelter multiple days if needed (water, warmth/cooling, sanitation).
4. **Is movement itself more dangerous than staying?** Driving in an active tornado outbreak, whiteout ice conditions, or through active floodwater is often more dangerous than sheltering. (See [Transportation & Vehicle Emergencies](../transportation-vehicle-emergencies/transportation-vehicle-emergencies.md) for on-the-road hazard judgment.)
5. **Do you have a safe place to go and a safe way to get there?** An evacuation to nowhere, or with no fuel/route, is not a plan — see the [Evacuation & Bug-Out guide](../evacuation-bugout-vehicle/evacuation-bugout-vehicle.md) for route planning, vehicle readiness, and what to load.

## Decision table

| Situation | Shelter-in-place | Evacuate |
|---|---|---|
| Tornado warning, storm not yet arrived | ✅ (interior room, lowest floor, no windows) | Only if in a mobile home / vehicle |
| House fire or gas smell | ❌ | ✅ immediately |
| Flash flood, water rising toward the home | Only if on high ground and water is not entering | ✅ before roads flood — do not wait |
| Hurricane, official mandatory evacuation | ❌ | ✅ leave early, before roads clog |
| Extended winter power outage, home holding heat | ✅ if you can stay warm and hydrated | If home can't hold safe temperature |
| Wildfire visible/approaching or "go" order issued | ❌ | ✅ immediately, don't wait for embers |
| Civil unrest/security threat near but not at home | ✅ (secure, low profile) | If threat is directed at your location |
| Chemical/hazmat release, "shelter-in-place" advised by officials | ✅ (seal room, shut HVAC) | Only if officials say evacuate |

*(For the specific actions inside each disaster type — sealing a room, safe rooms, storm timing — see [Disaster-Specific](../disaster-specific/disaster-specific.md).)*

> **Why sheltering has a shelf life for chemical/hazmat events:** sealing a room is a *short-term* measure — officials generally don't recommend holding a sealed room for more than **a few hours**, because outside air gradually seeps back in and the seal's effectiveness drops the longer you hold it, so past that window evacuation (once it's safe to move) usually becomes the better option. Follow local officials' instructions for the specific incident. This is exactly why Part 3's re-evaluation rule below (re-check every 2–4 hours) exists: a shelter-in-place decision is a *for-now* decision, not a *for-the-duration* one.

**Multi-hazard note:** don't assume only one hazard is in play. FEMA's planning guidance specifically calls out combined events (a pandemic during a hurricane, an earthquake followed by a tsunami, a winter storm during a power outage) — if two hazards could plausibly stack, plan and decide for the worse of the two, not just the one you noticed first.

## Write your own decision triggers in advance
The whole point of a trigger is that you set the number/condition **before** you're scared, so in the moment you just check a fact, not your feelings. Fill these in for your own home:

```
IF ___________________________ (specific, measurable)
THEN we ______________________ (specific action)

Examples to adapt:
IF water reaches the ______ step on the porch/driveway   THEN we evacuate NOW, before roads flood.
IF an official mandatory evacuation is issued for our zone THEN we leave within ___ hour(s), no debate.
IF we smell gas indoors                                   THEN everyone exits immediately, no one relights anything, call from outside.
IF the power has been out more than ___ days AND indoor temp exceeds ___°F/°F below ___°F THEN we relocate to [backup location].
IF [named family member] is not at the rally point by ___ (time) THEN [designated person] goes to [their last known location / school / workplace] to check.
IF an evacuation seems likely for our area                      THEN top off the vehicle's fuel now and never let it drop below half a tank until the risk has passed.
```

**A standing rule worth adopting outright (from FEMA guidance):** keep your gas tank at least half full **at all times** — as an everyday habit, not just when trouble is brewing — and **top it off to full the moment an evacuation seems even possible**; gas stations lose power and lines get long fast once an area starts evacuating. If your household evacuates, take only **one vehicle per family** if you can; it reduces traffic for everyone and gets more people out faster. (Full vehicle-readiness and route planning is in the [Evacuation & Bug-Out guide](../evacuation-bugout-vehicle/evacuation-bugout-vehicle.md).)

Re-evaluate on a clock, not just on feeling: in a slow-building event (hurricane, winter storm, flood watch), re-run the shelter-vs-evacuate decision **every 2–4 hours** as new information (forecasts, official updates) comes in, and write down the time and decision each time. Reversing an earlier decision is normal and smart — treat "we decided to stay this morning" as a re-checkable choice, not a commitment to be defended.

# PART 4 — FAMILY ROLES

Assign roles **before** an emergency so people act instead of asking "what should I do?" in the moment. Roles should fit real capability (age, mobility, calm-under-pressure), not just birth order. Write names next to each role and post it.

| Role | Responsibility | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Incident lead** | Makes the final call on shelter vs. evacuate; runs the 5-minute assessment and daily briefing | Usually the calmest decisive adult; needs a clearly-agreed backup if unavailable |
| **Second/backup lead** | Takes over if the lead is injured, absent, or separated | Must know everything the lead knows — no single point of failure |
| **Kids & vulnerable-persons handler** | Physically responsible for young children, elderly, disabled, or pets during the event | Should not also be tasked with heavy lifting/driving |
| **Medical/first-aid person** | Carries/knows the first aid kit, handles injuries, tracks medications | See [First Aid](../first-aid/first-aid.md) |
| **Supplies/go-bag person** | Knows where every bag and key document is; grabs them fast | Coordinate with [Supplies, Inventory & Rotation](../supplies-inventory-rotation/supplies-inventory-rotation.md) |
| **Communications person** | Sends the out-of-area contact updates; monitors radio/phone for official alerts | See [Communication](../communication/communication.md) |
| **Vehicle/driver** | Keeps vehicle fueled and ready; drives during evacuation | See [Evacuation & Bug-Out](../evacuation-bugout-vehicle/evacuation-bugout-vehicle.md) |
| **Security/lock-up** | Secures the home if evacuating (utilities off, doors locked) or watches entries if sheltering | See [Security & Defense](../security-defense/security-defense.md) |

**For households of one or two people:** don't skip this — just double up roles, and make sure at least one neighbor, friend, or relative nearby knows your plan and could check on you. For children old enough to help, give them a real, age-appropriate job (holding a flashlight, checking off a list, carrying their own small bag) — a job reduces a child's fear far more than being told to "stay calm." Full child-specific drills, go-bags, and reunification procedures are in [Children & Family Operations](../children-family-operations/children-family-operations.md).

**For anyone living alone, elderly, or with a disability or mobility limitation:** the American Red Cross specifically recommends building a **support team of at least three people** (neighbors, friends, relatives) who agree to check on you after a disaster, know your needs, and have a way in if you can't get to the door yourself — exchange spare house keys with them, tell them when you'll be out of town, and teach at least one of them to operate any home medical equipment you rely on. Write an information card describing the best way to communicate with or move you if needed, and keep it with your emergency supplies.

# PART 5 — RALLY POINTS

You need **two rally points**, because different emergencies make different places unreachable. FEMA's Family Emergency Communication Plan actually breaks this into **four tiers** — worth knowing even though the first one isn't a "meeting place" you travel to:

- **In your home** — for sudden severe weather (tornado, high wind), everyone's agreed spot is a small, interior, windowless room or hallway on the lowest floor — a closet, bathroom, or a built safe room/storm shelter. This is a "rally point" in the sense that everyone should move there together without being told twice. (See [Disaster-Specific](../disaster-specific/disaster-specific.md) for how to pick and reinforce this room.)
- **Near/in-neighborhood** (below) — for a fire or sudden event at the home itself.
- **Far/out-of-neighborhood** (below) — for when the home or block is unreachable.
- **Out-of-town** (below) — for area-wide disasters affecting the whole community.

## Primary/near rally point
- Just outside your home — a mailbox, a specific tree, a neighbor's driveway.
- Used for sudden events at the home itself (fire, gas leak) where everyone needs to get clear of the structure fast and account for each other before deciding anything else.

## Secondary/far rally point
- Outside the immediate neighborhood — a relative's house, a church, a school, a well-known local landmark — reachable on foot or by a short drive.
- Used when the home/block itself is unreachable, unsafe, or the family got separated while out (e.g., different people were at work/school when a wide-area event hit).

## Choosing good rally points
- Pick places that are **specific** ("the flagpole at the front entrance of Lowry Crossing Baptist Church," not "the church") and that **everyone in the family, including kids, can find and describe.**
- Prefer places that are normally accessible (not gated, not requiring a key) and unlikely to be affected by the same hazard as your home (not also in the floodplain, not also downwind of the same chemical plant).
- Have a **third, out-of-town** option for area-wide disasters where the whole town/region is affected — a relative's home in another city functions as both a far rally point and a place to actually shelter.
- Write down **walking directions**, not just an address, in case GPS/phones are down.
- **Also record each workplace's and school/daycare's own evacuation/pick-up site.** Ask in advance: what is their emergency plan, where do they send kids/staff if the building is evacuated, who is authorized to pick a child up, and how will they notify you? (FEMA's planning guidance flags this explicitly — a disaster can strike during school or work hours, when your household isn't together to begin with.) Sign up for their alert system the same way you signed up for your local one in Part 1.

**Rally point card:**
```
IN-HOME SAFE ROOM (interior, windowless, lowest floor):  ____________
NEAR RALLY POINT (right outside the home):  ________________________
FAR RALLY POINT (in town, reachable on foot/short drive): ____________
OUT-OF-TOWN RALLY POINT (relative/friend, different city): ___________
   Address: _____________________  Phone: _____________________
WORKPLACE evacuation/meeting site (per household adult): _____________
SCHOOL/DAYCARE evacuation site & authorized pick-up person(s): _______
```

# PART 6 — EMERGENCY CONTACT LIST & OUT-OF-AREA CONTACT

## Why an out-of-area contact
During wide-area disasters, **local phone circuits and cell towers get overloaded** long before long-distance ones do. A family member two states away is often easier to reach than someone three blocks away. Designate **one person outside your region** as the single point everyone calls/texts to report status — then everyone else calls that one person instead of each other, cutting through the jammed local network. FEMA's guidance adds one refinement: keep **at least two ways to reach that person** (e.g., their cell phone *and* an email or a second phone number) in case one method is down.

## Build the list now (before you need it)
Write it on paper (phones die, get lost, or lose signal) and keep copies: one in every go-bag, one on the fridge, one in each vehicle, one in a wallet/purse.


## Rules for using it
- Everyone (kids included, at an age-appropriate level) should know the out-of-area contact's phone number **from memory**, not just written down. FEMA's plan suggests actually testing this: ask household members to recite the important numbers from memory now, as a reality check on what they'd actually remember mid-emergency.
- Texting often gets through when calling doesn't — a text uses less bandwidth. Try text first in a congested-network event.
- Agree on a short status format so messages are useful fast: **"[Name] SAFE at [location] as of [time]."**
- Update the out-of-area contact any time your status or location changes materially — they may become the hub who tells other relatives what's going on.
- **Save your chosen contact in every phone under "ICE"** ("In Case of Emergency") — first responders and bystanders are trained to look for this if they need to identify who to call for someone who can't speak for themselves. Also build a **group contact list** on each household member's phone with everyone else in the household, so a single message reaches the whole group at once.
- **Have a backup that doesn't depend on a charged mobile phone:** a landline or corded/analog phone (these often still work when mobile networks and even power are down), a pay phone if one is still accessible nearby, or a prepaid phone card kept in a go-bag for anyone without a personal mobile phone.
- **Digital/social options as a supplement, not a replacement:** apps and sites like the FEMA app, American Red Cross "Safe and Well," and social-media check-in features (e.g., Facebook's Crisis/Safety Check) can broadcast your status to many people at once when phone lines are jammed — sign up before you need them, and treat them as one more channel, not your only plan.
- For the mechanics of radios, phone alternatives, and what to do with no signal at all, see [Communication](../communication/communication.md).

# PART 7 — DOCUMENTING THE PLAN

A plan that lives only in your head fails exactly when you need it — under stress, memory is unreliable, and you may be separated from the person who "knows the plan."

## What to write down
1. The 5-Minute Assessment Card (Part 1).
2. The Rule-of-Threes horizon notes — what "3 hours / 3 days / 3 weeks" priorities look like for *your* household specifically.
3. Your filled-in decision triggers (Part 3).
4. The family roles table (Part 4), with actual names.
5. Both rally points plus the out-of-town option (Part 5).
6. The full contact list (Part 6).
7. Where the go-bags, important documents, and supplies physically are (cross-reference [Supplies, Inventory & Rotation](../supplies-inventory-rotation/supplies-inventory-rotation.md) and [Documents, Money & Admin](../documents-money-admin/documents-money-admin.md) for what belongs in that kit).

## Where to keep it
- **One paper copy posted at home** (inside a cabinet door, on the fridge) — visible enough to reference quickly, not so exposed it's a privacy risk to visitors.
- **One copy in each go-bag and vehicle.**
- **One digital copy** (photo on phone, saved to a note app, emailed to yourself) as backup — but never rely on digital-only, since power/phones may be down exactly when you need it.
- **Give a copy to your out-of-area contact** — if you can't reach each other, they may need to relay your plan to someone local who can help.
- **Review and update it twice a year** (e.g., when clocks change) — kids grow, phone numbers change, workplaces move, and a stale plan gives false confidence. (FEMA's own guidance sets the floor at "at least once a year, or whenever information changes" — twice a year is simply the safer margin used in this guide.)

## Practice it, don't just write it
A plan you've only read is not the same as a plan you've run. FEMA's own recommended practice schedule is worth copying directly:
- **Test your evacuation and shelter plan** — actually walk/drive to both rally points, and physically move to your in-home safe room, at least once.
- **Test your communication plan** — have everyone actually call or text the out-of-area contact, so you find out now (not during a real event) whose phone doesn't have the number saved, whose number is wrong, or who forgets the plan under mild pressure.
- **Run a household emergency drill** the same way you'd run a fire drill — pick a scenario, call it out, and have everyone execute their role and rally point for real.
- **Refresh supplies** at least every 6 months alongside this review (see [Supplies, Inventory & Rotation](../supplies-inventory-rotation/supplies-inventory-rotation.md)).

# PART 8 — THE DAILY SITUATION BRIEFING (FOR PROLONGED EVENTS)

When an event stretches past the first day or two (extended power outage, aftermath of a major storm, ongoing regional disruption), run a short, structured check-in **at the same time every day**. This does two things: it keeps decisions grounded in current facts instead of yesterday's fear, and it gives everyone — especially kids — a predictable routine, which is one of the most effective things you can do for morale (see [Psychological First Aid](../psychological-first-aid/psychological-first-aid.md)).

## When to hold it
- Once daily at a fixed time (morning is usually best, before the day's tasks are decided) once the event passes the "3 hours" acute phase.
- More often (every 2–4 hours) during a fast-changing acute phase (an approaching storm, an active wildfire) — see the re-evaluation guidance in Part 3.

## The briefing agenda (10–15 minutes, keep it short)
1. **Headcount** — is everyone present and accounted for? Anyone due to arrive who hasn't?
2. **Status update** — any change in the hazard itself (new forecast, new official information, new visible danger)?
3. **Resource check** — water, food, fuel, medication, battery/power status. (Detailed consumption tracking belongs in [Survival Logistics & Resources](../survival-logistics-resources/survival-logistics-resources.md) — this briefing just asks "are we still OK, or is something running low enough to act on today?")
4. **Re-check your decision triggers** — has anything crossed a trigger from Part 3 since yesterday?
5. **Today's priorities** — pick 2–3 concrete things to accomplish today, ranked by the rule-of-threes horizon they belong to.
6. **Roles for today** — confirm who's doing what; adjust if someone is sick, exhausted, or a task changed.
7. **One thing everyone should know** — a single clear takeaway so even a child or a distracted family member leaves the briefing knowing at least one useful fact ("we boil water before drinking it," "don't go past the flagpole," "call [out-of-area contact] if we get separated").

## Briefing log (write it down each day — even three lines)
```
DAY __  DATE/TIME: __________
Headcount OK?  Y/N — notes: ______________________
Hazard status: ______________________
Resources (water/food/fuel/meds/power): ______________________
Any trigger crossed?  Y/N — action taken: ______________________
Today's top 2-3 priorities: 1) _____ 2) _____ 3) _____
Roles today: ______________________
One thing everyone should know: ______________________
```
Keeping even a brief daily log matters for two practical reasons beyond memory: it lets a new arrival (a returning family member, a relief worker, first responders) get up to speed in seconds, and it gives you an honest record afterward for insurance, recovery, or simply learning what worked (see [Recovery & Rebuilding](../recovery-rebuilding/recovery-rebuilding.md)).

# North Texas / Anna, TX specifics
- **Warning lead time varies enormously by hazard here.** Tornadoes may give only minutes — your decision trigger should already be "sirens/warning for our county = shelter in the lowest interior room now," not something to debate. Ice storms and hurricanes remnants (heavy rain/flood risk moving up from the Gulf) can give days of lead time — use that time to run the shelter-vs-evacuate framework calmly and stage supplies/fuel early, before roads and stores get crowded.
- **Flash flooding is a leading local killer** in Collin County and the wider blackland prairie — low-water crossings and creek-adjacent roads (tributaries of the East Fork Trinity River) flood fast. Your evacuation trigger should include water depth at specific local crossings you actually drive, not just "if it looks bad." Never drive through flooded roads — turn around, don't drown.
- **Rural + suburban mix** means rally points and contacts may span longer distances than in a dense city — confirm your far/out-of-town rally point is genuinely reachable given typical local road conditions (some rural roads flood or wash out and become impassable exactly when you'd need them).
- **Summer heat** turns "shelter-in-place during a power outage" into a 3-hour-horizon problem, not a 3-day one, if AC is out — build a heat-specific trigger ("if indoor temp exceeds ~85°F and power isn't expected back within X hours, relocate to [cooling location/relative's home]").
- **Grassfire risk** in dry periods on the prairie can move fast with wind — treat any "go" order or visible fast-moving smoke the same as the wildfire row in the decision table: evacuate, don't wait to see how close it gets.

# ⚠️ Safety — the most important warnings
- **Never wait for a "more official" order if you can already see the danger is inside your location or getting worse** — a mandatory evacuation order is a floor, not a ceiling; you're always allowed to leave earlier if your own assessment says so.
- **Don't let planning become paralysis.** The 5-minute assessment exists so you spend minutes, not hours, before acting on an active life threat.
- **Re-check your decision, don't defend it.** The most dangerous moment in many disasters is when new information arrives (water rising faster, a new warning) and people stick with an earlier "we're fine" decision out of stubbornness or denial.
- **Never drive through flooded roads or active wildfire/embers** to "stick to the evacuation route" — if the planned route is now the hazard, the plan is to find another way, not to force the original one.
- **A plan that lives only in one person's head is not a plan.** If the incident lead is the one who's hurt, absent, or panicked, everyone else needs to already know the roles, rally points, and triggers.
- **Kids and vulnerable family members need a job, not just an instruction to "stay calm."** Fear drops sharply when a person — including a child — has something concrete to do.
- **Do not skip the out-of-area contact.** Relying only on local calls during a wide-area event is one of the most common and avoidable communication failures.

# Troubleshooting
| Problem | Fix |
|---|---|
| Can't decide shelter vs. evacuate in the moment | Fall back to your pre-written decision triggers (Part 3); if none exist for this situation, default to the safer/more conservative option and re-check every 2–4 hours. |
| Family disagrees on the decision | The pre-assigned incident lead makes the final call — settle who that is *before* an emergency, not during one. |
| Family members are separated | Go to the near rally point first, then the far rally point; if neither works, everyone contacts the out-of-area contact, who relays positions. |
| No cell signal | Try text before voice call (uses less bandwidth); try again after a delay (networks often clear in bursts); see [Communication](../communication/communication.md) for radio alternatives. |
| Plan exists but nobody remembers it under stress | Keep a physical copy visible (fridge, go-bag) — you're not expected to recall it from memory alone; that's the point of writing it down. |
| Event drags on and things start feeling chaotic | Start (or recommit to) the daily briefing routine (Part 8) — a fixed, predictable check-in restores structure fast. |
| A decision trigger turns out to be wrong for the actual situation | Update it in the moment if lives are at risk, then correct the written plan afterward — the framework serves your safety, not the reverse. |

# Quick-reference checklist
- [ ] Ran the 5-minute situation assessment (life threats, event type, resources, trend, next action).
- [ ] Reviewed priorities against the 3-hour / 3-day / 3-week rule-of-threes horizons.
- [ ] Made the shelter-vs-evacuate decision using the framework and decision table, not a gut feeling alone.
- [ ] Wrote specific "if X then Y" decision triggers for the hazards most likely in your area.
- [ ] Assigned family roles (incident lead, backup, kids/vulnerable handler, medical, supplies, comms, driver, security) with real names.
- [ ] Chose a near rally point, a far rally point, and an out-of-town rally point, with specific descriptions/directions.
- [ ] Built a full emergency contact list including one out-of-area contact everyone knows by memory (with 2 ways to reach them) and "ICE" saved in every phone.
- [ ] Signed up for official alerts in advance (confirmed Wireless Emergency Alerts are enabled, have a NOAA Weather Radio or local alert sign-up, and the FEMA app).
- [ ] Asked each child's school/daycare, and each adult's workplace, about their emergency plan and pick-up/evacuation procedure — and who is authorized to pick up each child.
- [ ] Documented the whole plan on paper in multiple locations (home, go-bags, vehicles) plus a digital backup.
- [ ] Reviewed/updated the written plan in the last 6 months, and actually practiced it (drill + test communication plan), not just re-read it.
- [ ] Set up (or ready to start) a daily situation-briefing routine for any event lasting more than a day or two.
